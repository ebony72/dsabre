\section{\rev{The \dSABRE{} Routing Loop}}
\label{app:pseudocode}

\rev{Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsabre} is the pseudocode of the main routing
loop summarised in Section~\ref{sec:workflow} of the main paper and
drawn as a flowchart in its Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}.  Phase labels \textup{(P1)}--%
\textup{(P4)} match those used there: drain the front layer, classify
the remainder into intra- and inter-core gates, apply one SWAP or one
teleport, and checkpoint on forward progress.  Equation numbers refer
to the main paper.}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\dSABRE{} \texttt{route}$(C, \ell_0)$}
\label{alg:dsabre}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $W \leftarrow$ working copy of circuit DAG $C$;\ \ $\ell \leftarrow \ell_0$
\STATE $C_\mathrm{out} \leftarrow$ empty routed circuit on the physical qubits
\STATE save checkpoint $(\ell, W)$
\WHILE{$W$ has unexecuted gates}
  \STATE drain $F$ (execute 1Q gates and adjacent intra-core 2Q gates; append them to $C_\mathrm{out}$) \hfill\textit{(P1)}
  \IF{$W$ empty} \STATE \textbf{break} \ENDIF
  \STATE compute $F$, partition into $F_\mathrm{intra}, F_\mathrm{inter}$ \hfill\textit{(P2)}
  \IF{$F_\mathrm{intra}\!\neq\!\emptyset$}
    \STATE compute per-core intra-only extended set $E_c$ for each active core $c$ \hfill\textit{(P3a)}
    \STATE pick best SWAP $(u,v)$ within a core (Eq.~\ref{eq:intra})
    \STATE apply SWAP$(u,v)$ to $\ell$;\ \ append SWAP$(u,v)$ to $C_\mathrm{out}$
  \ELSIF{$F_\mathrm{inter}\!\neq\!\emptyset$}
    \STATE compute global $E$ if not cached \hfill\textit{(P3b)}
    \STATE generate candidates over $F_\mathrm{inter}$
    \STATE pick lowest-score candidate (Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre})
    \STATE apply teleport to $\ell$;\ \ append teleport (staging SWAPs $+$ teledata) to $C_\mathrm{out}$
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$|W|$ decreased}
    \STATE save checkpoint \hfill\textit{(P4)}
  \ELSIF{$L_\mathrm{deadlock}{=}50$ stalled iterations}
    \STATE restore checkpoint;\ \texttt{backup\_plan}$(W,\ell,C_\mathrm{out})$ \hfill\textit{// abort after $N_\mathrm{backup}^\mathrm{max}{=}50$}
  \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $(\ell, C_\mathrm{out}, \mathrm{metrics})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\rev{\textbf{Operational definition of a teleport (line 61).}  Executing
a candidate is deterministic given the state at the moment it is
scored.  The destination port $\pi_d$ need not be empty: if occupied,
its occupant is evicted---walked by intra-core SWAPs to the nearest
free slot in $c_\mathrm{next}$, exactly the rule also applied to the
source port $\pi_s$ if it is occupied.  That requires $c_\mathrm{next}$
to have a free slot to evict into, so the router only ever proposes
landing cores with at least one; a core with none is never scored as
$c_\mathrm{next}$ to begin with, rather than being scored and losing on
the capacity penalty (the underlying eviction cost is a large but
finite placeholder in that case, never reached by a scored candidate).
The source core is not screened this way, since departing it can only
free a slot---but if $c_\mathrm{src}$ happens to be completely full
when the move is applied, the port-$\pi_s$ eviction above has nowhere
to go, so the router first teleports one further qubit out of
$c_\mathrm{src}$ to open a slot (leaving $q_1$ itself untouched), and,
because that correction can itself land in and fill $c_\mathrm{next}$,
re-applies the same correction there if needed before evicting
$\pi_d$.  Once the move completes, $\ell$ and $\ell^{-1}$ are updated
in place: $q_1$'s image becomes $\pi_d$, the staging slot $n_s$ it
vacated becomes free, and $\pi_d$'s prior occupant, if any, takes the
image of wherever it was evicted to.}

\section{\rev{Computational Complexity}}
\label{app:complexity}

\rev{\noindent This appendix derives Eq.~\ref{eq:complexity} of
Section~\ref{sec:complexity}: the structural facts it rests on, why costs
are charged per applied move rather than per iteration, which terms are
required by the algorithm and which are artefacts an implementation may
remove, and a measured validation of the result.}

\subsection{\rev{Model and notation}}

\rev{A circuit is a DAG $C$ on $n$ logical qubits with $N{=}|C|$
operations, each on at most two qubits; $N_r$ denotes the operations not
yet routed at a given moment.  The architecture has $K$ cores of $M$
qubits, $P{=}KM$ in total, intra-core degree at most $\delta$ (four on a
grid, three on a heavy-hex tile), $|\Pi|$ communication ports per core,
and diameters $D_M$ within a core and $D_K$ over the core graph.  $L$ is
the extended-set capacity.  Over one call to \textsc{route}, write $A$ for
the intra-core SWAPs applied, $B$ for the teleports, and $T$ for the main
loop iterations.  All are counted as executed, not as bounded by
hyperparameters.}

\subsection{\rev{Four structural facts}}

\rev{\textbf{(F1) The DAG is sparse.}  A gate acts on at most two wires and
has at most one immediate predecessor and one immediate successor on each,
so in- and out-degree are at most two and $|E_C|\le 2N$.  Computing every
node's in-degree from scratch is therefore $\Theta(N)$, while updating them
on a retirement is $O(1)$.}

\rev{\textbf{(F2) Front-layer gates are qubit-disjoint.}  The front layer
holds, per wire, that wire's earliest unexecuted gate, so it is a matching
on qubits and $|F|\le n/2$.  An intra-core SWAP exchanges the contents of
two slots; each slot holds at most one logical qubit and each qubit lies in
at most one front gate, so a candidate SWAP changes the distance term of at
most \emph{two} front gates and every other term of $\Delta_F$ is
identically zero.  This is what removes the $|F_\mathrm{c}|$ factor---and
with it a factor $M$ from the chip-wide bound---from the per-candidate
cost of Eq.~\ref{eq:intra}.}

\rev{\textbf{(F3) BFS layers are matchings.}  In the inter-core extended
set of Section~\ref{sec:bfsext} a layer is the set of gates whose remaining
in-degree has just reached zero.  Two gates sharing a qubit are ordered
along that wire, so they can never become ready together: layer width is at
most $n/2$.  Single-qubit gates are drained before scoring, so every layer
contributes at least one gate to $E$, at most $L$ layers are peeled, and
every layer but the last is emitted in full---hence
$\sum_{k<\kappa}w_k\le L$ and only the truncated final layer can be wide.
One construction therefore costs $O(|F|+L)=O(n+L)$.}

\rev{\textbf{(F4) Retirement is downward closed.}  Gates leave the working
DAG only from the front layer, whether drained, executed after a SWAP, or
retired by deadlock recovery.  The retired set is thus a prefix: a
surviving node has no retired successors, and splicing a retired node out
never creates an edge between two survivors.  Two consequences follow.
In-degrees can be maintained by decrementing a retiring node's successors,
which by (F1) is $O(1)$.  And a checkpoint need not copy the DAG: it is a
mark in the retirement log, and a rollback replays the log onto a fresh
copy of the input.}

\subsection{\rev{Precomputation}}

\rev{The three tables of Section~\ref{sec:arch} cost $O(KM^2)$ for
$\dintra$ and $O(K^2)$ for $\dcore$, both unweighted breadth-first
searches, and $O(K^2\log K)$ for the port table $\dport$, a Dijkstra over
the $O(K)$ ports alone.  Equation~\ref{eq:dphys} is exact because any
$p\to q$ walk decomposes as an intra-core segment to the first port it
leaves through, a port-to-port walk, and an intra-core segment from the
last port it enters through; the port graph carries precisely those two
edge kinds.  Resident space is $O(KM^2+K^2)$ against $\Theta(P^2)$ for a
dense table---a factor of roughly $K$, since $P^2=K^2M^2$---and a query
costs $O(|\Pi|^2)$ before memoisation.  This is architecture setup, paid
once and not per circuit.}

\subsection{\rev{Charging per move, not per iteration}}

\rev{An iteration applies one SWAP in each core that holds an unexecuted
local gate, and the per-core costs sum to $O(Mn)$ over the chip because
$|F_\mathrm{c}|\le M/2$ and $\sum_c|F_\mathrm{c}|\le n/2$.  A separate
argument bounds the iteration count by $O(N(D_M{+}D_K))$, one move per
iteration.  Multiplying the two overstates the total: the first is tight
only when all $K$ cores are active, and an iteration in which all $K$ cores
are active retires $K$ SWAPs, which makes the second loose by the same
factor.  Splitting the sum by what each term is charged against removes the
double count:}
\begin{equation}
T_\mathrm{total}=\underbrace{\sum_{t\le T}\!E(t)}_{\text{once per iteration}}
 +\underbrace{\sum_{(t,c)}\!S_c(t)}_{\text{once per applied SWAP}}
 +\underbrace{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}}\!W(t)}_{\text{once per teleport}} ,
\label{eq:charge}
\end{equation}
\rev{where $E$ is lookahead reconstruction, $S_c$ intra-core SWAP scoring
in core $c$, and $W$ teleport scoring.  The number of (iteration, active
core) pairs is exactly $A$; every iteration applies at least one move, so
$T=O(A{+}B)$; and a gate needs $O(D_M)$ SWAPs and $O(D_K)$ teleports---one
teleport crosses a whole core, which is also why the relevant scale is
$D_M{+}D_K$ and not the chip diameter $O(D_MD_K)$.  Hence $A=O(ND_M)$ and
$B=O(ND_K)$.  Substituting $E=O(N_r)$, $S_c=O(|F_\mathrm{c}|{+}L)$ by (F2),
and $W=O(Mn{+}P)$ into Eq.~\ref{eq:charge} gives
Eq.~\ref{eq:complexity}.  Empirically the parallel branch is almost never
taken---$1.00$ to $1.01$ active cores per intra-core iteration across the
64-qubit suite---so the per-move charge is the tight one and the
per-iteration product was loose by close to a factor $M$.}

\subsection{\rev{The two lookahead sets}}

\rev{The two sets have genuinely different complexities, and the
distinction is what makes Eq.~\ref{eq:complexity} quadratic rather than
linear in $N$.}

\rev{The inter-core set $E$ costs $O(n{+}L)$ per call by (F1) and (F3),
provided remaining in-degrees are carried between calls rather than
rebuilt; rebuilding makes it $\Theta(N_r)$.  It is not $O(L)$: seeding
walks the front layer, and the burst-priority rule of
Section~\ref{sec:bfsext} must classify the truncated final layer, which
(F3) bounds only by $n/2$.  $E$ also depends on the DAG alone and not on
the layout, so it needs recomputation only when a gate retires---never
after a teleport, which removes no gate.}

\rev{The intra-core set $E_\mathrm{c}$ admits no bound better than
$O(N_r)$, and this is a property of the construction rather than of any
implementation.  A gate enters $E_\mathrm{c}$ only if both operands are
untainted and in the core, and taint, once acquired, propagates along the
wire forever; a single cross-core gate therefore removes both its qubits
permanently.  A core's size-$L$ quota is consequently often unreachable,
and the scan then runs to the end of the topological order.  One exact
early exit is available---once every still-requested core holds fewer than
two untainted qubits, no later node can be admitted, since $\ell$ is fixed
for the duration of the scan and taint only grows---but it shortens the
scan without removing the term.  Bounding $E_\mathrm{c}$ explicitly would
leave $O(Mn{+}P)$ per iteration and a total linear in $N$, at the cost of
changing the heuristic's output.}

\subsection{\rev{What is inherent}}

\rev{Four lower bounds hold for the algorithm as specified, independent of
implementation.  Every gate must be examined once, giving $\Omega(N)$.
Teleport selection is an $\arg\min$ over the candidates of \emph{all}
inter-core front gates, giving $\Omega(n)$ per teleport iteration unless
scores are maintained incrementally---and a gate retirement changes $E$ and
so dirties every candidate, which limits what incremental maintenance can
recover.  The staging SWAPs inside a teleport are output rather than
overhead, giving $\Omega(D_M)$ per teleport.  And $O(1)$ intra-core
distance queries require $\Omega(KM^2)$ space.}

\subsection{\rev{Measured validation}}

\rev{Table~\ref{tab:complexity_measured} collects the measurements behind
the claims above, taken on the 64-qubit suite unless noted.  The profile
attribution is the clearest: the $O(Mn{+}P)$ scoring that

runtime, while the $\Theta(N_r)$ lookahead and DAG bookkeeping account for
$85$--$90\%$, which is why compile time scales as $N^{2.1}$ and not

\section{\rev{A Capacity-Safe Mode That Cannot Fail}}
\label{app:safemode}

\rev{\noindent Appendix~\ref{app:complexity} shows that \dSABRE{}
terminates, but only by budget exhaustion: bounded counters stop the loop,
and nothing argues that it stops with every gate routed.  This appendix
closes that gap with an optional mode, states the conditions under which its
guarantee is a theorem, and measures what it costs.  It is off by default,
so every number elsewhere in this paper is unaffected.}

\subsection{\rev{How the default router can fail}}
\label{app:safemode_failure}

\rev{Capacity enters the scoring function as a soft penalty, while a
teleport is \emph{legal} whenever its destination core holds one free slot.
A legal move may therefore take a core to zero occupancy headroom, and
nothing pushes back.  Total free capacity $F{=}P{-}n$ is exactly conserved
by both operations---a SWAP moves no qubit between cores and a teleport
moves one---so routing cannot consume headroom, but it can \emph{concentrate}
it.}

\rev{On the 360-qubit QFT the concentration is severe.  The initial layout
is even, with $[24,16,19,25,15,27]$ free slots across the six cores; the
forward pass succeeds and still leaves $[68,2,2,22,20,12]$, one core holding
$54\%$ of all headroom.  The backward pass then reaches $[72,0,0,51,0,3]$
and abandons routing with $4535$ operations outstanding---not for want of
budget, which was more than twice what it used.  Every one of that pass's
$62$ deadlock recoveries, and all $83$ across the three initial layouts,
ran with at least one core at zero.  A qubit in such a core generally cannot
be teleported out, because clearing the outgoing communication port requires
a free slot to displace its occupant into; when both operands of a gate sit
in full cores, no move exists.  The failure is invisible in
Table~\ref{tab:scalability} because the best-of-three protocol reports a
surviving pass.}

\subsection{\rev{The invariant}}
\label{app:safemode_invariant}

\rev{Safe mode replaces the soft penalty with a legality condition, so that
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{free}(c)\;\ge\;r \quad\text{for every core } c,
\label{eq:safe_invariant}
\end{equation}
with reserve $r{=}2$, holds at every boundary between actions.  It is
feasible exactly when $P \ge n + rK + 1$; since $F$ is conserved this is a
property of the architecture and circuit, checked once.  The $+1$ is what
guarantees a \emph{donor}: if every core held at most $r$ free slots the
total would be at most $rK$, so some core always holds $r{+}1$ and can give
one away without breaching Eq.~\ref{eq:safe_invariant} itself.}

\rev{Two rather than one is the smallest reserve that makes the
\emph{optimal} relocation plan legal.  Walking a qubit along a shortest core
path touches each intermediate core twice: on arrival its headroom falls to
$r{-}1\ge 1$, still enough to clear the destination port, and its departure
restores it.  Only the endpoint keeps the deficit.  With a reserve of one,
an arrival can empty an intermediate core and strand the qubit there, and
the relocation must instead relay headroom \emph{ahead of} the qubit before
every hop---because the slot a hop vacates always reappears one core behind
it, never in front.  That costs approximately $3d$ teleports for a gate at
core distance $d$ instead of $d$.}

\subsection{\rev{Guaranteed gate execution}}
\label{app:safemode_transaction}

\rev{Under Eq.~\ref{eq:safe_invariant} any remote gate can be executed by a
transaction that cannot fail.  Choose a meeting core $m$ minimising
$\dcore(a,m)+\dcore(b,m)+\rho(m)$ over the cores holding the two operands and any
core with headroom to spare, where $\rho(m)$ is the cost of relaying enough
headroom to $m$; ensure $m$ can absorb the arrivals; then teleport the
operand (or operands) along a shortest core path and route the pair together
inside $m$.  Every hop is legal by the argument above, every intermediate
core is restored on departure, and the endpoint is covered by the relay, so
the state again satisfies Eq.~\ref{eq:safe_invariant} on exit.  The whole
thing is wrapped in one rollback, which no measured run has ever taken.}

\rev{When the meeting core already has headroom the cost is exactly $d$
teleports---the information-theoretic lower bound, since each teleport
advances one qubit by one core.  When it does not, the relay adds at most
$d$ more, and $2d$ is optimal among plans that leave the occupancy vector
unchanged: relocating a qubit from $a$ to $b$ shifts occupancy by
$({-}1,{+}1)$, so cancelling it needs one unit of counter-flow, itself
costing $\dcore(a,b)$.}

\subsection{\rev{Termination with success}}
\label{app:safemode_theorem}

\rev{\textbf{Theorem.}  Let the core graph be connected, every core's
coupling graph be connected with $M \ge r{+}3$ qubits, $P \ge n{+}rK{+}1$,
and the initial layout satisfy Eq.~\ref{eq:safe_invariant}.  Then safe mode
routes every gate of the input circuit; it does not abort.}

\rev{Eq.~\ref{eq:safe_invariant} is preserved by every ordinary action and
restored by every transaction, hence holds at every boundary; each
transaction therefore meets its precondition, succeeds, and retires a gate,
so the unrouted count strictly decreases and at most $|C|$ transactions
occur.  Between them the loop runs at most $L_\mathrm{d}$ non-progressing
iterations, giving $|C|(L_\mathrm{d}{+}2)$ overall---and, at any point,
$N_r(L_\mathrm{d}{+}1)$ iterations still required, a figure the router can
report.  Every former abort path instead drains the remaining gates through
the transaction, which bounds the worst case at $2D_K$ teleports per remote
gate.  The condition $M\ge r{+}3$ is what guarantees a relay can always find
a filler qubit that is not one of the gate's own operands; all five
hypotheses are checked before routing starts, so a violation is reported
rather than discovered inside a transaction.}

\subsection{\rev{What it costs}}
\label{app:safemode_cost}

\rev{Table~\ref{tab:safemode} gives the 64-qubit suite under the same
protocol as the main text.  The guarantee costs $4.2\%$ EPR pairs in
geometric mean and $0.1\%$ in total, and needs \emph{fewer} routing
iterations than the default---because the mechanism it replaces,
a reactive room-making step invoked when a teleport finds a full core, stops
being reachable at all.  Per-circuit variance is larger than the mean in
both directions, which is characteristic of the three-pass protocol: one
different choice in the forward pass propagates through the layout every
later pass inherits.}

\rev{The reserve is what does the work, not the strictness of the legality
rule.  Requiring ordinary teleports to preserve the full reserve
unaided---rather than letting the transaction re-establish it when it
runs---rejects $1.9$--$25\%$ of the moves the heuristic would otherwise
choose and costs $20.8\%$ EPR pairs, five times as much, for no additional
guarantee.  The setting reported here is the cheaper one.}

\rev{On the 360-qubit QFT, safe mode routes the pass the default router
abandons, from bit-identically the same starting layout.  The transaction
was invoked $126$ times, took no rollback, and left the chip at
$[23,3,2,81,14,3]$---headroom still concentrated, but with no core empty.}

\subsection{\rev{When it is worth enabling}}
\label{app:safemode_when}

\rev{The default router is not observed to fail on any suite in this paper
once the best-of-three protocol is applied, so the guarantee buys robustness
rather than better solutions.  It is worth enabling where a single routing
attempt must succeed---an automated pipeline with no seed to fall back on,
or a fill factor high enough that headroom concentration is likely.  It also
removes a dependence worth naming: without it, the 360-qubit result relies
on the protocol masking a failed seed.}

\section{Component Ablations}
\label{app:ablation}

This appendix supplements the mechanism-ablation table in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} of the main paper with per-circuit results
for the node-decay anti-oscillation guard inherited from \SABRE{}.

\subsection{Node-Decay}

\textbf{EPR results.}
Node decay has a circuit-dependent and scale-dependent effect on EPR count.
At 25q the impact is negligible: five of six circuits tie exactly and the
geometric-mean advantage of decay~ON is only $+0.9\%$.  At 36q the picture
favours decay~ON more strongly ($+8.3\%$ gmean), driven primarily by
\texttt{vqe\_su2}, where decay prevents oscillation on the two high-traffic
qubit slots that carry almost all inter-core traffic; without the penalty the
router repeatedly re-selects the same two SWAPs and accumulates six extra
EPR pairs (9$\,\to\,$15).  At 64q the balance reverses: decay~ON
\emph{increases} EPR by $-2.7\%$ gmean, with \texttt{graphstate} as the
largest outlier ($-21.1\%$).  On the larger H-grid topology, anti-oscillation
pressure forces the router onto longer SWAP detours that push qubits into
congested core boundaries and trigger additional teleportations.

\textbf{Local-SWAP results.}
Across all three suites and all circuits, decay~OFF uses markedly fewer local
SWAPs---reductions range from $\approx$$15\%$ to $\approx$$40\%$.  When the
router is free to reuse the same efficient SWAP path, it reaches executable
adjacency with fewer moves.  Decay~ON deflects the router onto alternative,
often longer, paths that happen to avoid recently penalised qubits.

\textbf{Takeaway.}
Node decay is a useful anti-oscillation guard on small, structured circuits
where a handful of qubits dominate inter-core traffic (36q, $+8.3\%$ EPR).
At larger scale (64q), the forced diversification of SWAP paths increases
congestion and net EPR cost ($-2.7\%$).  The mechanism is retained in the
default configuration because the 25q/36q suites represent the target regime
of current multi-core hardware, and the 64q loss is modest.  Future work
could make the decay step-size a tunable hyperparameter or apply it only when
the front layer contains a persistent inter-core bottleneck qubit.

\subsection{\rev{Parameter Sensitivity}}
\label{app:sensitivity}

\rev{We sweep each continuous hyperparameter one-at-a-time on the 25q
and 64q suites.  All defaults---$w_e{=}0.25$, $c_{\mathrm{pen}}{=}15$,
$|E|{=}20$, $\gamma{=}0.9$, and $w_{\mathrm{link}}{=}10$---sit on a
flat region of their respective curves (within ${\sim}2\%$ of the
local minimum), so performance is robust to small perturbations.  The
only sharp regressions occur when a control is pushed far from its
default: e.g.\ $w_e{\ge}1.0$ inflates 25q gmEPR by $3$--$5\times$ as
the lookahead dominates immediate preparation costs,
$c_{\mathrm{pen}}{=}0$ doubles gmEPR by inviting deadlocks in
saturated cores, and $\gamma{=}1.0$ (flat weighting) costs $7\%$ on
64q.  These observations confirm that the defaults are well-calibrated
and that no single hyperparameter is a hidden lever; tuning beyond the
defaults yields diminishing returns.}

\section{\rev{A Degree-Swept Synthetic Suite}}
\label{app:regcz}

\rev{Section~\ref{sec:ablation} of the main paper argues that how much a
mechanism can contribute depends on how much inter-core traffic the circuit
forces, and that a suite of application circuits samples that quantity rather
than controlling it.  This appendix defines a fourth, synthetic suite that
controls it, and reports the mechanism ablation across it.}

\subsection{\rev{Suite definition}}
\label{app:regcz_suite}

\rev{Each circuit fixes a random $d$-regular \emph{interaction graph} on 64
logical qubits and applies $L$ layers of single-qubit rotations followed by a
CZ across every edge of it, so every qubit has exactly $d$ partners and $d$
alone sets how much traffic must cross a core boundary under any layout.  $L$
is chosen per degree to hold the CX count near $2000$---within $4\%$ of the
64-qubit AE and QFT circuits---so that $d$ varies interaction locality at
constant gate volume rather than circuit size.  We use $d\in\{2,3,4,6,8\}$
with three interaction graphs each, 15 circuits in all.  CZs over a random
regular graph are the synthetic construction \texttt{pytket-dqc} ships for its
own benchmarks~\cite{pytketdqc2024}; we prefer it to uniformly random
circuits, which vary interaction locality only incidentally and are reported
to distort distributed-compilation benchmarks badly enough to reorder the
ranking of strategies~\cite{gragera2026distortion}.}

\rev{A circulant variant of the same construction---qubit $i$ joined to
$i{\pm}1$ and $i{\pm}2$ around a ring, degree 4, 17 layers---supplies the
second scalability family of Section~\ref{sec:scalability}, at $3400$, $6800$
and $12\,240$ CX for 100, 200 and 360 qubits.  The bounded range is what
matters here: a \emph{random} regular graph of the same degree puts a partner
in the same core with probability $(q_{\mathrm{core}}-1)/(n-1)$, which decays with
$n$, so its inter-core fraction would climb from $84\%$ to $95\%$ along the
series and the series would measure the graph rather than the machine.  On the
circulant family the margin over \TeleSABRE{} is stable: $-39.2\%$ at 100q
($692$ against $421$ EPR) and $-45.7\%$ at 200q ($1551$ against $842$).}

\subsection{\rev{Mechanism contribution against interaction-graph degree}}
\label{app:regcz_sweep}
\rev{Table~\ref{tab:mech} of the main paper gives one number per scoring
term, which invites the reading that the ranking it shows is a property of the
mechanisms.  It is not, or not only: how much a mechanism can contribute
depends on how much inter-core traffic the circuit forces, and a suite of
application circuits samples that quantity rather than controlling it.  The synthetic suite defined above controls it: gate
volume is held near $2000$ CX and only the interaction-graph degree $d$ varies,
so a mechanism's $\Delta$ read across a row of Table~\ref{tab:regcz} is its
contribution as a function of the one circuit property it is designed to
respond to.}

\rev{The two load-bearing mechanisms turn out to trade places, and not
symmetrically.  The capacity penalty is worth $+102.0\%$ at $d{=}2$---already
substantial---and past it stops being a matter of degree: with
$c_{\mathrm{pen}}{=}0$ every seed at $d{\ge}3$ deadlocks outright, so beyond
the sparsest point the mechanism is not making routing cheaper, it is what
makes routing possible at all.  The extended-set lookahead is worth
\emph{nothing} at $d{=}2$ ($-4.9\%$---marginally better without it) and
$+51.0\%$ at $d{=}3$, settling in the high twenties to mid thirties thereafter
($+28.3$--$36.3\%$).  The crossover sits between $d{=}2$ and $d{=}3$, and
it is what the two terms are for.  The capacity penalty prices
\emph{occupancy}, which is a live constraint only while a landing core can
plausibly be either full or empty---the sparse regime, in which a good layout
still leaves headroom worth protecting; past it, occupancy stops being
gradable at all and the router cannot proceed without pricing it.  The
lookahead prices \emph{future
demand}, and a qubit has competing future demands only once it has several
partners; at $d{=}2$ it has two, the front layer already names the right move,
and the lookahead adds noise.  The BFS extended set follows its own lookahead:
negative at $d{=}2$, then $+6.8$--$18.0\%$.  Table~\ref{tab:mech} ranks the
capacity penalty first because the application suites sit at the sparse end of
this axis---a property of the benchmark, not an ordering of the mechanisms.}

\rev{The baseline
itself saturates: gmEPR plateaus near
$1900$ from $d{=}4$ onward, roughly one EPR pair per CX, because past that
degree no layout can keep a qubit's partners co-located and the count is set by
the circuit rather than by the router.  Every mechanism's margin flattens with
it, which is the honest ceiling of this experiment.}

\section{Effect of Inter-Core Link Density}

We investigate whether increasing the number of quantum links in the B-grid
benefits routing.  In the standard $2{\times}2$ B-grid each adjacent core pair
shares a single physical link (4 links total).  We construct an \emph{enhanced}
variant that adds a second parallel link between every adjacent pair, yielding
8 inter-core links.  The new endpoints are placed at the interior boundary
positions symmetric to the originals: on a $4{\times}4$ core, where the original
C\textsubscript{0}--C\textsubscript{1} link uses row~1 of the boundary, the new
link uses row~2 (and vice versa for C\textsubscript{2}--C\textsubscript{3}), and
symmetrically for the vertical pairs.  No corner nodes are involved in any link.
Both compilers (TeleSABRE and \dSABRE{}) and \texttt{pytket-dqc} are
run on the 25q and 36q suites under the same protocol as the main
evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\textbf{pytket-dqc topology model.}

a simple adjacency list of core pairs, without modelling the number of physical
links per pair.  Its e-bit cost therefore depends only on \emph{which} core pairs
are connected, not on \emph{how many} physical links connect them.  Running
\texttt{pytket-dqc} on the enhanced architecture produces identical e-bit counts
(geometric means 48.1 and 16.0 for 25q and 36q respectively), confirming this
invariance.

\textbf{Key finding.}
Doubling the inter-core links does not buy a reduction in EPR consumption, and
the direction of the effect is not even consistent.  On the 25-qubit suite both
routers get \emph{worse}---TeleSABRE by $15.1\%$ and \dSABRE{} by $11.1\%$ in
geometric mean---while on the 36-qubit suite TeleSABRE improves by $7.8\%$ and
\dSABRE{} is essentially unchanged ($-0.1\%$).  The practical reading is that
link density is not a lever for
reducing communication cost: the gains it might buy are offset by what the
extra communication ports take away, and which effect dominates depends on the
workload.

\textbf{Discussion.}
Two mechanisms plausibly account for the 25-qubit regression.

\emph{(i) Communication-qubit proliferation.}
Each physical link requires two dedicated communication ports, one in each core.
Doubling the links doubles the number of comm qubits from 8 to 16 out of 64
physical qubits.  Comm qubits are attractive relay points in heuristic routing:
whenever a logical qubit sits on a comm port, the router perceives an
inexpensive path to the adjacent core and may initiate an otherwise unnecessary
teleportation.  This ``teleportation temptation'' grows with comm-qubit density,
increasing wasteful inter-core traffic.

\emph{(ii) Layout disruption from boundary saturation.}
The SABRE-style layout pass places logical qubits to minimise expected routing
cost.  With more boundary positions occupied by comm qubits, effective placement
freedom near core boundaries shrinks.  Circuits whose critical qubits should
remain inside a core may find their preferred positions displaced, forcing
additional intra-core SWAPs or cross-core handoffs that inflate EPR counts.

\emph{A mechanism we withdraw.}  An earlier version of this appendix offered a
third explanation---miscalibration of TeleSABRE's Hungarian layout weight---
resting on \texttt{qpeexact} at 36 qubits, which appeared to more than double
(100$\,\to\,$207 EPRs) when links were added.  That outlier was an artefact of a
misconfigured baseline: the driver misspelled the Hungarian allocator's
\texttt{init\_layout\_hun\_min\_free\_qubit} parameter, which TeleSABRE
silently ignores, so the allocator ran at its own default.  Measured correctly,
\texttt{qpeexact} \emph{improves} with more links (190$\,\to\,$164), the 36-qubit
suite improves overall, and the mechanism has nothing left to explain.  We
record the retraction rather than quietly drop it, since the same
configuration produced every TeleSABRE number in the submitted version.
Retuning the inter-core penalty for the denser topology would likely recover---or
surpass---the 4-link baseline.

\textbf{Takeaway.}
The standard 4-link B-grid is already well-matched to 25--36 qubit workloads on
a 64-qubit device: the bottleneck is layout quality and intra-core SWAP overhead,
not inter-core link bandwidth.  Increasing link count without co-optimising the
routing energy function and layout heuristic can substantially worsen EPR
efficiency.  These results suggest that future hardware design should pair
link-density increases with corresponding tuning of routing parameters, rather
than assuming that architectural headroom automatically translates into better
compiled outcomes.

\section{\rev{Comparison with \TeleSABRE{}: Action Sets and Heuristics}}
\label{app:telesabre}

\rev{Section~\ref{sec:related} states the three differences that drive
the measured gap.  This appendix gives the underlying action sets and
scoring rules precisely, and reports two measurements taken from
\TeleSABRE{}'s own decision stream (\texttt{code/probe\_telesabre\_actions.py}; raw counts under
\texttt{code/results/}).
Line references are to \texttt{src/telesabre.c} of the reference C
implementation.}

\subsection{\rev{When a teleport candidate exists}}
\label{app:ts_availability}

\rev{This is the structural difference between the two routers, and it
is one of availability rather than of scoring.  For a front gate
$g{=}(q_1,q_2)$ \TeleSABRE{} first computes a single \emph{attraction
path}: a Dijkstra shortest path on a contracted graph whose nodes are
the communication qubits plus the two operands.  It then proposes a
teleport moving $q_1$ out of its core only if \emph{all} of the
following hold:}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \rev{the first edge of that path leaves $\ell(q_1)$ for a
    communication qubit $\pi_s$ of the same core, and
    $(\ell(q_1),\pi_s)$ is a \emph{physical edge}---$q_1$ already sits
    adjacent to the port;}
  \item \rev{$\pi_s$ is unoccupied;}
  \item \rev{the next path node $\pi_d$ is an unoccupied communication
    qubit in a different core, which fixes the destination
    $c_{\mathrm{next}}$;}
  \item \rev{$c_{\mathrm{next}}$ has at least two free slots.}
\end{enumerate}

\rev{No staging SWAP is part of the action.  A qubit two hops from an
otherwise-usable port generates no teleport candidate at all; it can
only be brought to the port by SWAPs chosen in later iterations against
a scoring function in which that eventual teleport does not appear.
Condition~(iii) further means the destination is whichever core the
shortest path selects, so alternative neighbours are never proposed
even when free.  The branching factor is therefore at most two
candidates per separated front gate---one off each end of the path.}

\rev{\dSABRE{} instead treats the same move as a \emph{macro-action}
that carries its own preparation: the triple
$(q,c_{\mathrm{next}},(\pi_s,\pi_d))$ of Section~\ref{sec:tele} bundles
the staging SWAPs $\dintra(p_1,n_s)$, both port evictions, and the
teledata into one atomically scored decision, and $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$
prices exactly that preparation.  A blocked port raises a candidate's
score; it does not remove the candidate.  Every neighbouring core with
a free slot is enumerated, and every link to it, so the branching
factor is $2\sum_{c'\in N(c_{\mathrm{src}})}|\text{links}(c_{\mathrm{src}},c')|$:
four candidates per remote gate on the B-grid, four to six on the
H-grid, against \TeleSABRE{}'s two.}

\rev{The two routers thus draw the feasibility line in different
places.  \TeleSABRE{} keeps capacity and port occupancy as \emph{hard
constraints} that delete actions; \dSABRE{} converts them into
\emph{cost}, and keeps the action.  This is why \dSABRE{} needs a
capacity penalty---without one it would evict recklessly, which is what
Table~\ref{tab:mech} measures---and why \TeleSABRE{} needs a safety
valve, having no priced alternative when every teleport is illegal.}

\rev{A stronger question is worth separating from this one: when a
front gate $g{=}(p,p')$ has both operands in one core, does
\TeleSABRE{} ever teleport $p$ or $p'$ itself away, splitting a pair
that was one SWAP path from executing?  It does not, and it cannot.
Attraction paths are built only for gates whose operands are already in
different cores, and a teleport candidate moves only an endpoint of the
gate that generated its path; since a logical qubit lies in at most one
front gate, every teleported qubit belongs to a \emph{remote} front
gate.  The measurement agrees exactly: $0$ of $407$ teleports at 25
qubits and $0$ of $3725$ at 64 qubits move an operand of a same-core
front gate.  Neither router, then, destroys local work.  The difference
is one of ordering---\TeleSABRE{} will spend an EPR pair advancing some
\emph{other} qubit while a local pair waits, where \dSABRE{} drains the
local pair first---and it is a difference in the action set, not in the
tuning.}

\rev{This is a difference in policy, not a defect: deferring a teleport
until local work is drained is a heuristic commitment, and the
ablations of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} test it only indirectly.  The
measurement establishes two things.  The two routers follow different
action sequences, so the gap in Table~\ref{tab:main} is not
attributable to scoring alone; and the priority rule changes which
action is taken, rather than restating what the score would have
chosen anyway.}

\subsection{\rev{Stall handling, and where \TeleSABRE{} does not converge}}
\label{app:ts_stalls}

\rev{\TeleSABRE{}'s stall response is a \emph{safety valve}: after
$100$ iterations without progress it restores the layout saved at the
last progress point, narrows the front to a single gate, and drops the
extended set from the energy, retrying the reduced problem.  It is
therefore a checkpoint--restore mechanism, as \dSABRE{}'s is, and we
state the difference precisely: \dSABRE{} retires the stuck gate as an
atomic transaction, relaying room hop-by-hop along
$\mathrm{core\_path}(c_1,c_2)$ and teleporting one operand there, which
bounds a successful escape at $D$ EPR pairs and guarantees $|W|$ strictly
decreases (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}); \TeleSABRE{} narrows the
subproblem and re-enters the same greedy loop, with no forcing step and
no bound.  When that fails it exhausts its iteration budget and the
attempt is abandoned.}

\rev{On the 64-qubit suite this is not hypothetical.  Random fails on
all ten attempts of all three seeds, reporting no successful run, with
the safety valve firing $53$ times per attempt; it is the dash in
Table~\ref{tab:main}.  QAOA converges, but only after retries---six
attempts are abandoned before the seventh succeeds at $956$ EPR---which
is why a single-attempt run of QAOA reports far fewer operations than
the table does.  AE, by contrast, succeeds on its first attempt.  We
note this because the behavioural counts of
Table~\ref{tab:ts_actions} come from streaming one attempt per
circuit, which is the right unit for observing how the scorer chooses
among candidates but not for reading off EPR totals; the totals in
Table~\ref{tab:ts_telegate} are taken from the full protocol instead.}

\section{Comparison with pytket-dqc}
\label{app:pytketdqc}

\textbf{Background.}
\texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{pytketdqc2024} is a Python library from
Quantinuum that distributes circuits over multiple QPUs using
\emph{gate teleportation} (detached gates): a pre-shared e-bit allows
a non-local controlled-unitary to be executed without moving the
qubit, and consecutive non-local gates on the same wire can share the
same e-bit (multi-gate amortisation).  This contrasts with
\dSABRE{}'s \emph{state teleportation}, which physically moves a
qubit into a target core so that all subsequent gates on it run
locally.  Both primitives consume EPR pairs, but the reported counts
are not directly comparable: \texttt{pytket-dqc} charges one e-bit per
edge in the Steiner tree connecting all servers in a hyperedge, with
multiple consecutive non-local gates on the same wire amortised into a
single hyperedge.  \dSABRE{} charges one EPR per qubit-teleportation
hop regardless of how many gates follow (see Section~\ref{sec:setup}).  \texttt{pytket-dqc} assigns qubits to
QPUs \emph{statically} via KaHyPar hypergraph partitioning and does not
model intra-QPU SWAP routing; its output is an e-bit count only.

\subsection{\rev{Communication capacity: what the tool's authors claim, and
the regime they claim it in}}
\label{app:pytket_capacity}

\rev{Section~\ref{sec:pytket} reports that 13 of 21 \texttt{pytket-dqc}
distributions cannot be built within the $\deg(c)$ communication ports our
cores provide.  The underlying limitation is documented by the tool's
authors~\cite{andresmartinez2024heterogeneous}: they list the bounded
link-qubit register $\epsilon(A)$ among the properties a distribution should
respect, state that none of their optimisation routines take it into account,
and defer its satisfaction to a final pass over the generated circuit.  They
also note that the bound cannot be checked from their intermediate
representation at all---one must generate the distributed circuit and count
link qubits, which is not the same as counting e-bits, because register space
is reused.  That is the measurement our probe performs.}

\rev{They further argue that link-qubit registers stay manageable in practice,
and it is worth being precise about the regime in which that is established,
because it is not ours.  The supporting experiments use Pauli-gadget circuits
on small-world networks of three to five modules with an \emph{average module
size of four}, and the bounded arm fixes the register at \emph{three} link
qubits per module.  Their conclusion is that register size tracks average
module size rather than circuit size.  Our device sits far outside that range:
a $4{\times}4$ core offers $16$ physical qubits and $\deg(c)\le3$ ports, so the
fraction of each module given over to communication is roughly $19\%$ against
their $75\%$.  Their own finding---that demand scales with module size---is
therefore not contradicted by ours; it predicts it.  A core large enough to be
worth distributing over is a core whose port count is small relative to its
data capacity, and that is exactly where the amortisation the cost model
rewards stops being executable.}

\rev{The concurrency is structural rather than an artefact of gate order.  On
the 25-qubit AE circuit, whose published distribution needs $19$ simultaneous
link qubits against the $2$ the B-grid provides, every one of the $19$ links
live at the peak belongs to a hyperedge spanning $6$ to $31$ gates, and the
widest is held open across $2161$ of the circuit's $2218$ commands; not one is
a single-gate hyperedge.  Serialising them would require commuting whole
groups of controlled operations past the rotations that separate them on the
shared target register, which is not a valid rewrite.  The repair pass
described in the same appendix of~\cite{andresmartinez2024heterogeneous} splits
one offending hyperedge and regenerates the entire distributed circuit,
repeating as required; its cost therefore grows with precisely the nesting
depth that makes a distribution cheap, which is what our timing measurement
shows.  Both measurements are reproduced by \texttt{code/probe\_link\_qubits.py}
and \texttt{code/probe\_pytket\_repair.py}; the latter also contains a
five-gate circuit on two servers for which two simultaneous link qubits are
provably required, and for which the reported cost of $2$ e-bits becomes $4$
once the distribution is repaired to fit one port.}

benchmarks:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{KaHyPar weight-0 patch.}  \texttt{pytket-dqc} represents
  the circuit as a hypergraph whose qubit vertices carry weight~1 and
  gate vertices weight~0.  KaHyPar~$\geq$1.3.5 rejects zero-weight
  vertices.  We patched the allocator source file to
  assign weight~1 to all vertices and increase each server's target
  block size by $\lceil n_\mathrm{gates}/k \rceil$, so the capacity
  constraint still effectively counts only qubit vertices.

\item \emph{64-qubit QASM width.}  The pytket QASM loader defaults to
  a 32-bit classical register maximum; the 64-qubit benchmarks carry
  a 64-bit measurement register.  We pass \texttt{maxwidth=128} to
  \texttt{circuit\_from\_qasm}.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
All other \texttt{pytket-dqc} internals are unmodified.  We use the
\texttt{HypergraphPartitioning} allocator (KaHyPar connectivity
metric), best of 5 random seeds.  The QPU topology matches the core
graph of each architecture: a $2{\times}2$ grid (4 QPUs) for the
25q/36q B-grid and a $2{\times}3$ grid (6 QPUs) for the 64q H-grid.

Table~\ref{tab:fair} of the main text.  Here we

\textbf{Discussion.}
Starting from an identical qubit-to-core partition, \dSABRE{} reduces
EPR consumption over pytket-dqc by a geometric mean of $56.8\%$ on the
25q suite, $11.9\%$ on the 36q suite, and $41.5\%$ on the 64q suite.
Bear in mind the counting asymmetry noted in the main paper:
\texttt{pytket-dqc} amortises consecutive non-local gates into a
single hyperedge, so its e-bit advantage is most pronounced on
circuits with long runs of gates on the same cross-core wire.
\dSABRE{}'s EPR advantage is largest for circuits with high CX
density (ae, qft, qnn, random), where state teleportation moves a
qubit once and all subsequent gates run locally regardless of their
number.  On the 36q suite the gmean advantage is narrower because
\texttt{dj} regresses ($3\!\to\!12$ EPRs, $+300\%$): its interaction
graph is sparse enough that KaHyPar's static partition already
achieves near-zero cross-core communication, and \dSABRE{}'s
dynamic teleport decisions then disrupt this near-optimal static
assignment.

\textbf{Compile time.}
pytket-dqc's wall time is dominated by KaHyPar and scales with the
number of hyperedges (2-qubit gates): from $\sim$$0.1$\,s on tiny
circuits to $137$\,s on qnn-64q (5 seeds).  \dSABRE{}'s routing
time from a fixed initial layout is comparable for large circuits
($120.8$\,s for qnn-64q) and negligible ($<0.1$\,s) for small ones.
For circuits with few 2-qubit gates, pytket-dqc's KaHyPar overhead
exceeds \dSABRE{}'s routing time by one to two orders of magnitude.

\section{Comparison with DMapS}
\label{app:dmaps}

\textbf{Background.}
DMapS~\cite{luo2025dmaps} is a recent end-to-end DQC compiler that
shares \dSABRE{}'s problem framing -- jointly minimising EPR pair
usage and local SWAP insertions on a heterogeneous quantum chip
network -- but takes a different architectural approach.  Its mapping
stage \emph{DMapS-M} is two-stage: a KaHyPar hypergraph partition
assigns logical qubits to chips, then a SABRE-style routine maps
qubits to physical slots in parallel within each chip.  Its routing
stage \emph{DMapS-R} is a SABRE-derived heuristic that, at every
front-layer step, classifies non-executable two-qubit gates into four
categories (local-only $G_l$, remote needing telegate $G_{rnt}$,
remote-or-local $G_{rao}$, and remote-any $G_{ran}$) and \emph{always
prefers local SWAP candidates over remote ones} when both could
satisfy a front-layer gate.

\textbf{Cross-chip primitive in the output.}
Inspection of \texttt{router/car\_layout\_routing.py} shows that
DMapS-R only ever inserts \texttt{SwapGate} ops -- there is no
Cat-Comm or telegate primitive emitted in the routed circuit.  The
coupling map handed to the router includes the inter-chip
``remote connection'' edges, so a front-layer CX whose two qubits
happen to land on an inter-chip-link endpoint pair fires as a plain
CX (cost-modelled as one EPR via Cat-Comm in
\texttt{\_rm\_calculate\_cost}); every other unexecutable two-qubit
gate is resolved by inserting one or more SWAPs, of which the
\emph{remote} ones are the algorithm's main cross-chip mechanism.
A remote SWAP that exchanges two qubits across a chip boundary can
be decomposed into two one-way state teleportations -- one for each
direction -- and therefore consumes \emph{two} EPRs per remote SWAP.
\dSABRE{}'s teledata, in contrast, performs only the one-way half:
the incoming qubit lands in a slot that the layout has explicitly
kept free, so no return teleport is needed and the cost is one EPR
per relocation.  This 2$\times$ asymmetry in per-relocation cost is
the architectural source of the EPR gap observed below.

\textbf{Implementation notes.}
We installed DMapS at commit \verb|main| from
\url{https://github.com/RoccoLoter/DMapS} in an isolated Python~3.10
conda environment, using the pinned \texttt{qiskit==0.39.2},
\texttt{kahypar==1.3.7}, and \texttt{retworkx} that DMapS requires.
Five upstream changes were required to run DMapS over the full
$18$-circuit suite without crashes.  None of them touch any
algorithmic component of DMapS; they fix
import-time, register-bookkeeping, and partition-bookkeeping bugs.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Optional pytket-dqc baseline.}\;
  The top of \texttt{router/multi\_mode\_routing.py} unconditionally
  imports \texttt{tket\_dqc\_map} from DMapS's bundled
  \texttt{comparison\_algorithms/pytket\_dqc} subtree, which in turn
  needs a pytket-qiskit ABI that conflicts with DMapS's own qiskit
  pin.  We wrapped the import in \texttt{try/except} and stubbed the
  symbols so that the DMapS-M$+$DMapS-R pipeline, which does not call
  them, still loads.  The \texttt{tket\_dqc} comparison baselines
  inside DMapS are not exercised here -- they are not on our path and
  are evaluated separately in Appendix~\ref{app:pytketdqc}.

\item \emph{Hard-coded canonical-register size.}\;
  The output-conversion helper \texttt{\_conv\_circ\_qubits\_idx} in
  the same file allocates a final qiskit \texttt{QuantumRegister}
  with \texttt{size=66}.  The author left a TODO comment to fix this.
  For any device with more than $66$ physical qubits (in our case the
  $6{\times}16 = 96$-qubit H-grid), the routed circuit cannot be
  materialised.  We replace the constant with
  \texttt{size = max(int(name[1:]) for name in qubits\_name\_n\_idx)\,+\,1},
  derived from the device qubit-name map that DMapS already passes
  into the function.

\item \emph{Multi-register circuit rewriting in
  \texttt{generate\_new\_circuit}.}\;
  The same file builds a single canonical register and rewrites every
  input gate as
  \texttt{Qubit(register=canonical\_register, index=qubit.index)},
  where \texttt{qubit.index} is the qubit's position \emph{within its
  own original register}, not its global position.  When the input
  circuit carries more than one quantum register -- as the
  MQTBench-emitted QAE (\texttt{eval}\,$+$\,\texttt{q}) and QPE
  (\texttt{q}\,$+$\,\texttt{psi}) circuits do -- the index-$0$
  qubits of each original register both rewrite to
  \texttt{canonical[0]}.  Any two-qubit gate touching both registers'
  index-$0$ qubits then triggers a
  \texttt{CircuitError('duplicate qubit arguments')}.  We replaced
  \texttt{qubit.index} with the qubit's global circuit index obtained
  by \texttt{input\_circ.find\_bit(qubit).index}, eliminating the
  collision for any number of input registers.  This fixes the
  failures on \texttt{ae-25}, \texttt{ae-64}, and \texttt{qpeexact-36}.

\item \emph{Partitioner indexing of isolated qubits.}\;
  In \texttt{partitioner/qucirc\_partitioner.py}, the hypergraph
  builder records each circuit qubit in \texttt{vers\_wgt\_dict} only
  if that qubit appears in some two-qubit-gate block, then later the
  \texttt{run()} method iterates
  \texttt{quantum\_circuit.qubits} and indexes the resulting
  \texttt{qubits\_tmp\_idx} bidict by each one
  (line~104).  Qubits that participate only in single-qubit gates
  -- typical for the Bernstein--Vazirani query qubits in
  \texttt{bv-36}, where 18 of 36 qubits are idle under CX -- never get
  a temp index and trip a \texttt{KeyError}.  We pre-seed
  \texttt{vers\_wgt\_dict} with weight~1 for every qubit that appears
  in any op of the DAG (single-qubit ops included), but \emph{not} for
  canonical-register slots that no op references; the latter are
  padding qubits introduced when the device has more physical qubits
  than the input circuit uses, and giving them positive weight would
  inflate the initial-partition capacity demand and trip an
  \texttt{IndexError} in the block-filling loop.  Weight is~$1$
  (not~$0$) because KaHyPar~1.3.7 rejects zero-weight vertices.  In
  the dependent comprehension at line~104 we additionally guard
  against padding qubits with \texttt{if qubit in qubits\_tmp\_idx},
  which is safe because the resulting \texttt{init\_partition} array
  is only used by a commented-out
  \texttt{setInitialPartition} call.  This fixes the failure on
  \texttt{bv-36}.

\item \emph{Multiprocessing entry-point.}\;
  \texttt{mapper/two\_phase\_mapper} uses
  \texttt{multiprocessing.Pool} for the per-chip parallel SABRE
  passes.  On macOS the default start method is \texttt{spawn},
  which re-imports the calling module; without an
  \texttt{if \_\_name\_\_ == "\_\_main\_\_"} guard a fork-bomb
  ensues.  We added the guard to our driver script.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Patches (1)--(4) live inside DMapS and total fewer than thirty lines
across two files; (5) is in our adapter only.  All of them are
marked with a \texttt{PATCH:} comment so they can be located by
\texttt{grep} when DMapS is upgraded.  With these in place, every
circuit in the $18$-circuit benchmark suite produces a routed circuit
and a metrics record; the failure column of
Table~\ref{tab:dmaps_results} is now empty.

\textbf{Driver script.}
A self-contained driver, \texttt{code/run\_dmaps\_bench.py}, performs
the following for every (suite, circuit) pair:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Build the architecture from the same
  \texttt{build\_b\_grid\_architecture(2,2,4)} or
  \texttt{build\_h\_grid\_architecture(2,3,4)} call used by the main
  \dSABRE{} benchmark, and serialise the chip graph plus inter-core
  links into DMapS's device JSON schema
  (\texttt{"has multiple chips"}, \texttt{"chips"},
  \texttt{"remote connection"}).  Physical qubits are renumbered so
  that DMapS's internal global index agrees with our chip-major
  ordering, giving the cheap \texttt{chip = idx\,//\,16} lookup used
  later.
\item Run DMapS with default parameters
  (\texttt{chip\_type="zcz"}, \texttt{intra\_chip\_all2all=False},
  \texttt{heuristic="lookahead"}) five times per circuit.  DMapS
  exposes no random seed, so reruns are independent KaHyPar partitions.
\item Classify every two-qubit op in the returned mapped circuit by
  the chip affiliations of its endpoints.  A cross-chip CX (one that
  fires on an inter-chip coupling edge) contributes one EPR -- the
  Cat-Comm price implicit in DMapS's cost model.  A cross-chip SWAP
  contributes two EPRs, since it is realisable as two one-way
  teleportations exchanging the two qubits across the boundary.  An
  intra-chip SWAP contributes one local SWAP.  The overall overhead
  reported in Table~\ref{tab:dmaps_results} uses the DMapS paper's
  weighting $1{:}10{:}20$ (local SWAP : remote CX : remote SWAP).
\item Suppress KaHyPar's C-level stdout via file-descriptor
  redirection.  Best-of-five and the full per-seed record are written
  to \texttt{code/results/results\_dmaps\_bench.json}.
\end{enumerate}

(one EPR via Cat-Comm) without paying for any qubit relocation,
whereas \dSABRE{} always teleports a qubit first.  Second, on every
remaining circuit, including all of \texttt{qft}, \texttt{qnn},
\texttt{random}, \texttt{qaoa-36}, \texttt{ae}, \texttt{qpeexact-36},
\texttt{bv-36}, and \texttt{dj-36}, DMapS loses on EPR by between
$1.60\times$ and $9.33\times$.  The largest gaps are on circuits
where \dSABRE{} relocates a single hot qubit with one teleport and
amortises many subsequent two-qubit gates locally (\texttt{bv-36}:
\dSABRE{} pays~1 EPR to move the answer bit once; DMapS pays~7 by
firing five Cat-Comm CXs and one TP-Comm SWAP).

The breakdown in the \texttt{rCX} / \texttt{rSWAP} columns explains
why.  On the dense rows, the EPR spend that goes through remote
SWAPs ($2\!\cdot\!\textsf{rSWAP}$) is comparable to or larger than
the spend that goes through cross-chip CXs.  For example
\texttt{qnn-64q} attributes $\tfrac{2\cdot264}{933}\approx 57\%$ of
its EPRs to remote SWAPs and \texttt{random-64q} attributes
$\tfrac{2\cdot380}{1138}\approx 67\%$.  Each of those SWAPs is the
two-teleport bidirectional exchange described above; \dSABRE{}
relocates the same qubit with a single one-way teleportation into a
reserved free slot, paying half the EPR cost.

Compounding this, DMapS-M partitions the logical qubits with KaHyPar
\emph{before} routing and never relocates a qubit to a different
chip mid-execution; the only way to move a qubit across chips at
routing time is the same 2-EPR remote SWAP.  \dSABRE{}'s teleport
action is available throughout routing, so it can correct mapping
mistakes on the fly at one EPR each.  The combination -- 2$\times$
per-relocation cost, plus an inability to amortise multiple remote
gates on the same control qubit through a single relocation -- is
why the gap widens with circuit density rather than circuit size
\emph{per se}.

The local-SWAP picture is the dual of the EPR picture.  Local SWAP
ratios are below~$1$ for $16$ of the $18$ circuits (range
$0.08$ -- $0.95$); the two exceptions are the very-sparse
\texttt{bv-36} and \texttt{dj-36} (ratios $1.92$ and $5.00$), where
\dSABRE{} routes essentially everything with a single teleport and a
handful of local SWAPs, leaving DMapS's chain of remote SWAPs no room
to save anything in absolute terms.  On dense circuits, DMapS-R's
explicit local-SWAP-first heuristic and its willingness to take the
2-EPR remote SWAP shortcut both reduce local SWAP count compared
with \dSABRE{}, which spends a small number of local SWAPs walking a
qubit to a teleport launchpad before relocating it.  The reduction
in local SWAPs is not a saving in any meaningful sense: under the
DMapS paper's own $1{:}10{:}20$ weighting, the EPR overspend
($10\!\cdot\!\Delta\textsf{rCX} + 20\!\cdot\!\Delta\textsf{rSWAP}$)
overwhelms the local-SWAP underspend on every dense row of
Table~\ref{tab:dmaps_vs_dsabre}.

Variance behaviour confirms that the partition step dominates.
Structured circuits have $\text{stdev}/\text{mean}$ below $10\%$
across the five seeds; dense circuits range $5$ -- $16\%$,
with \texttt{qaoa-36q} reaching $\pm 1053$ (range $5618$ -- $8277$)
and \texttt{qnn-64q} reaching $\pm 1666$ (range $13092$ -- $17035$).
A single sub-optimal KaHyPar cut can change the cross-chip work by
tens of percent.  This is consistent with the original DMapS
paper's own remark that partition quality bounds the achievable EPR.
Cumulative wall-clock for the full $18$-circuit run was approximately
$15$~minutes on a single Apple-silicon core.

\textbf{Comm-qubit handling in the paper vs.\ in the released code.}
The DMapS paper draws a sharp distinction between \emph{data qubits}
(which store program information) and \emph{communication qubits}
(which exist only to create remote EPR pairs, with the device
figures deliberately hiding the comm qubits).  Each remote physical
link, in the paper's text, ``typically links one data qubit and
two communication qubits'' on either side, giving an intended
topology of \texttt{data}\,$\to$\,\texttt{comm}\,$\to$\,\texttt{comm}
\,$\to$\,\texttt{data} across a chip boundary; the four-class gate
taxonomy ($g_l, g_{rao}, g_{rnt}, g_{ran}$) refers explicitly to
``data qubits connected to communication qubits'' as the special
boundary slots from which Cat-Comm CXs can be fired.

The released DMapS implementation does \emph{not} carry this
distinction.  We verified two facts:
\begin{itemize}
\item All bundled device JSONs list every \texttt{"remote connection"}
  endpoint as a regular member of the corresponding chip's
  \texttt{qubits} array.  For example, the file
  \texttt{tests/hardware\_info/zcz/one\_xiaohong\_5module\_net.json}
  --- the Net~Zuchongzhi device used in the paper's own experiments
  --- contains no separate comm-qubit entities.  The qubits the
  loader flags \texttt{is\_comm\_qubit=True} are exactly those whose
  names appear in a \texttt{"remote connection"} entry; i.e.\
  ordinary data qubits sitting on the chip boundary.
\item \texttt{ScChip.qubits\_num}, the value consumed as chip
  capacity by DMapS-M, is the full physical qubit list, comm
  flag-bearers included.  The paper's remark that ``their number is
  set equal to the data qubits adjacent to communication qubits''
  describes \emph{intent} but is not enforced anywhere in the code.
\end{itemize}

Consequently, inter-chip links in the loaded device are modelled as
a single coupling edge between two ordinary data qubits: the
\texttt{data}\,$\to$\,\texttt{comm}\,$\to$\,\texttt{comm}
\,$\to$\,\texttt{data} four-hop path of the paper is collapsed to a
single \texttt{data}\,$\to$\,\texttt{data} edge.  This matches the
code-level audit in the previous subsection: every cross-chip CX or
SWAP in the routed output lands on exactly one of these edges.  The Cat-Comm and
TP-Comm realisations of those cross-chip operations are not
materialised in the routed circuit; they only enter through the
\texttt{\_rm\_calculate\_cost} scoring term that charges
$2\cdot\textit{chip\_dist}-1$ EPRs per cross-chip CX.

Our adapter inherits the same modelling: each inter-chip link is one
edge between two of the chip's data qubits, those endpoints are
flagged \texttt{is\_comm\_qubit}, and chip capacity is the full
$16$-qubit grid.  This matches \dSABRE{}'s architecture
(\texttt{code/architecture.py}), which also flags inter-core-link
endpoints as comm qubits without removing them from the data-qubit
pool, so the head-to-head comparison is fair: both compilers see the
same number of usable slots per chip and the same inter-chip
topology.

\textbf{Fill-ratio sweep.}
The discussion above implies that DMapS's relative EPR cost should
shrink as the number of logical qubits approaches the number of
physical qubits, because \dSABRE{}'s one-way teleport advantage
depends on the existence of a free physical slot for the incoming
qubit to land in.  In the limit of zero free slots every
relocation has to displace an occupant, and dSABRE's cheapest move
becomes a full $2$-EPR exchange -- the same cost as DMapS's remote
SWAP.  To test this directly we ran the QFT family at
$n \in \{24, 36, 48, 56\}$ logical qubits on the same $64$-qubit
B-grid, varying \emph{fill} ($n/64 \in \{37.5\%, 56.2\%, 75.0\%, 87.5\%\}$)
while keeping the architecture fixed.  Each row is best of five
seeds, generated from \texttt{code/run\_fill\_sweep.py}.

\section{Entanglement-Lifetime Sensitivity}
\label{app:dmax}

Section~\ref{sec:lifetime} of the main paper reports that
\texttt{pytket-dqc}'s e-bit advantage on structured circuits is
conditional on the hardware sustaining entanglement across many gates.
This appendix gives the per-circuit sweep behind that conclusion.

Every \texttt{pytket-dqc} e-bit count assumes an \emph{unbounded}
entanglement lifetime: the link qubit created by one starting process
stays entangled until the last gate of its hyperedge has executed.
Bandini \emph{et al.}~\cite{bandini2026optimized} argue this is
unrealistic, since EPR-pair quality decays quickly, and cap
gates-per-EPR at $D_{\max}$, proposing $D_{\max}{=}3$ as realistic.
We re-score \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s distributions under this bound with a
counter that generalises its own cost model: an established link serves
at most $D_{\max}$ gates before it dies and must be re-established from
a still-live neighbour on the hyperedge's Steiner tree.  The counter is
conservative in \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s favour---intermediate links age
only when they themselves serve gates, embedded and home-server gates
age nothing, and wall-clock time is ignored---and reproduces
\texttt{pytket-dqc}'s own cost exactly at $D_{\max}{=}\infty$ on every
distribution evaluated.  \dSABRE{} needs no adjustment at any
$D_{\max}$: a teledata EPR pair is consumed instantaneously by its
teleport, so teledata counts are lifetime-invariant by construction.

Table~\ref{tab:dmax} reports the 64q sweep.  Three structural
observations.  (i)~The circuits on which gate teleportation wins at
$D_{\max}{=}\infty$ are exactly those whose distributions hold links
across many gates; QNN's winning distribution requires individual link
qubits to serve up to $102$ gates while remaining entangled across
$4262$ of the circuit's $8126$ CX time-slots, more than half its
execution.  (ii)~GHZ and Graphstate are $D_{\max}$-\emph{insensitive}:
their hyperedges hold roughly one gate each, so these residual wins
reflect partition quality rather than held entanglement, and the
shared-mapping experiment of the main paper shows \dSABRE{} matches
them when routed from the same partition.  (iii)~Dense circuits move
least (Random $908\!\to\!967$, Multiplier $2950\!\to\!5004$): with no or
short shared-control runs there is little amortisation to lose, and
\dSABRE{} already led those rows.

The same sweep on the smaller suites only widens \dSABRE{}'s margins.
At $D_{\max}{=}3$ the best \texttt{pytket-dqc} pipeline inflates AE-25
from $48$ to $136$ e-bits and QFT-25 from $59$ to $164$, and
QPEexact-36 inflates from $44$ to $192$, turning the one structured 36q
circuit it won into a $69\%$ \dSABRE{} advantage.  A $D_{\max}$-aware
re-partition could soften, though not eliminate, this inflation;
\texttt{pytket-dqc} currently offers no such option.

\TeleSABRE{} leads overall, as a compiled binary against interpreted Python
should, but loses to \dSABRE{} by $10\times$ on Graphstate, where its sampling
needs many iterations to converge; \dSABRE{} is slowest on the two largest
circuits, where Eq.~\ref{eq:complexity}'s $N_r$ lookahead term, not its $Mn$
scoring term, dominates.  \texttt{pytket-dqc} is an order of magnitude behind both even though its
column already includes its five attempts, and its cost is notably \emph{flat}
in circuit size---$239$--$479$\,s across a $6.6\times$ range of CX
count---because hypergraph partitioning and Steiner-tree embedding are
dominated by the partitioner rather than by gate count, and because four of the
nine circuits consumed their budget in the embedding distributor before falling
back.  The scalability rows show the same shapes at larger scale: across
$100\to360$ qubits \dSABRE{} rises $22\times$ ($159\to3428$\,s) and
\TeleSABRE{} $17\times$ over the two sizes it completes, while
\texttt{pytket-dqc} rises only $2.2\times$ ($900\to1990$\,s).  Its cost is set
