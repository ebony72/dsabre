\begin{abstract}
Distributed quantum computers execute a circuit across small quantum processing
units linked by entanglement. Their compiler must minimise consumption of
Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR) pairs while still routing large circuits
quickly and reliably on hardware with finite communication ports and finite
core capacity. We present \dSABRE{}, a distributed extension of the
widely used \SABRE{} routing principle. Rather than treating a teleport as a
high-cost SWAP, \dSABRE{} scores each inter-core teledata move as one
macro-action: the EPR pair it spends, the port staging it requires,
the destination capacity it consumes, and the front-layer progress
it provides with lookahead discounted by depth in the circuit's directed
acyclic graph (DAG). A hard capacity floor on every teleport, together with a
bounded recovery transaction, makes \dSABRE{} provably complete: it routes
every gate and never aborts at saturation, on any instance that passes a
pre-routing check. Across 21 Munich
Quantum Toolkit (MQT) Bench circuits with 25--64 logical qubits, \dSABRE{}
reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption by 49--62\% relative to \TeleSABRE{}.
It completes all 45 evaluated circuit--architecture instances against
\TeleSABRE{}'s 40, and compiles a 360-qubit QFT on twenty cores
in 48\,s. These results
show that capacity-aware, feasibility-preserving extensions of a simple
\SABRE{}-style objective can provide effective, scalable, and robust distributed
routing. Code is available at \url{https://github.com/ebony72/dsabre}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Distributed quantum computers (DQCs) combine several small quantum processing
units (QPUs) through entanglement links~\cite{cuomo2020towards}. They offer a
practical route to scaling beyond a single processor, but introduce a
compilation problem absent from monolithic devices: a two-qubit gate whose
operands occupy different cores requires inter-core communication. In this
work that communication is supplied by an Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR)
pair. EPR generation is slow, noisy and rate-limited relative to local
operations in current hardware~\cite{stephenson2020high,daiss2021quantum}, so
a router should avoid spending an EPR pair unless the move it enables is
worthwhile.

Distributed compilation has three connected tasks: (i)~allocate logical
qubits to cores, (ii)~choose how to realise a non-local interaction, and
(iii)~route and schedule the resulting local and inter-core
operations~\cite{escofet2024review}. Hypergraph partitioning addresses the first task
by exposing global communication structure~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024}.
An allocation alone, however, does not ensure that the computation can execute
on a device with finite ports, finite core capacity, and non-trivial intra-core
connectivity. During routing, a state may have to be moved to a communication
port, that port may be occupied, and the target core may lack a spare physical
slot. These are dynamic constraints of the evolving layout, not properties of
the initial partition alone.

\textbf{A practical routing objective.}
A useful compiler must be effective, efficient, and robust: it should reduce
the expensive resource, compile instances at useful scale, and return a valid
routed circuit. These
requirements motivate a \SABRE{}-style design. \SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling}
is the layout and routing pass Qiskit~\cite{qiskit2024} selects by default: it
chooses a SWAP by the progress it makes on the \emph{front-layer}
two-qubit gates, with bounded lookahead. Its appeal is not global optimality
but a simple, scalable decision rule that performs well in production
compilation flows. The question addressed here is what that rule must price
when the machine is several QPUs rather than one.

The answer is not to replace a SWAP by a teleport on a higher-weight edge. A
SWAP is symmetric and preserves the occupancy of the regions it joins. A
teleport is directional: it is feasible only after its source state reaches a
communication port, and it consumes a free slot in the destination core.
Thus, an inter-core move has three inseparable consequences: it spends an EPR
pair, may require port staging before it is possible, and reduces the capacity
left for future moves. Treating these effects as one macro-action is the
unifying design principle of \dSABRE{}.

\dSABRE{} is a \SABRE{}-style teledata router that scores a candidate
macro-action---source staging, port eviction if needed, and one
teleport---by the routing progress it buys and the resources it consumes. The
progress term is \SABRE{}'s front-layer distance reduction plus a lookahead
discounted by depth in the circuit's directed acyclic graph (DAG), the
dependency graph defined in Section~\ref{sec:qcircuits}. The two added
costs are an availability term for staging at a port and a graded capacity
term for the destination core: the two routing-state constraints introduced
when a one-way teleport replaces a local SWAP. Latency, coherence, link
contention, and fidelity are teleport-specific too, but lie outside the model
optimised here (Section~\ref{sec:future}). The capacity floor is deliberately
kept out of the score, as a hard admission test applied when candidates are
generated (Section~\ref{sec:principle}): a weight can be outvoted by a large
progress term, an admission test cannot. The score therefore ranks feasible
progress-making moves, while the floor and the bounded recovery transaction keep
finite capacity from becoming an unrecoverable mid-route dead end
(Sections~\ref{sec:principle}--\ref{sec:recovery}).

\textbf{Scope of communication.}
This paper deliberately optimises teledata, which moves one logical state to a
new core and consumes its EPR pair immediately. Because no entanglement is
retained, an EPR count does not depend on how long a link qubit can hold its
state, and the occupancy of the cores is the only resource a route must manage
over time---the capacity problem studied
here. Telegate can be preferable when a group of
compatible remote gates shares an entangled resource, but its benefit depends
on gate grouping, link-buffer occupancy, and the lifetime of retained
entanglement~\cite{eisert2000optimal}: it is a distinct joint
primitive-selection problem, not an omitted zero-cost candidate. \dSABRE{}
does not claim teledata dominates telegate, and identifies burst extraction and
telegate selection as extensions (Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\textbf{Scope of architecture and comparison.}
The algorithm is defined on an arbitrary \emph{connected} coupling graph with
designated ports and queries that graph through distance tables: each core's
intra-core graph is connected and so is the core graph, so every distance the
router queries is finite. Nothing further is assumed---not degree, regularity,
diameter, or the number and placement of ports.

The evaluation exercises that generality over four architectures: the
grid-of-grids devices of \TeleSABRE{} and two networks of IBM heavy-hex cores,
a ring and a star. The reported EPR reductions are established across all
four, which differ in core degree, diameter, and port placement. Likewise, EPR counts are directly
comparable only when methods use
the same communication primitive, architecture, and cost accounting.
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is the primary head-to-head baseline
because it shares the teledata setting and local-SWAP accounting.
DMapS~\cite{luo2025dmaps} and
\texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{andresmartinez2024heterogeneous,pytketdqc2024}
count EPR pairs under a different primitive---a remote SWAP and a telegate,
respectively---and under different assumptions about communication capacity
and entanglement lifetime. Those two comparisons are therefore reported
separately, each stating the assumptions its EPR count has to be read under.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A unified capacity-aware teledata score.} We formulate every
inter-core move as a macro-action and extend \SABRE{}'s distance-progress
objective with the two routing-state costs that distinguish teleportation
from a
SWAP: port availability and the destination capacity it consumes. The score
uses front-layer distance reduction plus DAG-depth-discounted lookahead, and
exhausts executable local front-layer work before considering an
EPR-consuming action.

\item \textbf{Feasibility-aware routing with completion conditions.} We give
pre-routing capacity conditions, a hard legality floor, and a recovery
transaction (Algorithm~\ref{alg:recover}) for the saturated
states in which the ordinary loop has no legal move that makes progress.
Under these checkable conditions \dSABRE{} routes every gate and does not
abort; this is a completion guarantee, not a claim of EPR optimality.
The measurements locate where it earns its place: no reported route among
the 21 circuits at 25--64 qubits invokes recovery at all, whereas at scale it
retires 6, 10, and 66 gates over the reported 100-, 200-, and 360-qubit
routes.

\item \textbf{Evidence of practical effectiveness and efficiency.} Across 21
Munich Quantum Toolkit (MQT) Bench circuits with 25--64 logical qubits,
\dSABRE{} reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption by 49--62\% relative to
\TeleSABRE{}. It completes all 45 evaluated circuit--architecture
instances, against \TeleSABRE{}'s 40. The implementation also compiles a 360-qubit
QFT on twenty cores in $48$\,s, with compile time growing near-linearly in
gate count at fixed architecture (Section~\ref{sec:scalability}). Ablations separate the effects of capacity handling,
lookahead construction, and recovery, while the cross-model experiments
distinguish like-for-like comparisons from results contingent on different EPR
use assumptions.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:background} gives the circuit, teleportation, architecture and
\SABRE{} background; Section~\ref{sec:dsabre} presents \dSABRE{} and its
completion argument; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} evaluates it; and
Sections~\ref{sec:related}--\ref{sec:future} discuss related work and
extensions.  Supplementary material at the URL above carries the full
completion proof, the complexity validation, per-circuit and per-seed counts,
benchmark provenance, the parameter and cost-model sweeps, and the capacity,
lifetime and action-level audits behind the cross-model comparisons.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Circuits and routing}
\label{sec:qcircuits}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{fig/dag_example}
\caption{DAG for a circuit with $\mathrm{CX}(q_1,q_2)$,
$\mathrm{H}(q_3)$, and $\mathrm{CX}(q_2,q_3)$. The first two gates have no
predecessors and form the initial front layer. The final CX becomes ready only
after both have executed.}
\label{fig:dag-example}
\end{figure}

A quantum circuit is represented as a DAG: each
node is a gate and each edge records a dependency induced by a shared logical
qubit. The \emph{front layer} is the set of dependency-free gates and can be
executed in parallel when the hardware permits. This paper considers
single-qubit (1Q) gates and two-qubit (2Q) gates, in particular CX gates.
Together, CX and arbitrary 1Q unitaries are \emph{exactly} universal:
they decompose any $n$-qubit unitary~\cite{barenco1995elementary}, whereas a
finite set such as $\{\mathrm{CX},H,T\}$ is only \emph{approximately} so.
Hardware provides a finite set: the benchmark circuits of
Section~\ref{sec:setup} are transpiled into IBM's
$\{\mathrm{CX},R_z,\sqrt{X},X\}$.

Let a circuit contain $n$ logical qubits and let the target architecture have
$P\geq n$ physical qubits. An initial layout is an injective mapping from
logical to physical qubits. A 2Q gate is executable only when its operands
are adjacent in the coupling graph. Routing therefore inserts local SWAPs and,
in a distributed machine, inter-core communication operations; each local SWAP
costs $c_{\mathrm{swap}}$ and each inter-core teleport $c_{\mathrm{tele}}$. A SWAP
exchanges the states of adjacent qubits and decomposes into three CX gates.
Figure~\ref{fig:dag-example} illustrates the DAG and front-layer notion used
by the router; \emph{circuit depth} is the length of its longest path.



\subsection{Teleportation}
\label{sec:teleportation}

Quantum teleportation transfers a qubit state between two locations using a
pre-shared EPR pair and classical communication;
the state is removed from the sender and reconstructed at the
receiver~\cite{bennett1993teleporting}. In this paper, one consumed EPR pair is one
unit of inter-core communication cost, and ``EPR'' as a unit (e.g.\ ``$10$
EPRs'') denotes EPR-pair count.

Two communication primitives are relevant. \emph{Teledata} moves a logical
qubit to another core, after which subsequent gates may execute locally.
\emph{Telegate} implements a non-local 2Q gate without relocating either
logical qubit~\cite{eisert2000optimal}. Teledata can amortise its cost over a
qubit's subsequent residence in the destination core. Telegate can amortise
one entangled state over a compatible group of remote gates, but this requires
an available link qubit and entanglement that survives for the group duration;
Section~\ref{sec:pytket} measures what those two assumptions are worth.

\subsection{Distributed architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

We model the distributed quantum computer as a weighted coupling graph
$G_\arch=(V_\arch,E_\arch,w)$. Its physical-qubit vertices are partitioned into $K$ cores,
$V_\arch=V_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_K$. Intra-core edges $E_{\mathrm{intra}}$ model
local connectivity and have unit weight. Inter-core edges
$E_{\mathrm{inter}}$ join designated communication ports and have weight
$w_{\mathrm{link}}=10$, the cost of a teleport relative to a unit intra-core
hop. A communication port is an ordinary physical slot that also
terminates an inter-core link; it may hold a data qubit when it is not used
for communication. We assume throughout that $G_\arch$ is \emph{connected},
and in the stronger form the router actually requires: the subgraph induced
by each $V_i$ is connected, and so is the core graph on $\{1,\dots,K\}$
induced by $E_{\mathrm{inter}}$. Every distance below is then finite.

\dSABRE{} uses three distance tables: physical distance $\dphys$,
intra-core distance $\dintra$, and core-graph distance $\dcore$. It therefore
applies to arbitrary connected intra-core graphs and inter-core topologies. Our
experiments instantiate the grid-of-grids architectures of
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:arch}, and the
networks of IBM heavy-hex cores of Section~\ref{sec:heavyhex}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{fig/architectures}}
\caption{Distributed architectures used in the experiments. Grey vertices are
physical qubits, teal vertices are communication ports, and bold edges are
inter-core EPR links. Each core is a $4\times4$ grid: (a) a $2\times2$
B-grid and (b) a $2\times3$ H-grid.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Physical distances need not be stored in a dense all-pairs table: every
inter-core path exits and enters through ports, so with $\Pi_i$ the ports of
core $i$, for $p\in V_i$ and $q\in V_j$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\dphys(p,q)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi_i,\,\pi'\in\Pi_j}\big[
  &\dintra(p,\pi)+\dport(\pi,\pi')\\
  &+\dintra(\pi',q)\big].
\end{split}
\label{eq:dphys}
\end{equation}
The implementation memoises these queries.

\subsection{\SABRE{}-style routing}
\label{sec:routing}

\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} is a heuristic router that repeatedly executes
ready gates and inserts a candidate SWAP when a front-layer 2Q gate is not
adjacent. Its score favours a SWAP that reduces the distance of
front-layer gates while also considering a bounded lookahead set. We use the
\LightSABRE{} form~\cite{zou2024lightsabrelightweightenhancedsabre}:
\begin{equation}
H = \frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{g\in F}\Delta(g)
+ w_e\,\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{g\in E}\Delta(g),
\label{eq:sabre}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the front layer, $E$ is the extended set, $w_e$ weights the
lookahead term against the front-layer term, and
$\Delta(g)=d^g_{\mathrm{new}}-d^g_{\mathrm{old}}$ is the distance change for
gate $g$. A negative value denotes progress. \dSABRE{} retains this
front-layer-plus-lookahead principle, but adapts the candidate action and
score to account for port staging and finite capacity in the destination core.
Building on a heuristic that production toolchains already run keeps compile
time practical and the output executable: the router carries the layout
with it and admits only moves that layout allows. What remains open is what
the \SABRE{} objective must become when one chip becomes several.

\section{dSABRE}
\label{sec:dsabre}

\dSABRE{} is a \SABRE{}-style router for multi-core processors. It uses teledata alone: one teleport consumes one EPR pair and moves one logical qubit across one adjacent-core link. Thus, unlike an amortised telegate, no entanglement lifetime or live EPR buffer is modelled.

The routing state is a layout $\ell$ and inverse $\ell^{-1}$, a DAG $W$ of unexecuted gates, its front layer $F$ of gates with no unexecuted predecessors, and a bounded extended set $E$ beyond $F$. A front-layer 2Q gate is \emph{intra-core} when both operands are in one core and \emph{inter-core} otherwise. Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation.

\subsection{Routing Policy, Score, and Legality}
\label{sec:principle}

\dSABRE{} has two actions: an intra-core SWAP and an inter-core teleport. It first resolves non-adjacent intra-core front gates by local SWAPs. Only when none remain does it score teleports for inter-core front gates. This priority rule avoids imposing an artificial exchange rate between the local and global scorers.

A teleport may require port eviction and staging SWAPs, and consumes one free slot in its landing core. We treat these operations and the teleport as one \emph{macro-action}: a SWAP is a single primitive, while a teleport is a short sequence scored and applied as one unit. \emph{Atomic} below means that such a sequence either completes or leaves the state untouched. Recovery (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}) has the same property, but is the fallback for when neither ordinary action makes progress rather than a third ordinary action. If $\Delta\Phi(a)$ is the change action $a$ induces in a front-layer-plus-lookahead routing potential---negative when $a$ leaves the waiting gates closer to executable---the conceptual objective is
\begin{equation}
s(a)=\underbrace{\mathrm{cost}(a)}_{\text{resource spent}}
+\underbrace{\mathrm{pen}(a)}_{\text{capacity consumed}}
+\underbrace{\Delta\Phi(a)}_{\text{progress bought}}.
\label{eq:principle}
\end{equation}
All compared teleport candidates consume one EPR pair, so this constant is omitted from their ranking.

\paragraph*{Legality rule}
The capacity penalty ranks feasible moves; it does not determine feasibility. Feasibility is enforced by a separate \emph{legality floor}: an ordinary teleport is generated only if its landing core $c_{\mathrm{next}}$ has
\begin{equation}
f_{c_{\mathrm{next}}}\ge\phi_1=2,
\label{eq:legality}
\end{equation}
the free slots being counted before the move. A teleport consumes one slot of its landing core, so after the arrival that core still retains at least $\phi_1-1=1$ free slot, and local SWAPs preserve every core's occupancy. Hence ordinary routing never creates a full core, whose occupied communication port could otherwise prevent a later departure. Equation~\eqref{eq:legality} is used in candidate generation and in the recovery proof.

\subsection{Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarises the routing loop and Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsabre} states it in full.  The router retains a \emph{progress checkpoint}---the layout and its inverse, the remaining DAG $W$, the \emph{trace} of SWAPs, teleports and executed gates emitted so far, and the counters that summarise them---as they stood immediately after the last decrease in $|W|$, and initially the input layout, the full DAG and an empty trace.  Restoring it rewinds the trace as well as the layout, so the two never disagree: the operations left in the trace are exactly those that carry the input layout to the current one.  Each iteration is:

\textbf{P1: Execute.} Repeatedly execute front-layer 1Q gates and adjacent intra-core 2Q gates. Stop if $W$ is empty.

\textbf{P2: Classify.} Partition the remaining front layer into non-adjacent intra-core gates $F_{\mathrm{intra}}$ and inter-core gates $F_{\mathrm{inter}}$.

\textbf{P3: Route.} If $F_{\mathrm{intra}}\neq\emptyset$, apply the lowest-score local SWAP of each active core. Otherwise enumerate legal teleport macro-actions for $F_{\mathrm{inter}}$ and try them in score order. Each attempt is atomic: a failed eviction, staging sequence, or teleport restores the pre-attempt state. If no candidate is legal or none commits, restore the progress checkpoint and invoke recovery; by Lemma~\ref{lem:commit} in Section~\ref{sec:recovery} the second case cannot arise.

\textbf{P4: Checkpoint or recover.} A decrease in $|W|$ replaces the checkpoint and resets the stalled counter. Otherwise increment it; after $L_{\mathrm{deadlock}}$ stalled iterations, restore the checkpoint and invoke recovery. Recovery is atomic: it either retires its target gate and restores the reserve, or restores its entry state---a branch Theorem~\ref{thm:completion} in Section~\ref{sec:recovery} rules out, leaving it an assertion on the hypotheses rather than an expected outcome.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\dSABRE{} \texttt{route}$(C,\ell_0)$.  An empty legal candidate set goes straight to recovery rather than waiting out the stall window, since no local SWAP can create headroom in a neighbouring core.  \textsc{Recover} is the transaction of Section~\ref{sec:recovery}.}
\label{alg:dsabre}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $W\leftarrow$ working DAG of $C$; $\ell\leftarrow\ell_0$; initialise $\ell^{-1}$
\STATE verify the preconditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:completion}, relaying vacancies until Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant} holds; initialise a checkpoint\label{ln:pre}
\WHILE{$W\neq\emptyset$}
  \STATE Drain executable 1Q gates and adjacent intra-core front-layer 2Q gates
  \IF{$W=\emptyset$} \STATE \textbf{break} \ENDIF
  \STATE Form $F$, partition it into $F_{\rm intra}$ and $F_{\rm inter}$, and build $E$ if stale
  \IF{$F_{\rm intra}\neq\emptyset$}
    \FOR{each active core $c$ with $F_c\neq\emptyset$}
      \STATE Apply the minimum-score intra-core SWAP of core $c$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:intra}
    \ENDFOR
  \ELSE
    \STATE Enumerate teleport macro-actions for $F_{\rm inter}$ whose landing core $c'$ has $f_{c'}\ge\phi_1=2$
    \STATE Try candidates in score order; each attempt either commits atomically or restores its entry state
    \IF{the candidate set is empty, or no candidate commits}
      \STATE restore the checkpoint and invoke \textsc{Recover}$(W,\ell)$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$|W|$ decreased}
    \STATE replace the checkpoint and reset the stalled counter
  \ELSIF{the stalled counter reaches $L_{\rm deadlock}$}
    \STATE restore the checkpoint and invoke \textsc{Recover}$(W,\ell)$
  \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN routed circuit, final layout, and metrics
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Intra-Core SWAP Heuristic}
\label{sec:intra}

For active core $c$, let $F_c$ be its non-adjacent intra-core front gates and let $E_c\subseteq E$ contain the extended-set gates whose operands currently both reside in $c$. The global construction of $E$ supplies one consistent depth $\mathrm{dep}(g)$ for both scorers: the layer of $g$ in a \emph{breadth-first} (BFS) expansion of the remaining DAG, so a gate one dependency beyond the front layer has $\mathrm{dep}=1$, one beyond that $\mathrm{dep}=2$. For a candidate SWAP, define
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\Delta}_E^c=
\sum_{g\in E_c}\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g)}
\left(d^g_{\mathrm{new}}-d^g_{\mathrm{old}}\right),
\label{Delta_E_Tilde}
\end{equation}
where $0<\gamma\le1$ and both distances are $\dintra$. The candidates are the physical edges $(u,v)$ within $c$ incident to an operand of a gate in $F_c$; the selected SWAP minimises
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{intra}}=
\frac{\Delta_F^c}{|F_c|}+w_e\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}_E^c}{|E_c|}.
\label{eq:intra}
\end{equation}
When $E_c=\emptyset$ the lookahead term is zero. This is the \SABRE{} objective of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sabre} with one change: \SABRE{} weights every gate of its extended set equally, having filled that set in topological order, which fixes no notion of how far ahead a gate lies, whereas $\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g)}$ discounts by exactly that. Candidate evaluations are independent across active cores.

\subsection{Inter-Core Teleportation Scoring}
\label{sec:tele}

For an inter-core front gate $g=(q_1,q_2)$, both operands are considered as sources. For source $q_1$ in $c_{\mathrm{src}}$, the router enumerates each adjacent core $c_{\mathrm{next}}$ and each link $(\pi_s,\pi_d)$ joining them, retaining only candidates that satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:legality}. Let $p_1=\ell(q_1)$ and let $n_s$ be a neighbour of $\pi_s$ minimising $\dintra(p_1,n_s)$. The macro-action clears both ports, stages $q_1$ to $n_s$, and teleports it across $(\pi_s,\pi_d)$. Clearing a port means walking a free slot to it by intra-core SWAPs, so each core involved must hold one. The source core does, because ordinary routing leaves no core full, as Section~\ref{sec:recovery} proves; Eq.~\eqref{eq:legality} gives the landing core two, one of which walks to $\pi_d$ to receive the arriving state while the other leaves the core non-full once it lands.

The score is
\begin{equation}
s=d_{\mathrm{prep}}+c_{\mathrm{cap}}+\Delta_F+w_e\widetilde{\Delta}_E,
\label{eq:dsabre}
\end{equation}
where lower is better and
\begin{align}
d_{\mathrm{prep}}={}&\dintra(p_1,n_s)+d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi_s)+d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi_d), \\
c_{\mathrm{cap}}={}&c_{\mathrm{pen}}\max\{0,\tau-f_{c_{\mathrm{next}}}\}, \\
\Delta_F={}&d^g_{\mathrm{new}}-d^g_{\mathrm{old}}, \\
\widetilde{\Delta}_E={}&\sum_{h\in E(q_1)}\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(h)}
\left(d^h_{\mathrm{new}}-d^h_{\mathrm{old}}\right).
\label{eq:dEtilde}
\end{align}
Here $d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi)$ is the number of SWAPs that clear port $\pi$ and is zero when $\pi$ is empty, so an occupied port is priced rather than silently excluded. $E(q_1)\subseteq E$ is the set of extended-set gates having $q_1$ as an operand, and for a gate $h$ with operands $q_1$ and $q'$, $d^h_{\mathrm{old}}=\dphys(p_1,\ell(q'))$ and $d^h_{\mathrm{new}}=\dphys(\pi_d,\ell(q'))$.

$\Delta_F$ uses the same $\dphys$, and every distance term is measured on the weighted architecture graph $G_\arch$---an intra-core edge costs one, an inter-core link $10$---so $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$, $\Delta_F$ and $\widetilde{\Delta}_E$ share one scale. Unlike Eq.~\eqref{eq:intra}, neither progress term is divided by a set size, because a teleport moves one operand of one gate and front-layer gates are qubit-disjoint, so exactly one front-layer term ever changes. The capacity term prices the immediate landing core, not the core holding the other operand.

The candidates generated for every gate of $F_{\mathrm{inter}}$ are pooled into a single ranking, so one arg\,min chooses which remote gate to advance as well as how to advance it. Equation~\eqref{eq:dEtilde} is an approximation adopted for efficiency in two respects: it sums only over gates involving the teleported qubit, ignoring extended-set gates the move affects through its evictions, and it scores the layout after the teleport rather than the intermediate staging and eviction layouts.

\subsection{Depth-Ordered Extended Set}
\label{sec:bfsext}

This section constructs the inter-core extended set $E$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dEtilde}. The set is a breadth-first expansion of the remaining DAG. Gates in BFS layer $k$ receive $\mathrm{dep}(g)=k$; within a layer, gates sharing a qubit with a front-layer gate are emitted first. Construction stops after $L$ gates, so every retained gate is no deeper than any omitted gate. Maintained remaining in-degrees give $O(L)$ construction time per update.

The intra-core sets $E_c$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:intra} are breadth-first for the same reason: $E_c$ is not built separately but selected from $E$, as the gates whose two operands currently reside in $c$, keeping the depth $E$ has already assigned them. One construction therefore serves both scorers, and $\mathrm{dep}(g)$ means the same thing in each. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} measures what the BFS order is worth against a topological one.

\subsection{Capacity Conditions and Recovery}
\label{sec:relief}
\label{sec:recovery}

The ordinary score ranks candidate actions but does not ensure that a legal action always exists. \TeleSABRE{} reports the same failure mode and leaves a \SABRE{}-style safety valve for it to future work~\cite{russo2025telesabre}. We therefore impose a hard capacity floor and use a recovery transaction when ordinary routing stalls. Let $f_c$ be the number of free slots in core $c$, and let  $F_{\mathrm{free}}=\sum_{c=1}^{K} f_c=P-n$. 
 Local SWAPs preserve every per-core occupancy count. 
 
 % Since an arrival consumes one free slot, the destination retains at least one afterward.

Before routing, we require 
\begin{align}
F_{\mathrm{free}}&\geq 2K+1, \label{eq:feasible}\\
f_c&\geq 2 \quad\text{for every core }c.\label{eq:invariant}
\end{align}
Throughout routing we further require
\begin{equation}
f_c\geq 2 \quad\text{before every teleport into }c.
\label{eq:teleportge2}
\end{equation} 
Equation~\eqref{eq:teleportge2} is broader than the legality floor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:legality}, which governs candidate generation in ordinary routing alone: it is required of every teleport the router emits, including those inside the recovery transaction below. The two free slots form a reserve: one allows an occupied port to be evacuated, and the other allows an arriving state to be accepted. Ordinary routing need not preserve this full reserve, but it preserves at least one free slot per core; the online appendices measure some core below the reserve at $24.6\%$ of action boundaries on the 64-qubit suite. 

\begin{lemma}[No full core]
\label{lem:nofull}
If Eqs.~\eqref{eq:invariant} and \eqref{eq:teleportge2} hold, then every state reached by ordinary routing or rollback satisfies $f_c\geq 1$ for every core $c$. 
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
%Induct over the states the router reaches. Local SWAPs, port evictions among them, leave every per-core count unchanged. An ordinary teleport decreases only its landing core's count, which Eq.~\eqref{eq:legality} puts at two or more before the move, and increases its source's. A rollback restores a state the router has already reached, for which the claim holds by the induction hypothesis; it is therefore the only step that needs the checkpoint to be a past state rather than an arbitrary one. Only $f_c\ge1$ is used; Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant} supplies it with a slot to spare.

An intra-core SWAP does not change any $f_c$. A teleport increases the source-core count by one and decreases the destination-core count by one; the latter remains at least one by Eq.~\eqref{eq:teleportge2}. A rollback restores a previously reached state, so the result follows by induction. 
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Legal candidates commit] \label{lem:commit} 
Suppose Eqs.~\eqref{eq:invariant} and \eqref{eq:teleportge2} hold. If every intra-core coupling graph is connected, then every teleport obeying Eq.~\eqref{eq:teleportge2} can be completed atomically. 
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof} Consider an admitted teleport from core $c_s$ through port $\pi_s$ to core $c_d$ through port $\pi_d$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull}, $c_s$ contains at least one free slot. Choose any neighbour $n_s$ of $\pi_s$. Since the coupling graph of core $c_s$ is connected, its edge transpositions generate the full symmetric group on its vertex set. Hence intra-core SWAPs can simultaneously place the source qubit at $n_s$ and a free slot at $\pi_s$. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:teleportge2}, $c_d$ contains at least two free slots. Connectivity similarly allows one of them to be placed at $\pi_d$, leaving at least one other free slot in $c_d$. The teleport consumes the free slot at $\pi_d$, so $c_d$ remains non-full. Thus source staging, port evacuation, and the teleport are all executable, and the atomic macro-action commits. 
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Recovery transaction}
Recovery uses two cores, chosen for different reasons. A \emph{donor} is any core with a free slot to spare, $f_{c_d}\ge3$; Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible} guarantees a donor exists. The \emph{meeting core} is where the blocked gate will execute. A \emph{relay} advances one vacancy one hop along a shortest core-graph path from the donor \emph{nearest} the core in deficit, by teleporting a \emph{filler}---any qubit other than the two operands of the blocked gate, which the transaction holds fixed---the opposite way.

%When ordinary routing stalls, \textsc{Recover} first restores Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant}. A core with $f_c\geq3$ is a \emph{donor}; Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible} guarantees that one exists. To increase the free capacity of a deficient core, recovery chooses a nearest donor and relays one vacancy along a shortest core-graph path by teleporting filler qubits in the opposite direction. The two operands of the blocked gate are never used as fillers. Choosing a nearest donor ensures that every proper intermediate core has at most two free slots. Since every core has at least five physical sites, such an intermediate core contains at least three logical qubits and hence at least one filler other than the two protected operands. Each relay reduces 
%\[ \delta=\sum_c\max\{0,2-f_c\} \] 
%by one without creating a new deficit, so at most $K$ relays restore Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant}. Recovery then chooses one operand core as the meeting core, relays the globally surplus vacancy to it if necessary, and moves the other operand there along a shortest core path. An intermediate core temporarily retains at least one free slot while holding the moving operand and returns to the two-slot reserve when the operand leaves. The meeting core retains at least two free slots after the arrival. Intra-core SWAPs then make the operands adjacent, and the blocked front-layer gate is executed. A third core with at least four free slots may be used as an optional meeting core, but completion does not depend on this optimisation. 

Algorithm~\ref{alg:recover} states the transaction. For simplicity, the meeting core is selected from the two operand cores, so only one operand moves, and line~\ref{ln:top} tops $m$ up to three free slots so that it is back at the reserve once the arrival lands. Theorem~\ref{thm:completion} below shows that these choices always succeed; it does not claim they are cheap. The nearest donor, the operand meeting core, and the shortest-path walks are fixed to make completion provable, and a transaction spends EPR like any other action.

%\begin{proof} 
%The total free capacity is invariant. By Lemma~\ref{lem:no-full-core}, ordinary routing never creates a full core. On entry to recovery, vacancy relays reduce $\delta$ to zero in finitely many steps. Equation \eqref{eq:global-capacity} then supplies one surplus vacancy, which is relayed to an operand meeting core. The other operand can consequently be moved there, the blocked gate executed, and the two-slot reserve retained. Thus every recovery transaction strictly decreases the number $|W|$ of unexecuted gates. Between two decreases of $|W|$, at most $L_{\mathrm{deadlock}}$ stalled ordinary iterations occur. Hence, whenever $W\neq\varnothing$, $|W|$ decreases after finitely many iterations. Since $|W|$ is initially finite and nonnegative, it eventually reaches zero. 
%\end{proof} 

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Recover}$(W,\ell)$: the guaranteed transaction of Section~\ref{sec:recovery}, which retires one blocked inter-core gate and returns with Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant} restored.}
\label{alg:recover}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE record the entry state; on any failed precondition below, restore it and abort
\STATE pick a blocked inter-core front gate $g=(q_1,q_2)$, in cores $a$, $b$
\WHILE{some core $c_t$ has $f_{c_t}<2$}\label{ln:res1}
  \STATE walk a vacancy to $c_t$ from its nearest donor \COMMENT{re-establish Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant}}
\ENDWHILE
\STATE choose $m\in\{a,b\}$ with smaller relay shortfall \label{ln:meet}
\STATE relay vacancies until $f_m\ge3$\label{ln:top}
\STATE walk the other operand to $m$ along a shortest core path
\STATE SWAP within $m$ until $\ell(q_1),\ell(q_2)$ are adjacent; execute $g$
\RETURN the updated $W$, with $g$ retired, and $\ell$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Termination with success]
\label{thm:completion}
Suppose the core graph and every intra-core coupling graph are connected, every core has at least four physical sites, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:feasible}--\eqref{eq:invariant} hold initially, and every teleport obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:teleportge2}. Then \dSABRE{} routes every gate of the input DAG and does not abort.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$F_{\mathrm{free}}$ is conserved, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible} supplies a donor in every reachable state, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull} no action creates a full core. Checkpoint--rollback and the invocation of recovery after $L_{\mathrm{deadlock}}$ stalled iterations are steps of \dSABRE{} itself (P4 of Section~\ref{sec:workflow}), not further hypotheses. It therefore suffices to show that one invocation of Algorithm~\ref{alg:recover} always retires its gate and returns in a state again satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant}.

\emph{Relays (line~\ref{ln:res1}).} Let $c_t$ have $f_{c_t}<2$ and take a shortest core path $c_0,\dots,c_h=c_t$ from a donor $c_0$ minimising $\dcore(c_0,c_t)$. Hop $i$ teleports a filler from $c_i$ to $c_{i-1}$, so $c_{i-1}$ loses a slot and $c_i$ gains one; performing the hops in the order $i=1,\dots,h$ keeps every core's count of free slots at or above its entry value except $c_0$, which ends one down but started at three. Each hop is a teleport primitive and commits by Lemma~\ref{lem:commit}, its receiving core meeting Eq.~\eqref{eq:teleportge2} throughout: $f_{c_0}\ge3$ for the donor, and for the rest $f_{c_{i-1}}\ge2$, since Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull} leaves it one free slot and the preceding hop adds another. A filler exists at every hop---the only use of the four-site hypothesis. The blocked gate is inter-core, so its operands sit in different cores and no core holds more than one of them. Were some $c_i$, $1\le i\le h$, to hold no eligible filler, every qubit in it would be a held operand, so it would hold at most one and have $f_{c_i}\ge M-1\ge3$. For an intermediate that makes it a donor with $\dcore(c_i,c_t)<\dcore(c_0,c_t)$, contradicting the choice of $c_0$; for $c_t$ it contradicts $f_{c_t}<2$. Each repetition reduces the total deficit $\delta=\sum_c\max\{0,2-f_c\}$ by one and creates no new deficit, and Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull} makes every summand $0$ or $1$, so $\delta\le K$ and at most $K$ vacancy walks, of at most $D_K$ relays each, restore Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant}.

\emph{Rendezvous (lines~\ref{ln:meet}--\ref{ln:top}).} Take $m=a$. Every core now holds two free slots and $F_{\mathrm{free}}\ge2K+1$, so some core holds three; one vacancy walk from it, by the argument above, leaves $f_a\ge3$ without pushing any core below two. Walk the operand in $b$ to $a$ along a shortest core path. Every core on it is at the reserve before the operand arrives, and $a$ is at three, so each hop meets Eq.~\eqref{eq:teleportge2}. An intermediate core dips to one free slot while it holds the operand---enough to clear its outgoing port---and returns to two when the operand leaves; $b$ ends one up and $a$ one down, at $f_a\ge2$. So Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant} holds when the walk ends: this is why line~\ref{ln:top} tops $a$ up first rather than repairing afterwards. Intra-core connectivity then makes the operands adjacent by SWAPs, which change no count, and the gate executes.

So the transaction strictly decreases $|W|$ and preserves Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant}. Between two decreases of $|W|$, the ordinary loop performs at most $L_{\mathrm{deadlock}}$ stalled iterations before recovery is invoked. Strong induction on $|W|$ proves that the router reaches $|W|=0$ in finitely many steps and never aborts.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Satisfying the hypotheses}
Every device and workload evaluated here satisfies the hypotheses with substantial margin. Connectivity is needed to route at all: without it a core cannot bring its own qubits together, and the circuit splits into independent components. The size condition asks a core to hold four physical sites, the smallest used here holding 16. Equation~\eqref{eq:invariant} need not be arranged by the user at all: Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping} shows how to build a layout that reserves at least two sites in every core, so the condition holds by construction. Equation~\eqref{eq:teleportge2} is enforced rather than assumed---by the legality floor on ordinary teleports, and by the reserve on the ones recovery emits. That leaves Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible} as the only condition on the workload, and it is loose: the device may be filled to $1-(2K+1)/P$, which is $86\%$ for the 16-site cores used here and $91$--$92\%$ for the 25-site ones. The tightest instance in this paper, the 64-qubit suite on the H-grid, has 32 free slots where 13 suffice. A circuit that violates Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible} is rejected before routing rather than part-way through.

\subsection{Initial Layout}
\label{sec:initial_mapping}

Theorem~\ref{thm:completion} starts from a layout that already holds the per-core reserve of Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant}; this section constructs one. The default layout bootstraps from Qiskit's \texttt{SabreLayout} on the full architecture graph, but reserves slots in every core. It withholds the $k$ minimum-degree sites that are farthest, by total shortest-path distance, from the rest of their core---corners on grids and leaves on heavy-hex cores. The value $k\ge2$ is chosen so that the resulting layout satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariant}; the reservation is per core rather than a whole-device quota. Forward, backward, and final-forward passes are run, retaining the better forward route by EPR count. Three \texttt{SabreLayout} seeds are used, so one compilation performs nine routes in all, of which the reported one is the best forward pass.

\subsection{Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

Let $N$ be the number of gates, and let $L$, the intra-core and core-graph degrees, and the number of links joining one adjacent pair of cores be constants. Distance and shortest-path tables are precomputed once per architecture, in $O(KM^2D_M+K^2(D_K+\log K))$ time and space, and are not charged below; $\dphys$ is composed on demand by Eq.~\eqref{eq:dphys} rather than materialised, which avoids the $\Theta(P^2)$ table a whole-chip all-pairs search would build.

Three costs then compose. Maintaining the extended set is $O(L)$ per retired gate, hence $O(N)$ over a route. One routing iteration is dominated by teleport selection: front-layer gates are qubit-disjoint and the core degree and link multiplicity are bounded, so a candidate set has $O(n)$ members, each of which may cost the current implementation an $O(M)$ scan of its landing core, giving $O(M(K+n))$ with the free-slot census. Recovery follows after at most $L_{\mathrm{deadlock}}=4D_K(D_M+1)$ stalled iterations and retires a gate, so ordinary iterations number $O(ND_K(D_M+1))$ and transactions at most $N$, each costing $O(KM(K+D_K))$ to restore the reserve and move an operand. Summing the three and simplifying with $K=O(n)$ and $D_K\le K-1$,
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{total}} = O\!\left( NM\left(D_KD_Mn+K^2\right) \right).
\label{eq:complexity}
\end{equation}
For grid-of-grids architectures $D_K=O(\sqrt{K})$ and $D_M=O(\sqrt{M})$, so $D_KD_M=O(\sqrt{P})$ with $P=KM$, giving $T_{\mathrm{total}}=O(NM(\sqrt{P}n+K^2))$. This is a deliberately loose common bound in two respects: the $K^2$ term is the recovery contribution, charged once per gate even though Section~\ref{sec:tier2} measures recovery firing on a small fraction of them, and the $O(M)$ scans are an artefact of the implementation that maintained free-slot and donor structures would remove. The online appendices derive each term in full and record the structural properties the derivation uses and the measurements that check it against the implementation.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Goal.} We evaluate whether \dSABRE{} reduces EPR consumption while retaining practical compile time and completing instances on finite-capacity multi-core devices.  Our primary comparison is with \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}, because it uses the same physical architectures, counts local SWAPs, and supports one-EPR teledata moves.  It is also the strongest baseline of its family: it reports a 28\% geometric-mean reduction in inter-core state transfers over Hungarian Qubit Assignment~\cite{escofet2023hqa,escofet2024review}, the partitioning-based multi-core mapper it measures itself against.  The comparison with \texttt{pytket-dqc} is reported separately: its EPR unit is the same, but an EPR pair there represents different work and assumes a different machine model.

\textbf{Benchmarks and architectures.} We use 21 MQT Bench circuits~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench}, transpiled to the IBM native basis with Qiskit optimisation level 3; measurements and barriers are removed.  The 25-qubit suite contains AE, QFT, QNN, Random, GHZ, and Graphstate.  The 36-qubit suite contains BV, DJ, QAOA, QPEexact, VQE-SU2, and W-state.  The 64-qubit suite contains AE, QFT, QNN, Random, GHZ, Graphstate, QPEexact, QAOA, and a 16-bit Multiplier.  The scalability study uses two families, QFT and QPEexact, at 100, 200, and 360 logical qubits.

The 25- and 36-qubit suites run on a $2\times2$ B-grid with four $4\times4$ cores (64 physical qubits and four inter-core links).  The 64-qubit suite runs on a $2\times3$ H-grid with six $4\times4$ cores (96 physical qubits and seven links).  These are the \TeleSABRE{} benchmark topologies.  We additionally use four 27-qubit heavy-hex cores connected as a ring or star, and $5\times5$ grid cores for the scalability experiments.  Every core graph and every intra-core coupling graph is connected, as Section~\ref{sec:arch} assumes.  Counting a circuit once per architecture on which it is routed---the 21 suite circuits, the six scalability instances, and the nine 64-qubit circuits on each of the two heavy-hex networks---the evaluation covers 45 instances.

\textbf{Protocol.} Unless stated otherwise, \dSABRE{} uses the fixed parameters in Table~\ref{tab:notation} and its default capacity-reserving initial layout.  \dSABRE{} and \TeleSABRE{} each report the best result from three seeds, following their standard use; \texttt{pytket-dqc} reports the best of five.  We report EPR pairs and local SWAPs.  For \TeleSABRE{}, EPR is the sum of teledata and telegate operations.  A tool is reported as non-convergent on an instance when none of its seeds returns a valid routing.  For \TeleSABRE{} this is its own verdict rather than a limit we impose: every seed exhausts its ten internal attempts at the tool's own deadlock criterion, well inside the wall-clock budget of $300$\,s per seed ($900$\,s on the scalability series).  A failed run is reported explicitly and is excluded from a baseline-only geometric mean; the corresponding \dSABRE{} mean over all completed circuits is also shown.  Per-seed results and commands are provided in the supplementary material.  All measurements ran single-threaded on one machine (Apple M2, 16\,GB, macOS 26.5).  \dSABRE{} is pure Python (3.13, Qiskit 2.3) and \TeleSABRE{} a compiled C++ binary, so the times of Section~\ref{sec:scalability} weigh an interpreted implementation against a native one; \texttt{pytket-dqc} 0.0.1 (pytket 2.16, KaHyPar 1.3.5) is given $900$\,s per distributor per circuit.

\subsection{Robustness, Scale, and Runtime}
\label{sec:scalability}

\textbf{Topology.}\label{sec:heavyhex} The core here is a 27-qubit IBM heavy-hex tile, which shares none of the grid's regularity---degrees over $\{1,2,3\}$, six degree-1 leaves, and diameter 12 against the $4\times4$ grid's 6---and four such tiles are wired as a ring (4 links) and as a star (3 links, with a hub every inter-core route must cross).  Nothing is specialised for either: the corner reservation of Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping} reads vertex degree and remoteness, so it selects leaf qubits where it selected grid corners.  On the ring both routers complete all nine 64-qubit circuits and \dSABRE{} reduces EPRs by 52.0\% against \TeleSABRE{}; on the star the reduction is 41.5\%, with \TeleSABRE{} again failing on Random where \dSABRE{} uses 475 EPR pairs.  The improvement is therefore not specific to regular grid cores or a mesh core graph.

\textbf{Scale.}\label{sec:largecircuits} We scale two circuit families, QFT and QPEexact, while holding core occupancy approximately constant: 100, 200, and 360 logical qubits use 6, 12, and 20 $5\times5$ cores, respectively.  On QFT, \dSABRE{} uses 201 EPR pairs at 100 qubits against \TeleSABRE{}'s 248, and 766 against 1232 at 200 qubits; at 360 qubits \TeleSABRE{} does not converge in any of three runs, while \dSABRE{} completes at 1890.  On QPEexact the margin is larger and the baseline gives out earlier: 246 EPR pairs against 586 at 100 qubits, and no convergence at either 200 or 360 qubits, where \dSABRE{} completes at 896 and 1938.  Three of the five non-convergent instances in this paper are therefore these large routes; the other two are Random at 64 qubits, on the H-grid and on the heavy-hex star.  These cases show that \dSABRE{}'s capacity-aware routing and recovery framework remains operational as the core graph grows.  They are not evidence about \emph{why} the baseline stops: non-convergence there could equally reflect search behaviour, candidate cycling, or implementation limits, and we do not attribute it to capacity handling.

\textbf{Runtime.} On the eight 64-qubit circuits completed by \TeleSABRE{}, the geometric-mean compilation time is 1.9~s for \dSABRE{}, 4.7~s for \TeleSABRE{}, and 107~s for \texttt{pytket-dqc}; on the largest circuit, the $13\,040$-CX Multiplier, the three take $23.7$, $136.3$, and $1393$~s.  The gap is not uniform---on QNN at $8126$ CX the two routers are within $0.2$~s---and the tools use different search procedures, so these are end-to-end practical costs rather than an operation-level comparison.  Timing includes the complete routing or distribution attempt; for \dSABRE{} that is layout generation and all nine routes.  Within one architecture, compile time tracks gate count: a log--log fit of per-seed time against CX count gives an exponent of $0.97$ ($R^2{=}0.95$) over the nine 64-qubit circuits, and $0.92$ and $1.21$ over the 36- and 25-qubit suites.  Each fit holds the device fixed, so it measures growth in circuit size alone; the scalability series, where the machine grows with the circuit, is fitted separately in the online appendices.

\subsection{Ablation and Completion}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:mech} isolates the main design choices on the complete 64-qubit suite.  Replacing the BFS depth-ordered extended set with a topological-order set costs $12.4\%$ in geometric-mean EPR, and removing the capacity score $9.4\%$.  Removing both the score and the legality floor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:legality} aborts QNN and Multiplier and costs $87.8\%$ over the seven circuits every configuration completes, whereas removing the floor alone costs $0.2\%$ and no aborts: the penalised band $f_{c_{\mathrm{next}}}<\tau{=}3$ contains the region $f_{c_{\mathrm{next}}}<\phi_1{=}2$ that the floor forbids, so while the score is present the router seldom proposes an illegal landing. \subsection{Comparison with \texttt{pytket-dqc}}
\label{sec:pytket}

This comparison must be interpreted as a cost-model study, not a like-for-like router comparison.  \texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{andresmartinez2024heterogeneous,pytketdqc2024} uses hypergraph partitioning and gate teleportation, allowing one EPR pair to serve several gates when it remains live.  We run \textsc{CoverEmbeddingSteinerDetached}, its strongest embedding distributor, falling back to \textsc{PartitioningHeterogeneous} where that returns no distribution in budget (at 64 qubits: QNN, Multiplier, Random), and report the best of five seeds.  Its input network is more permissive than the evaluated device: all qubits are available for data and the number of live link qubits per core is unbounded.\label{sec:fair}

\label{sec:pytket_results}Under those assumptions (Table~\ref{tab:fair}), \dSABRE{} uses 56.1\% and 13.8\% fewer EPR pairs on the 25- and 36-qubit suites; at 64 qubits \dSABRE{} uses $15.3\%$ more, 173.3 EPR pairs against \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s 150.2 e-bits.  However, 4/6, 2/6, and at least 6/9 of the respective distributions provably cannot be materialised within the evaluated communication-port capacity---25-qubit AE requires 20 simultaneous link qubits at a core where the device provides two.

\label{sec:lifetime}Entanglement lifetime is a separate assumption.  Re-scoring the same \texttt{pytket-dqc} distributions with a maximum of three gates per EPR pair changes the 64-qubit result to a 59.6\% \dSABRE{} advantage.  The reversal shows where gate teleportation is valuable---circuits with long reusable interaction groups---but also that its reported EPR count depends on maintaining entanglement across more than ten gates per pair.  \dSABRE{}'s teledata count is independent of that lifetime because each pair is consumed immediately.

\paragraph*{Takeaway} The controlled \TeleSABRE{} comparison establishes the main result: capacity-aware teledata routing reduces EPR use and improves completion on the tested finite-capacity architectures.  The \texttt{pytket-dqc} study identifies a complementary limitation---gate-teleportation amortisation can be preferable when long-lived entanglement and sufficient communication memory are available---and should not be read as a direct win or loss without those qualifications.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Distributed compilation combines qubit allocation, communication selection,
routing, and scheduling. This section positions \dSABRE{} at the routing
stage; Section~\ref{sec:intro} gives the broader pipeline.

\textbf{\SABRE{}-style distributed routing.}
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is the closest prior router and the
main experimental baseline. Both methods extend a \SABRE{}-style,
distance-and-lookahead search to multi-core hardware. \TeleSABRE{} selects
from local SWAP and inter-core candidates in one pooled search and uses
shortest paths on a contracted communication graph. \dSABRE{} differs in
three respects. It prices \emph{availability}: a teledata move is scored as a
macro-action including port staging and any required eviction, rather than as
a bare hop an occupied port would delete. It \emph{selects} by a priority
rule: no teleport is scored while any same-core front-layer gate remains,
including those whose operands still need local SWAPs---under
\TeleSABRE{}'s pooled arg\,min, $59.5\%$ of its 64-qubit communication
operations fire while a same-core front gate is still waiting. And it charges
\emph{capacity} at a different point in the search: \TeleSABRE{} folds it into
the shortest path it computes between the two operands, as a penalty on the
weights of a core near its limit, so capacity steers which route is taken,
whereas \dSABRE{} scores it on each candidate's own landing core and,
separately from the score, admits no candidate that breaks
Eq.~\eqref{eq:legality}. \TeleSABRE{} additionally considers telegate
operations, so the two action sets differ as well.

DMapS~\cite{luo2025dmaps} is a concurrent end-to-end approach that combines
KaHyPar-based partitioning with a \SABRE{}-derived router. Its cross-core
primitive is a two-EPR remote SWAP, whereas \dSABRE{} teleports one logical
state into a layout-reserved free slot, and that choice propagates into a
different device model. A remote SWAP exchanges two states across the link
and a one-EPR remote CNOT moves nothing, so both leave each chip's occupancy
unchanged. DMapS therefore never needs a free slot: a chip may stay full for
the whole route, and no capacity condition is maintained during
routing. Its coupling graphs omit communication qubits accordingly, so
every physical qubit carries data, an inter-chip link counts one hop in its qubit
distance matrix exactly as an intra-chip edge does, and no remote operation can
be blocked by an occupied port.
These are modelling choices, not errors, but they leave the two EPR counts
comparable only with the assumptions stated. Over the nine-circuit 64-qubit
suite \dSABRE{} uses $1.43\times$ fewer EPR pairs in geometric mean, DMapS
winning only on GHZ and Graphstate, which KaHyPar partitions almost
perfectly; the per-circuit head-to-head is in the online appendices.

\textbf{Device model.}
The three comparisons differ in what they assume the hardware provides, and
\dSABRE{}'s assumptions are the most conservative. Following \TeleSABRE{}, it
represents communication ports explicitly, treats a port as an ordinary slot
that must be evacuated before it can carry a state, gives an inter-core link
a cost of its own, bounds every core's capacity, and consumes each EPR pair
at once. The other two relax exactly these points: \texttt{pytket-dqc} allows
unboundedly many live link qubits per core and entanglement that survives a
whole group of gates, and DMapS omits the ports and with them the capacity
question. Modelling more of the device is not demanding more of it:
\dSABRE{} asks only for a connected coupling graph, the one to four ports
per core the evaluated devices already have, two spare slots per core and
one more on the device (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:feasible}--\eqref{eq:invariant}),
and no entanglement storage at all, whereas the alternative models assume
communication memory or occupancy-preserving remote operations the
evaluated architecture does not represent. Extra ports, heterogeneous link
costs, or a non-uniform core graph enter through the same distance tables and
the same $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ term, leaving the score and the completion
argument unchanged.

\textbf{Allocation, communication, and scheduling.}
Hypergraph-based methods assign qubits to cores by minimising cut
communication~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024}. They are
complementary to \dSABRE{}: their output can supply an initial allocation,
while \dSABRE{} resolves the local movement and capacity constraints that
arise during execution. Finally,
time-aware DQC compilers schedule entanglement generation and remote
operations subject to link and buffer constraints~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler}.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{Telegate and communication bursts.}
Scoring a telegate is not the hard part. A telegate executes a remote gate without relocating either operand, so it
consumes no destination capacity and enters Eq.~\eqref{eq:dsabre} with
$c_{\mathrm{cap}}=0$, keeping only the port staging $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ its two
link qubits need; its progress term is the front-layer gate it retires outright
rather than a distance reduction. Implemented that way and allowed to compete
with teledata, it costs $93.5\%$ more EPR on the 64-qubit suite, and the best of
seventeen scoring variants we swept is still $1.0\%$ worse than teledata
alone.
Amortisation is the reason, and it is measurable. One pair buys the moved qubit
a residence in its new core, over which it serves $15.75$ of that qubit's
two-qubit gates on average across this suite, with a median of $8$, and $83.4\%$ of moves serve two or
more; an ungrouped telegate serves exactly one, so it can only compete on the
remaining sixth. A telegate therefore pays only
in grouped form, for which the router must expose commutation-derived bursts,
choose the beneficiary core, and hold entanglement for the group's duration.
AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm} and QuComm~\cite{wu2023qucomm} give
pre-processing directions, and lifetime-bounded grouping
in~\cite{bandini2026optimized} shows that a burst is worth taking only when its
entanglement survives the hardware limit. The measurement is in the online
appendices.

\textbf{Scheduling, fidelity, and interconnect co-design.}
This paper minimises EPR consumption rather than makespan or output error, and
the trace it emits is unscheduled. A scheduling pass should batch teleports
whose links, ports, and staging operations do not conflict, and should account
for entanglement-generation rate, storage lifetime, and hardware error rates;
existing time-aware compilers~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler} provide
starting points. The objective should stay explicitly multi-objective, since
EPR count, circuit depth, and fidelity trade off in hardware-dependent ways.
The interconnect is the other free variable. The evaluated devices give each
pair of adjacent cores a single link and each core one to four ports, which
is what the baseline architectures provide rather than a limit of the method:
Section~\ref{sec:tele} already enumerates every link joining two cores as
its own candidate, though on the evaluated devices that loop never has more
than one link to weigh.
Widening it should relieve the availability term $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ first,
since a second link turns an occupied port into an alternative rather than an
obstacle, and should interact with the capacity mechanism, because a core with
more ports has more ways to spend and to recover a free slot. Finding where
the return on added links falls off against its hardware cost is a useful
input to interconnect
co-design~\cite{escofet2023interconnect,wu2023qucomm,liu2025codesign}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented \dSABRE{}, a \SABRE{}-style teledata router for
multi-core distributed quantum computers. Its design principle is to treat an
inter-core move as a complete macro-action rather than a local SWAP with a
larger edge cost, scoring the routing progress it buys---front-layer distance
reduction and DAG-depth-discounted lookahead---against the two costs specific
to a directional teleport, port staging and destination capacity. Capacity
enters twice, and the separation is the point: as a graded score term that
ranks candidates, and as a hard legality floor that, with a bounded recovery
transaction, makes \dSABRE{} provably complete---every gate routed, no abort at
saturation---under checkable pre-routing conditions.

On 21 MQT Bench circuits at 25--64 logical qubits \dSABRE{} reduces
geometric-mean EPR consumption by 49--62\% against \TeleSABRE{} under the
shared teledata cost model, completes all 45 evaluated circuit--architecture
instances against the baseline's 40, and compiles a 360-qubit QFT on twenty
cores in 48\,s. Removing both capacity mechanisms costs $87.8\%$ and leaves two
instances unrouted: the principal advance is not the number of heuristic terms
but the separation of resource-aware ranking from an explicit legality
condition and a completion-preserving recovery. Recovery itself is a
safeguard, not an optimisation---no reported route at 25--64 qubits invokes it,
and from 100 to 360 qubits it retires 6 to 66 gates.

These reductions are in one resource, under one communication model. The
direct comparison is with \TeleSABRE{}, which shares teledata and local-SWAP
accounting; remote-SWAP and telegate-based tools need their own capacity and
entanglement-lifetime assumptions stated before their counts can be read
against ours. The emitted traces are also unscheduled, so a lower EPR count does
not by itself imply a shorter makespan or a higher-fidelity result. Joint
selection between teledata and grouped telegate operations, followed by time-
and fidelity-aware scheduling of the resulting trace, is the natural next
step.
