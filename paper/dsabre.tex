\begin{abstract}
Distributed quantum computers execute a circuit across small quantum processing
units linked by entanglement. Their compiler must minimise consumption of
Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR) pairs while still routing large circuits
quickly and reliably on hardware with finite communication ports and finite
core capacity. We present \dSABRE{}, a distributed extension of the
widely used \SABRE{} routing principle. Rather than treating a teleport as a
high-cost SWAP, \dSABRE{} scores each inter-core teledata move as one
macro-action: the EPR pair it spends, the port staging needed to make it
available, the destination capacity it consumes, and the front-layer progress
it provides with lookahead discounted by depth in the circuit's directed
acyclic graph (DAG). The availability and capacity
terms are the two routing-state constraints that a directional teleport
introduces beyond a
local SWAP. A checked capacity condition and bounded recovery mechanism further
ensure that, under stated preconditions, \dSABRE{} terminates after
routing every gate and does not abort at saturation. Across 21 Munich
Quantum Toolkit (MQT) Bench circuits with 25--64 logical qubits, \dSABRE{}
reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption by 49--62\% relative to \TeleSABRE{}.
It completes all 45 evaluated circuit--architecture instances against
\TeleSABRE{}'s 40, and compiles a 360-qubit QFT on twenty cores
in 48\,s. These results
show that capacity-aware, feasibility-preserving extensions of a simple
\SABRE{}-style objective can provide effective, scalable, and robust distributed
routing. Code is available at \url{https://github.com/ebony72/dsabre}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Distributed quantum computers (DQCs) combine several small quantum processing
units (QPUs) through entanglement links~\cite{cuomo2020towards}. They offer a
practical route to scaling beyond a single processor, but introduce a
compilation problem absent from monolithic devices: a two-qubit gate whose
operands occupy different cores requires inter-core communication. In this
work that communication is supplied by an Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR)
pair. EPR generation is slow, noisy and rate-limited relative to local
operations in current hardware~\cite{stephenson2020high,daiss2021quantum}, so
a router should avoid spending an EPR pair unless the move it enables is
worthwhile.

Distributed compilation has three connected tasks: (i)~allocate logical
qubits to cores, (ii)~choose how to realise a non-local interaction, and
(iii)~route and schedule the resulting local and inter-core operations
~\cite{escofet2024review}. Hypergraph partitioning addresses the first task
by exposing global communication structure~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024}.
An allocation alone, however, does not ensure that the computation can execute
on a device with finite ports, finite core capacity, and non-trivial intra-core
connectivity. During routing, a state may have to be moved to a communication
port, that port may be occupied, and the target core may lack a spare physical
slot. These are dynamic constraints of the evolving layout, not properties of
the initial partition alone.

\textbf{A practical routing objective.}
A useful compiler must be effective, efficient, and robust: it should reduce
the expensive resource, compile instances at useful scale, and return a valid
routed circuit. These
requirements motivate a \SABRE{}-style design. \SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling}
is the layout and routing pass Qiskit~\cite{qiskit2024} selects by default: it
selects a SWAP by the progress it makes on the \emph{front-layer}
two-qubit gates, with bounded lookahead. Its appeal is not global optimality
but a simple, scalable decision rule that performs well in production
compilation flows. The question addressed here is what that rule must price
when the machine is several QPUs rather than one.

The answer is not to replace a SWAP by a teleport on a higher-weight edge. A
SWAP is symmetric and preserves the occupancy of the regions it joins. A
teleport is directional: it is feasible only after its source state reaches a
communication port, and it consumes a free slot in the destination core.
Thus, an inter-core move has three inseparable consequences: it spends an EPR
pair, may require port staging to become available, and reduces the capacity
available for future moves. Treating these effects as one macro-action is the
unifying design principle of \dSABRE{}.

\dSABRE{} is a \SABRE{}-style teledata router that scores a candidate
macro-action---source staging, port eviction if needed, and one
teleport---by the routing progress it buys and the resources it consumes. The
progress term is \SABRE{}'s front-layer distance reduction plus a lookahead
discounted by depth in the circuit's directed acyclic graph (DAG), the
dependency graph defined in Section~\ref{sec:qcircuits}. The two added
costs are an availability term for staging at a port and a graded capacity
term for the destination core: the two routing-state constraints introduced
when a one-way teleport replaces a local SWAP. Latency, coherence, link
contention, and fidelity are teleport-specific too, but lie outside the model
optimised here (Section~\ref{sec:future}). The capacity floor is deliberately
kept out of the score, as a hard admission test applied when candidates are
generated (Eq.~\ref{eq:legality}): a weight can be outvoted by a large
progress term, an admission test cannot. The score therefore ranks feasible
progress-making moves, while the floor and the bounded recovery mechanism keep
finite capacity from becoming an unrecoverable mid-route dead end
(Sections~\ref{sec:principle}--\ref{sec:recovery}).

\textbf{Scope of communication.}
This paper deliberately optimises teledata, which moves one logical state to a
new core and consumes its EPR pair immediately, so that EPR
accounting is independent of entanglement storage lifetime and the
capacity-aware routing problem is isolated. Telegate can be preferable when a group of
compatible remote gates shares an entangled resource, but its benefit depends
on gate grouping, link-buffer occupancy, and the lifetime of retained
entanglement~\cite{eisert2000optimal}: it is a distinct joint
primitive-selection problem, not an omitted zero-cost candidate. \dSABRE{}
does not claim teledata dominates telegate, and identifies burst extraction and
telegate selection as extensions (Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\textbf{Scope of architecture and comparison.}
The algorithm is defined on an arbitrary \emph{connected} coupling graph with
designated ports and queries that graph through distance tables: each core's
intra-core graph is connected and so is the core graph, so every distance the
router queries is finite. Nothing further is assumed---not degree, regularity,
diameter, or the number and placement of ports.

The evaluation exercises that generality over four architectures: the
grid-of-grids devices of \TeleSABRE{} and two networks of IBM heavy-hex cores,
a ring and a star. The reported EPR reductions are established across all
four, which differ in core degree, diameter, and port placement. Likewise, EPR counts are directly
comparable only when methods use
the same communication primitive, architecture, and cost accounting.
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is the primary head-to-head baseline
because it shares the teledata setting and local-SWAP accounting. Comparisons
with DMapS~\cite{luo2025dmaps} and \texttt{pytket-dqc} are controlled
cross-model studies that state the remote-SWAP or telegate assumptions,
communication capacities, and entanglement-lifetime conditions needed to
interpret their EPR counts.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A unified capacity-aware teledata score.} We formulate every
inter-core move as a macro-action and extend \SABRE{}'s distance-progress
objective with the two routing-state costs that distinguish teleportation
from a
SWAP: port availability and the destination capacity it consumes. The score
uses front-layer distance reduction plus DAG-depth-discounted lookahead, and
exhausts executable local front-layer work before considering an
EPR-consuming action.

\item \textbf{Feasibility-aware routing with completion conditions.} We give
pre-routing capacity conditions and a recovery
transaction (Algorithm~\ref{alg:recover}) for the saturated
states in which the ordinary loop has no legal move that makes progress.
Under the stated conditions \dSABRE{} routes every gate and does not
abort; this is a completion guarantee, not a claim of EPR optimality.
The measurements locate where it earns its place: no reported route among
the 21 circuits at 25--64 qubits invokes recovery at all, whereas at scale it
retires 6, 10, and 66 gates over the reported 100-, 200-, and 360-qubit
routes.

\item \textbf{Evidence of practical effectiveness and efficiency.} Across 21
Munich Quantum Toolkit (MQT) Bench circuits with 25--64 logical qubits,
\dSABRE{} reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption by 49--62\% relative to
\TeleSABRE{}. It completes all 45 evaluated circuit--architecture
instances, against \TeleSABRE{}'s 40. The implementation also compiles a 360-qubit
QFT on twenty cores in $48$\,s, with compile time growing near-linearly in
gate count at fixed architecture (Section~\ref{sec:scalability}). Ablations separate the effects of capacity handling,
lookahead construction, and recovery, while the cross-model experiments
distinguish like-for-like comparisons from results contingent on different EPR
use assumptions.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:background} gives the circuit, teleportation, architecture and
\SABRE{} background; Section~\ref{sec:dsabre} presents \dSABRE{} and its
completion argument; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} evaluates it; and
Sections~\ref{sec:related}--\ref{sec:future} discuss related work and
extensions.  Supplementary material at the URL above carries the full
completion proof, the complexity validation, per-circuit and per-seed counts,
benchmark provenance, the parameter and cost-model sweeps, and the capacity,
lifetime and action-level audits behind the cross-model comparisons.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Circuits and routing}
\label{sec:qcircuits}

A quantum circuit is represented as a directed acyclic graph (DAG): each
node is a gate and each edge records a dependency induced by a shared logical
qubit. The \emph{front layer} is the set of dependency-free gates and can be
executed in parallel when the hardware permits. This paper considers
single-qubit (1Q) gates and two-qubit (2Q) gates, in particular CX gates.
Together, CX and arbitrary 1Q unitaries are \emph{exactly} universal:
they decompose any $n$-qubit unitary~\cite{barenco1995elementary}, whereas a
finite set such as $\{\mathrm{CX},H,T\}$ is only \emph{approximately} so.
Hardware provides a finite set: the benchmark circuits of
Section~\ref{sec:setup} are transpiled into IBM's
$\{\mathrm{CX},R_z,\sqrt{X},X\}$.

Let a circuit contain $n$ logical qubits and let the target architecture have
$P\geq n$ physical qubits. An initial layout is an injective mapping from
logical to physical qubits. A 2Q gate is executable only when its operands
are adjacent in the coupling graph. Routing therefore inserts local SWAPs and,
in a distributed machine, inter-core communication operations; each local SWAP
costs $c_{\mathrm{swap}}$ and each inter-core teleport $c_{\mathrm{tele}}$. A SWAP
exchanges the states of adjacent qubits and decomposes into three CX gates.
Figure~\ref{fig:dag-example} illustrates the DAG and front-layer notion used
by the router; \emph{circuit depth} is the length of its longest path.

\subsection{Teleportation}
\label{sec:teleportation}

Quantum teleportation transfers a qubit state between two locations using a
pre-shared Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR) pair and classical communication;
the state is removed from the sender and reconstructed at the receiver
~\cite{bennett1993teleporting}. In this paper, one consumed EPR pair is one
unit of inter-core communication cost, and ``EPR'' as a unit (e.g.\ ``$10$
EPRs'') denotes EPR-pair count.

Two communication primitives are relevant. \emph{Teledata} moves a logical
qubit to another core, after which subsequent gates may execute locally.
\emph{Telegate} implements a non-local 2Q gate without relocating either
logical qubit~\cite{eisert2000optimal}. Teledata can amortise its cost over a
qubit's subsequent residence in the destination core. Telegate can amortise
one entangled state over a compatible group of remote gates, but this requires
an available link qubit and entanglement that survives for the group duration;
Section~\ref{sec:pytket} measures what those two assumptions are worth.

\subsection{Distributed architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

We model the distributed quantum computer as a weighted coupling graph
$G_\arch=(V,E_\arch,w)$. Its physical-qubit vertices are partitioned into $K$ cores,
$V=V_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_K$. Intra-core edges $E_{\mathrm{intra}}$ model
local connectivity and have unit weight. Inter-core edges
$E_{\mathrm{inter}}$ join designated communication ports and have weight
$w_{\mathrm{link}}=10$, the cost of a teleport relative to a unit intra-core
hop. A communication port is an ordinary physical slot that also
terminates an inter-core link; it may hold a data qubit when it is not used
for communication. We assume throughout that $G_\arch$ is \emph{connected},
and in the stronger form the router actually requires: the subgraph induced
by each $V_i$ is connected, and so is the core graph on $\{1,\dots,K\}$
induced by $E_{\mathrm{inter}}$. Every distance below is then finite.

\dSABRE{} uses three distance tables: physical distance $\dphys$,
intra-core distance $\dintra$, and core-graph distance $\dcore$. It therefore
applies to arbitrary connected intra-core graphs and inter-core topologies. Our
experiments instantiate the grid-of-grids architectures of
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:arch}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{fig/architectures}}
\caption{Distributed architectures used in the experiments. Grey vertices are

Physical distances need not be stored in a dense all-pairs table: every
inter-core path exits and enters through ports, so with $\Pi_i$ the ports of
core $i$, for $p\in V_i$ and $q\in V_j$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\dphys(p,q)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi_i,\,\pi'\in\Pi_j}\big[
  &\dintra(p,\pi)+\dport(\pi,\pi')\\
  &+\dintra(\pi',q)\big].
\end{split}
\label{eq:dphys}
\end{equation}
The implementation memoises these queries.

\subsection{\SABRE{}-style routing}
\label{sec:routing}

\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} is a heuristic router that repeatedly executes
ready gates and inserts a candidate SWAP when a front-layer 2Q gate is not
adjacent. Its score favours a SWAP that reduces the distance of
front-layer gates while also considering a bounded lookahead set. We use the
\LightSABRE{} form~\cite{zou2024lightsabrelightweightenhancedsabre}:
\begin{equation}
H = \frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{g\in F}\Delta(g)
+ w_e\,\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{g\in E}\Delta(g),
\label{eq:sabre}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the front layer, $E$ is the extended set, $w_e$ weights the
lookahead term against the front-layer term, and
$\Delta(g)=d^g_{\mathrm{new}}-d^g_{\mathrm{old}}$ is the distance change for
gate $g$. A negative value denotes progress. \dSABRE{} retains this
front-layer-plus-lookahead principle, but adapts the candidate action and
score to account for port staging and finite capacity in the destination core.
Building on a heuristic that production toolchains already run bounds the
compile time and keeps the output executable: the router carries the layout
with it and admits only moves that layout allows. What remains open is what
the \SABRE{} objective must become when one chip becomes several.

\section{dSABRE}
\label{sec:dsabre}

\dSABRE{} is a \SABRE{}-style router for multi-core processors. It uses teledata alone: one teleport consumes one EPR pair and moves one logical qubit across one adjacent-core link. Thus, unlike an amortised telegate, no entanglement lifetime or live EPR buffer is modelled.

The routing state is a layout $\ell$ and inverse $\ell^{-1}$, a DAG $W$ of unexecuted gates, its front layer $F$ of gates with no unexecuted predecessors, and a bounded extended set $E$ beyond $F$. A front-layer 2Q gate is \emph{intra-core} when both operands are in one core and \emph{inter-core} otherwise. Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation.

\subsection{Routing Policy, Score, and Legality}
\label{sec:principle}

\dSABRE{} has two actions: an intra-core SWAP and an inter-core teleport. It first resolves non-adjacent intra-core front gates by local SWAPs. Only when none remain does it score teleports for inter-core front gates. This priority rule avoids imposing an artificial exchange rate between the local and global scorers.

A teleport may require port eviction and staging SWAPs, and consumes one free slot in its landing core. We treat these operations and the teleport as one \emph{macro-action}: a SWAP is a single primitive, while a teleport is a short sequence scored and applied as one unit. \emph{Atomic} below means that such a sequence either completes or leaves the state untouched. Recovery (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}) has the same property, but is the fallback for when neither ordinary action makes progress rather than a third ordinary action. If $\Delta\Phi(a)$ is the change action $a$ induces in a front-layer-plus-lookahead routing potential---negative when $a$ leaves the waiting gates closer to executable---the conceptual objective is
\begin{equation}
s(a)=\underbrace{\mathrm{cost}(a)}_{\text{resource spent}}
+\underbrace{\mathrm{pen}(a)}_{\text{capacity consumed}}
+\underbrace{\Delta\Phi(a)}_{\text{progress bought}}.
\label{eq:principle}
\end{equation}
All compared teleport candidates consume one EPR pair, so this constant is omitted from their ranking.

\paragraph*{Legality rule}
The capacity penalty ranks feasible moves; it does not determine feasibility. Feasibility is enforced by a separate \emph{legality floor}: an ordinary teleport is generated only if its landing core $c_{\mathrm{next}}$ has
\begin{equation}
f_{c_{\mathrm{next}}}\ge\phi_1=2,
\label{eq:legality}
\end{equation}
the free slots being counted before the move. A teleport consumes one slot of its landing core, so after the arrival that core still retains at least $\phi_1-1=1$ free slot, and local SWAPs preserve every core's occupancy. Hence ordinary routing never creates a full core, whose occupied communication port could otherwise prevent a later departure. Equation~\ref{eq:legality} is used in candidate generation and in the recovery proof.

\subsection{Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarises the routing loop and Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsabre} states it in full.  The router retains a \emph{progress checkpoint}---the layout and its inverse, the remaining DAG $W$, the \emph{trace} of SWAPs, teleports and executed gates emitted so far, and the counters that summarise them---as they stood immediately after the last decrease in $|W|$, and initially the input layout, the full DAG and an empty trace.  Restoring it rewinds the emitted circuit as well as the layout, so what is emitted always replays against the state it is restored to.  Each iteration is:

\textbf{P1: Execute.} Repeatedly execute front-layer 1Q gates and adjacent intra-core 2Q gates. Stop if $W$ is empty.

\textbf{P2: Classify.} Partition the remaining front layer into non-adjacent intra-core gates $F_{\mathrm{intra}}$ and inter-core gates $F_{\mathrm{inter}}$.

\textbf{P3: Route.} If $F_{\mathrm{intra}}\neq\emptyset$, apply the lowest-score local SWAP. Otherwise enumerate legal teleport macro-actions for $F_{\mathrm{inter}}$ and try them in score order. Each attempt is atomic: a failed eviction, staging sequence, or teleport restores the pre-attempt state. If no candidate is legal or none commits, restore the progress checkpoint and invoke recovery; by Lemma~\ref{lem:commit} the second case cannot arise.

\textbf{P4: Checkpoint or recover.} A decrease in $|W|$ replaces the checkpoint and resets the stalled counter. Otherwise increment it; after $L_{\mathrm{deadlock}}$ stalled iterations, restore the checkpoint and invoke recovery. Recovery is atomic: it either retires its target gate and restores the reserve, or restores its entry state---a branch Theorem~\ref{thm:completion} rules out, leaving it an assertion on the hypotheses rather than an expected outcome.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\dSABRE{} \texttt{route}$(C,\ell_0)$.  An empty legal candidate set goes straight to recovery rather than waiting out the stall window, since no local SWAP can create headroom in a neighbouring core.  \textsc{Recover} is the transaction of Section~\ref{sec:recovery}.}
\label{alg:dsabre}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $W\leftarrow$ working DAG of $C$; $\ell\leftarrow\ell_0$; initialise $\ell^{-1}$
\STATE verify the preconditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:completion}; initialise a checkpoint
\WHILE{$W\neq\emptyset$}
  \STATE Drain executable 1Q gates and adjacent intra-core front-layer 2Q gates
  \IF{$W=\emptyset$} \STATE \textbf{break} \ENDIF
  \STATE Form $F$, partition it into $F_{\rm intra}$ and $F_{\rm inter}$, and build $E$ if stale
  \IF{$F_{\rm intra}\neq\emptyset$}
    \FOR{each active core $c$ with $F_c\neq\emptyset$}
      \STATE Apply the minimum-score intra-core SWAP of core $c$ using Eq.~\ref{eq:intra}
    \ENDFOR
  \ELSE
    \STATE Enumerate teleport macro-actions for $F_{\rm inter}$ whose landing core $c'$ has $f_{c'}\ge\phi_1$
    \STATE Try candidates in score order; each attempt either commits atomically or restores its entry state
    \IF{the candidate set is empty, or no candidate commits}
      \STATE restore the checkpoint and invoke \textsc{Recover}$(W,\ell)$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$|W|$ decreased}
    \STATE replace the checkpoint and reset the stalled counter
  \ELSIF{the stalled counter reaches $L_{\rm deadlock}$}
    \STATE restore the checkpoint and invoke \textsc{Recover}$(W,\ell)$
  \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN routed circuit, final layout, and metrics
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Intra-Core SWAP Heuristic}
\label{sec:intra}

For active core $c$, let $F_c$ be its non-adjacent intra-core front gates and let $E_c\subseteq E$ contain the extended-set gates whose operands currently both reside in $c$. The global construction of $E$ supplies one consistent depth $\mathrm{dep}(g)$ for both scorers. For a candidate SWAP, define
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\Delta}_E^c=
\sum_{g\in E_c}\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g)}
\left(d^g_{\mathrm{new}}-d^g_{\mathrm{old}}\right),
\label{Delta_E_Tilde}
\end{equation}
where $0<\gamma\le1$ and both distances are $\dintra$. The candidates are the physical edges $(u,v)$ within $c$ incident to an operand of a gate in $F_c$; the selected SWAP minimises
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{intra}}=
\frac{\Delta_F^c}{|F_c|}+w_e\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}_E^c}{|E_c|}.
\label{eq:intra}
\end{equation}
When $E_c=\emptyset$ the lookahead term is zero. This is the \SABRE{} objective of Eq.~\ref{eq:sabre} with one change: \SABRE{} weights every gate of its extended set equally, having filled that set in topological order, which fixes no notion of how far ahead a gate lies. Here $\mathrm{dep}(g)$ is the layer of $g$ in a \emph{breadth-first} (BFS) expansion of the remaining DAG---one dependency beyond the front layer is $\mathrm{dep}=1$, one beyond that $\mathrm{dep}=2$ (Section~\ref{sec:bfsext})---so $\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g)}$ discounts by exactly that. Candidate evaluations are independent across active cores.

\subsection{Inter-Core Teleportation Scoring}
\label{sec:tele}

For an inter-core front gate $g=(q_1,q_2)$, both operands are considered as sources. For source $q_1$ in $c_{\mathrm{src}}$, the router enumerates each adjacent core $c_{\mathrm{next}}$ and each link $(\pi_s,\pi_d)$ joining them, retaining only candidates that satisfy Eq.~\ref{eq:legality}. Let $p_1=\ell(q_1)$ and let $n_s$ be a neighbour of $\pi_s$ minimising $\dintra(p_1,n_s)$. The macro-action clears both ports, stages $q_1$ to $n_s$, and teleports it across $(\pi_s,\pi_d)$. Each port has a free slot to be cleared into: the source core has one because ordinary routing leaves no core full, as Section~\ref{sec:recovery} proves, and Eq.~\ref{eq:legality} gives the landing core two, of which one absorbs the port's occupant and the arrival still leaves one behind.

The score is
\begin{equation}
s=d_{\mathrm{prep}}+c_{\mathrm{cap}}+\Delta_F+w_e\widetilde{\Delta}_E,
\label{eq:dsabre}
\end{equation}
where lower is better and
\begin{align}
d_{\mathrm{prep}}={}&\dintra(p_1,n_s)+d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi_s)+d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi_d), \\
c_{\mathrm{cap}}={}&c_{\mathrm{pen}}\max\{0,\tau-f_{c_{\mathrm{next}}}\}, \\
\Delta_F={}&d^g_{\mathrm{new}}-d^g_{\mathrm{old}}, \\
\widetilde{\Delta}_E={}&\sum_{h\in E(q_1)}\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(h)}
\left(d^h_{\mathrm{new}}-d^h_{\mathrm{old}}\right).
\label{eq:dEtilde}
\end{align}
Here $d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi)$ is the number of SWAPs that clear port $\pi$, and is zero when $\pi$ is empty. $E(q_1)\subseteq E$ is the set of extended-set gates having $q_1$ as an operand, and for a gate $h$ with operands $q_1$ and $q'$, $d^h_{\mathrm{old}}=\dphys(p_1,\ell(q'))$ and $d^h_{\mathrm{new}}=\dphys(\pi_d,\ell(q'))$: the teleported qubit is priced at the destination port it lands on. $\Delta_F$ is measured with the same $\dphys$, so the two progress terms are on one scale; and unlike Eq.~\ref{eq:intra}, neither is divided by a set size, because a teleport moves one operand of one gate and front-layer gates are qubit-disjoint, so exactly one front-layer term ever changes. The candidates generated for every gate of $F_{\mathrm{inter}}$ are pooled into a single ranking, so one arg\,min chooses which remote gate to advance as well as how to advance it. The preparation term prices availability: an occupied port is costly, not silently excluded. The capacity term prices the immediate landing core, not the core holding the other operand.
All distance terms use the weighted architecture graph $G_\arch$---an intra-core edge costs one and an inter-core link $10$---so $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$, $\Delta_F$, and $\widetilde{\Delta}_E$ share a cost scale.
Equation~\ref{eq:dEtilde} is an approximation adopted for efficiency in two respects: it sums only over gates involving the teleported qubit, ignoring extended-set gates the move affects through its evictions, and it scores the layout after the teleport rather than the intermediate staging and eviction layouts.

\subsection{Depth-Ordered Extended Set}
\label{sec:bfsext}

This section constructs the inter-core extended set $E$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:dEtilde}. The set is a breadth-first expansion of the remaining DAG. Gates in BFS layer $k$ receive $\mathrm{dep}(g)=k$; within a layer, gates sharing a qubit with a front-layer gate are emitted first. Construction stops after $L$ gates, so every retained gate is no deeper than any omitted gate. Maintained remaining in-degrees give $O(L)$ construction time per update.

The intra-core sets $E_c$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:intra} are breadth-first for the same reason: they are read off this one set as the gates of $E$ whose two operands currently reside in $c$, carrying the depths $E$ already assigned, so one construction serves both scorers and $\mathrm{dep}(g)$ means the same thing in each. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} measures what the BFS order is worth against a topological one.

\subsection{Capacity Conditions and Recovery}
\label{sec:relief}
\label{sec:recovery}

The ordinary score is a preference rule, not a proof that an action will always exist. Recovery handles the exceptional state in which ordinary routing stalls, by a \emph{guaranteed transaction} that provably retires the blocked gate.

A free slot is a physical site holding no logical qubit; port eviction moves a port's occupant to a free site in the same core, so any non-full core can clear an occupied port.

Let $f_c$ be the number of free slots in core $c$ and let $F_{\mathrm{free}}=\sum_c f_c=P-n$ be the total number of free slots. Recovery keeps a \emph{reserve} of two free slots per core---one to evacuate a port with, one to receive an arriving state---so before routing we require
\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{free}}\ge 2K+1,
\label{eq:feasible}
\end{equation}
and initially
\begin{equation}
f_c\ge 2\qquad\text{for every core }c.
\label{eq:invariant}
\end{equation}
The arguments below need only that the reserve is at least two; a larger one costs a proportionally larger Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible}. The total number of free slots is conserved. The reserve is required only on entry to and exit from recovery; ordinary routing need not preserve it, and measurements in the online appendices show that on the 64-qubit suite some core sits below it at $24.6\%$ of action boundaries.

Two consequences of Eq.~\ref{eq:legality} are used repeatedly and are recorded here.

\begin{lemma}[Ordinary routing creates no full core]
\label{lem:nofull}
Assume Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} holds of the initial layout. Then after every ordinary action---and after every rollback---every core has at least one free slot.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Induct over the states the router reaches. Local SWAPs, port evictions among them, leave every per-core count unchanged. An ordinary teleport decreases only its landing core's count, which Eq.~\ref{eq:legality} puts at two or more before the move, and increases its source's. A rollback restores a state the router has already reached, for which the claim holds by the induction hypothesis; it is therefore the only step that needs the checkpoint to be a past state rather than an arbitrary one. Only $f_c\ge1$ is used; Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} supplies it with a slot to spare.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Legal candidates commit]
\label{lem:commit}
Assume Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull}, that every core's coupling graph is connected, and that the staging slot $n_s$ is chosen among the neighbours of $\pi_s$ lying in the source qubit's own component of that graph with $\pi_s$ deleted. Then every teleport macro-action admitted by Eq.~\ref{eq:legality} completes: no candidate that is generated is rejected at execution time.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A macro-action fails only if it cannot evacuate its source or destination port, cannot stage its qubit, or cannot land the arriving state. By Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull} both ports lie in cores holding a free slot, and intra-core connectivity moves that vacancy along a path to the port, so both evictions succeed. The staging SWAPs must then avoid $\pi_s$: a path through it would push a bystander onto the port on its last SWAP and undo the eviction. Deleting $\pi_s$ can disconnect the core---on the heavy-hex tiles used in Section~\ref{sec:heavyhex} both ports are cut vertices, though on grid cores none is---so the nearest port neighbour is not always reachable. Some neighbour always is: every component of the core minus $\pi_s$ is adjacent to $\pi_s$, because the core is connected, so the source qubit's own component contains one, and the lemma's hypothesis selects it. Staging SWAPs preserve occupancy. The arrival then consumes one of the at least two free slots Eq.~\ref{eq:legality} requires of the landing core.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Recovery transaction}
Recovery uses two distinct cores, and they are chosen for different reasons. A \emph{donor} is any core with a free slot to spare, $f_{c_d}\ge3$; Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} guarantees one exists, since otherwise every core would hold at most two and $F_{\mathrm{free}}\le2K$. The \emph{meeting core} is where the blocked gate will execute. They coincide only by accident. A \emph{relay} advances one vacancy one hop along a shortest core-graph path from a donor towards a core in deficit, by teleporting a \emph{filler}---any qubit other than the two operands of the blocked gate, which the transaction holds fixed---the opposite way.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:recover} states the transaction; its steps are sequential, and the teleports it emits are not themselves subject to Eq.~\ref{eq:legality}, the reserve playing that role for them. Line~\ref{ln:meet} admits two families of meeting core. If $m\in\{a,b\}$ only one operand moves, and line~\ref{ln:top} tops $m$ up to three free slots so that it is back at the reserve once the arrival lands. If $m$ is a third core both operands arrive, so $m$ is proposed only when it \emph{already} holds four: no relay is requested, and none could be promised, since Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} guarantees one surplus vacancy and not two. The proof below uses only the first family; third-core rendezvous shortens the operand walks when a core happens to have the local slack, and completion never depends on it.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Recover}$(W,\ell)$: the guaranteed transaction of Section~\ref{sec:recovery}, which retires one blocked inter-core gate and returns with Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} restored.}
\label{alg:recover}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE record the entry state; on any failed precondition below, restore it and abort
\STATE pick a blocked inter-core front gate $g=(q_1,q_2)$, in cores $a$ and $b$
\WHILE{some core $c_t$ has $f_{c_t}<2$}\label{ln:res1}
  \STATE relay a vacancy to $c_t$ from a donor \COMMENT{re-establish Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}}
\ENDWHILE
\STATE choose $m$ minimising $\dcore(a,m)+\dcore(b,m)$ plus its relay shortfall, over $m\in\{a,b\}$ and over third cores already holding $f_m\ge4$\label{ln:meet}
\STATE relay vacancies until $f_m\ge3$ \COMMENT{no-op for a third core}\label{ln:top}
\STATE teleport each operand not in $m$ to $m$, hop by hop along a shortest path
\STATE SWAP within $m$ until $\ell(q_1),\ell(q_2)$ are adjacent; execute $g$
\RETURN the updated $W$, with $g$ retired, and $\ell$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Termination with success]
\label{thm:completion}
Suppose the core graph and every intra-core coupling graph are connected, every core has at least five physical qubits, Eqs.~\ref{eq:feasible}--\ref{eq:invariant} hold initially, the staging rule of Lemma~\ref{lem:commit} is used, and checkpoint--rollback is enabled. Then \dSABRE{} routes every gate of the input DAG and does not abort.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$F_{\mathrm{free}}$ is conserved, so Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} supplies a donor in every reachable state, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull} no ordinary action creates a full core. It suffices to show that one invocation of Algorithm~\ref{alg:recover} always retires its gate and returns in a state again satisfying Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}.

\emph{Relays (line~\ref{ln:res1}).} Let $c_t$ have $f_{c_t}<2$ and take a shortest core path $c_0,\dots,c_h=c_t$ from a donor $c_0$. Hop $i$ teleports a filler from $c_i$ to $c_{i-1}$, so $c_{i-1}$ loses a slot and $c_i$ gains one; performing the hops in the order $i=1,\dots,h$ keeps every core's count at or above its entry value except $c_0$, which ends one down but started at three. Each hop is an ordinary teleport primitive and is legal for the reasons of Lemma~\ref{lem:commit}, its receiving core holding a free slot throughout---by Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull} for $c_0$, by the preceding hop for the rest. The filler exists because $c_i$ must hold some qubit other than the two operands the transaction is holding fixed; a core holding nothing else has $M-2\ge3$ free slots, so it is a donor and the walk starts there instead---this is where, and only where, the five-site hypothesis is used. Each repetition reduces the total deficit $\sum_c\max\{0,2-f_c\}$ by one, so finitely many restore Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}.

\emph{Rendezvous (lines~\ref{ln:meet}--\ref{ln:top}).} Take $m=a$. Every core now holds two free slots and $F_{\mathrm{free}}\ge2K+1$, so some core holds three; one relay from it, by the argument above, leaves $f_a\ge3$ without pushing any core below two. Walk the operand in $b$ to $a$ along a shortest core path. An intermediate core dips to one free slot while it holds the operand---enough to clear its outgoing port---and returns to two when the operand leaves; $b$ ends one up and $a$ one down, at $f_a\ge2$. So Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} holds when the walk ends, with both operands in $a$: this is why line~\ref{ln:top} tops $a$ up first rather than repairing afterwards. Intra-core connectivity then makes the operands adjacent by SWAPs, which change no count, and the gate executes.

So the transaction strictly decreases $|W|$ and preserves Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}. Between two decreases of $|W|$, the ordinary loop performs at most $L_{\mathrm{deadlock}}$ stalled iterations before recovery is invoked. Strong induction on $|W|$ proves that the router reaches $|W|=0$ in finitely many steps and never aborts.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Satisfying the hypotheses}
The hypotheses are mild, and every device and workload evaluated in this paper satisfies them with substantial margin. Connectivity is needed to route at all: a core whose coupling graph is disconnected cannot bring two of its own qubits together, and a disconnected core graph can only execute circuits independently within its connected components. The size condition asks a core to hold five physical qubits, the smallest used here holding 16. Checkpoint--rollback is an implementation property, enabled by default. It is not what re-establishes Lemma~\ref{lem:nofull}, which holds throughout ordinary routing anyway: its role is to discard a branch that has stopped making progress and to leave the emitted trace and the restored layout consistent with each other before the deterministic transaction begins. Equation~\ref{eq:invariant} is a property of the initial layout rather than something the user must arrange: the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping} reserves at least two sites in every core and so establishes it by construction. That leaves Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} as the only condition constraining the workload, and it constrains it loosely: the device may be filled to $1-(2K+1)/P$, which is $86\%$ for the 16-site cores used here and $91$--$92\%$ for the 25-site ones. The tightest instance in this paper, the 64-qubit suite on the H-grid, has 32 free slots where 13 suffice. A circuit that violates Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} is rejected before routing rather than part-way through.

The heavy-hex star of Section~\ref{sec:heavyhex} is the tightest evaluated case and clears every hypothesis. Its hub is a cut vertex of the core graph, so every relay between two leaves crosses it---longer walks, but no step made illegal---and two of the hub tile's three ports are cut vertices of its own coupling graph, which is the situation Lemma~\ref{lem:commit} handles and which no grid core presents. Capacity is not close: four 27-site cores hold 64 logical qubits, leaving 44 free slots where nine suffice.

\subsection{Initial Layout}
\label{sec:initial_mapping}

The default layout bootstraps from Qiskit's \texttt{SabreLayout} on the full architecture graph, but reserves slots in every core. It withholds the $k$ minimum-degree sites that are farthest, by total shortest-path distance, from the rest of their core---corners on grids and leaves on heavy-hex cores. The value $k\ge2$ is chosen so that the resulting layout satisfies Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}; the reservation is per core rather than a whole-device quota. Forward, backward, and final-forward passes are run, retaining the better forward route by EPR count. Three \texttt{SabreLayout} seeds are used, so one compilation performs nine routes in all, of which the reported one is the best forward pass.

\subsection{Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

Let $N$ be the number of gates in the circuit. Distance and shortest-path tables are precomputed once per architecture and are not charged below. Here $L$, the intra-core degree, the core-graph degree, and the number of links joining one adjacent pair of cores are constants, so a core has $O(M)$ edges, the core graph has $O(K)$, and there are $O(K)$ communication ports. The last two are what make the teleport candidate count $O(n)$ below. All-pairs breadth-first search inside every core then costs $O(KM^2)$ for $\dintra$, and $O(KM^2D_M)$ to materialise the intra-core paths, whose $KM^2$ entries have $O(D_M)$ sites each; the same search on the core graph costs $O(K^2)$ for $\dcore$ and $O(K^2D_K)$ for the core paths. Dijkstra over the port graph gives the port-level table $\dport$ in $O(K^2\log K)$, and $\dphys$ is then composed on demand by Eq.~\ref{eq:dphys} rather than materialised, which avoids the $\Theta(P^2)$ table a whole-chip all-pairs search would build. The precomputation is therefore
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{pre}}=O\!\left(KM^2D_M+K^2(D_K+\log K)\right),
\label{eq:precompute}
\end{equation}
in $O(KM^2+K^2)$ space.

The extended set is rebuilt only after a gate retires, at the $O(L)$ per update of Section~\ref{sec:bfsext} and hence $O(NL)=O(N)$ over a route. In one local-routing iteration, each active core enumerates $O(M)$ candidate edges---its front gates are qubit-disjoint, so $|F_c|\le M/2$---and scores each in $O(L)$, since a SWAP moves at most two front-layer operands; over all active cores the iteration costs $O(n)$, because $\sum_c|F_c|\le n/2$. One teleport-selection iteration costs $O(M(K+n))$: $O(KM)$ for the free-slot census and $O(nM)$ for the $O(n)$ candidates, each of which scans its landing core for an eviction target. That last scan is an implementation bound, not an inherent one: maintaining per-core free-site and port-occupancy structures would replace it by a lookup. The teleport iteration therefore dominates.

Since recovery follows after at most $L_{\mathrm{deadlock}}=4D_K(D_M+1)$ stalled iterations and retires at least one gate, the number of ordinary iterations is $O(ND_K(D_M+1))$, so
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{ord}}=O\!\left(ND_K(D_M+1)M(K+n)\right).
\label{eq:tord}
\end{equation}
One recovery transaction costs $O(KM(K+D_K))$ time and occurs at most once per retired gate: it relays $O(K)$ vacancies over core paths of at most $D_K$ hops, each hop scanning its core in $O(M)$ for a filler and a free slot, over an $O(KM)$ donor search, giving $T_{\mathrm{rec}}=O(NKM(K+D_K))$. Summing the extended set, $T_{\mathrm{ord}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{rec}}$, and using $D_M,D_K,M\ge1$ and $K=O(n)$, and absorbing $KD_K\le K^2$,
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{total}}=O\!\left(NM\left(D_KD_Mn+K^2\right)\right).
\label{eq:complexity}
\end{equation}
This is a deliberately loose common bound over both regimes; the $K^2$ term is the recovery contribution and is charged once per gate even though Section~\ref{sec:tier2} measures recovery firing on a small fraction of them.
For grid-of-grids architectures, $D_K=O(\sqrt{K})$ and $D_M=O(\sqrt{M})$, so $D_KD_M=O(\sqrt{P})$ with $P=KM$, giving $T_{\mathrm{total}}=O(NM(\sqrt{P}n+K^2))$.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Goal.} We evaluate whether \dSABRE{} reduces EPR consumption while retaining practical compile time and completing instances on finite-capacity multi-core devices.  Our primary comparison is with \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}, because it uses the same physical architectures, counts local SWAPs, and supports one-EPR teledata moves.  It is also the strongest baseline of its family: it reports a 28\% geometric-mean reduction in inter-core state transfers over Hungarian Qubit Assignment~\cite{escofet2023hqa,escofet2024review}, the partitioning-based multi-core mapper it measures itself against.  The comparison with \texttt{pytket-dqc} is reported separately: its EPR unit is the same, but an EPR pair there represents different work and assumes a different machine model.

\textbf{Benchmarks and architectures.} We use 21 MQT Bench circuits~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench}, transpiled to the IBM native basis with Qiskit optimisation level 3; measurements and barriers are removed.  The 25-qubit suite contains AE, QFT, QNN, Random, GHZ, and Graphstate.  The 36-qubit suite contains BV, DJ, QAOA, QPEexact, VQE-SU2, and W-state.  The 64-qubit suite contains AE, QFT, QNN, Random, GHZ, Graphstate, QPEexact, QAOA, and a 16-bit Multiplier.  The scalability study uses two families, QFT and QPEexact, at 100, 200, and 360 logical qubits.

The 25- and 36-qubit suites run on a $2\times2$ B-grid with four $4\times4$ cores (64 physical qubits and four inter-core links).  The 64-qubit suite runs on a $2\times3$ H-grid with six $4\times4$ cores (96 physical qubits and seven links).  These are the \TeleSABRE{} benchmark topologies.  We additionally use four 27-qubit heavy-hex cores connected as a ring or star, and $5\times5$ grid cores for the scalability experiments.  Every core graph and every intra-core coupling graph is connected, as Section~\ref{sec:arch} assumes.  Counting a circuit once per architecture on which it is routed---the 21 suite circuits, the six scalability instances, and the nine 64-qubit circuits on each of the two heavy-hex networks---the evaluation covers 45 instances.

\textbf{Protocol.} Unless stated otherwise, \dSABRE{} uses the fixed parameters in Table~\ref{tab:notation} and its default capacity-reserving initial layout.  \dSABRE{} and \TeleSABRE{} each report the best result from three seeds, following their standard use; \texttt{pytket-dqc} reports the best of five.  We report EPR pairs and local SWAPs.  For \TeleSABRE{}, EPR is the sum of teledata and telegate operations.  A tool is reported as non-convergent on an instance when none of its seeds returns a valid routing.  For \TeleSABRE{} this is its own verdict rather than a limit we impose: every seed exhausts its ten internal attempts at the tool's own deadlock criterion, well inside the wall-clock budget of $300$\,s per seed ($900$\,s on the scalability series).  A failed run is reported explicitly and is excluded from a baseline-only geometric mean; the corresponding \dSABRE{} mean over all completed circuits is also shown.  Per-seed results and commands are provided in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Main Comparison with \TeleSABRE{}}
\label{sec:mainresults}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the main results.  \dSABRE{} lowers geometric-mean EPR consumption by 50.8\%, 62.0\%, and 49.1\% on the 25-, 36-, and 64-qubit suites, respectively, on the circuits completed by both routers.  It also uses fewer geometric-mean local SWAPs in every suite.  At 64 qubits, \TeleSABRE{} fails on Random in all three attempts, whereas \dSABRE{} completes it using 732 EPR pairs.  The result is therefore not only an average EPR reduction: \dSABRE{} also completes one additional circuit under the same architecture and run budget.  Over the whole evaluation, \dSABRE{} completes 45 of the 45 circuit--architecture instances and \TeleSABRE{} 40 of 45, under the run budget of Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\subsection{Robustness, Scale, and Runtime}
\label{sec:scalability}

\textbf{Topology.}\label{sec:heavyhex} The core here is a 27-qubit IBM heavy-hex tile, which shares none of the grid's regularity---degrees over $\{1,2,3\}$, six degree-1 leaves, and diameter 12 against the $4\times4$ grid's 6---and four such tiles are wired as a ring (4 links) and as a star (3 links, with a hub every inter-core route must cross).  Nothing is specialised for either: the corner reservation of Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping} reads vertex degree and remoteness, so it selects leaf qubits where it selected grid corners.  On the ring both routers complete all nine 64-qubit circuits and \dSABRE{} reduces EPRs by 52.0\% against \TeleSABRE{}; on the star the reduction is 41.5\%, with \TeleSABRE{} again failing on Random where \dSABRE{} uses 475 EPR pairs.  The improvement is therefore not specific to regular grid cores or a mesh core graph.

\textbf{Scale.}\label{sec:largecircuits} We scale two circuit families, QFT and QPEexact, while holding core occupancy approximately constant: 100, 200, and 360 logical qubits use 6, 12, and 20 $5\times5$ cores, respectively.  On QFT, \dSABRE{} uses 201 EPR pairs at 100 qubits against \TeleSABRE{}'s 248, and 766 against 1232 at 200 qubits; at 360 qubits \TeleSABRE{} does not converge in any of three runs, while \dSABRE{} completes at 1890.  On QPEexact the margin is larger and the baseline gives out earlier: 246 EPR pairs against 586 at 100 qubits, and no convergence at either 200 or 360 qubits, where \dSABRE{} completes at 896 and 1938.  Three of the five non-convergent instances in this paper are therefore these large routes; the other two are Random at 64 qubits, on the H-grid and on the heavy-hex star.  These cases show that \dSABRE{}'s capacity-aware routing and recovery framework remains operational as the core graph grows.  They are not evidence about \emph{why} the baseline stops: non-convergence there could equally reflect search behaviour, candidate cycling, or implementation limits, and we do not attribute it to capacity handling.

\textbf{Runtime.} On the eight 64-qubit circuits completed by \TeleSABRE{}, the geometric-mean compilation time is 1.9~s for \dSABRE{}, 4.7~s for \TeleSABRE{}, and 107~s for \texttt{pytket-dqc}; on the largest circuit, the $13\,040$-CX Multiplier, the three take $23.7$, $136.3$, and $1393$~s.  The gap is not uniform---on QNN at $8126$ CX the two routers are within $0.2$~s---and the tools use different search procedures, so these are end-to-end practical costs rather than an operation-level comparison.  Timing includes the complete routing or distribution attempt; for \dSABRE{} that is layout generation and all nine routes.  Within one architecture, compile time tracks gate count: a log--log fit of per-seed time against CX count gives an exponent of $0.97$ ($R^2{=}0.95$) over the nine 64-qubit circuits, and $0.92$ and $1.21$ over the 36- and 25-qubit suites.  Over the six scalability instances the fitted exponent is $2.2$ instead, because there the device grows with the circuit---twenty $5{\times}5$ cores at 360 qubits against six at 100---which Eq.~\ref{eq:complexity} charges through its $K^2$ term.  The near-linear reading is therefore a statement about circuit size at fixed hardware, not about scaling the machine.

\subsection{Comparison with \texttt{pytket-dqc}}
\label{sec:pytket}

This comparison must be interpreted as a cost-model study, not a like-for-like router comparison.  \texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{pytketdqc2024} uses hypergraph partitioning and gate teleportation, allowing one EPR pair to serve several gates when it remains live.  We run its strongest default distributor that returns a result and report the best of five seeds.  Its input network is more permissive than the evaluated device: all qubits are available for data and the number of live link qubits per core is unbounded.\label{sec:fair}

\label{sec:pytket_results}Under those assumptions (Table~\ref{tab:fair}), \dSABRE{} uses 56.1\% and 13.8\% fewer EPR pairs on the 25- and 36-qubit suites; at 64 qubits, \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s 150.2 e-bits against \dSABRE{}'s 173.3 EPR pairs is a 15.3\% advantage for \texttt{pytket-dqc}.  However, 4/6, 2/6, and at least 6/9 of the respective distributions provably cannot be materialised within the evaluated communication-port capacity---25-qubit AE requires 20 simultaneous link qubits at a core where the device provides two.

\label{sec:lifetime}Entanglement lifetime is a separate assumption.  Re-scoring the same \texttt{pytket-dqc} distributions with a maximum of three gates per EPR pair changes the 64-qubit result to a 59.6\% \dSABRE{} advantage.  The reversal shows where gate teleportation is valuable---circuits with long reusable interaction groups---but also that its reported EPR count depends on maintaining entanglement across roughly ten gates per pair.  \dSABRE{}'s teledata count is independent of that lifetime because each pair is consumed immediately.

\paragraph*{Takeaway} The controlled \TeleSABRE{} comparison establishes the main result: capacity-aware teledata routing reduces EPR use and improves completion on the tested finite-capacity architectures.  The \texttt{pytket-dqc} study identifies a complementary limitation---gate-teleportation amortisation can be preferable when long-lived entanglement and sufficient communication memory are available---and should not be read as a direct win or loss without those qualifications.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Distributed compilation combines qubit allocation, communication selection,
routing, and scheduling. This section positions \dSABRE{} at the routing
stage; Section~\ref{sec:intro} gives the broader pipeline.

\textbf{\SABRE{}-style distributed routing.}
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is the closest prior router and the
main experimental baseline. Both methods extend a \SABRE{}-style,
distance-and-lookahead search to multi-core hardware. \TeleSABRE{} selects
from local SWAP and inter-core candidates in one pooled search and uses
shortest paths on a contracted communication graph. \dSABRE{} differs in
three respects. It prices \emph{availability}: a teledata move is scored as a
macro-action including port staging and any required eviction, rather than as
a bare hop an occupied port would delete. It \emph{selects} by a priority
rule, draining executable same-core front-layer gates first---under
\TeleSABRE{}'s pooled arg\,min, $59.5\%$ of its 64-qubit communication
operations fire while a same-core front gate is still waiting. And it charges
\emph{capacity} against the landing core's slack, backed by the legality
floor of Eq.~\ref{eq:legality}. The central difference is thus whether port
availability and destination capacity are represented in the candidate action
and its cost. \TeleSABRE{} additionally considers telegate operations.

DMapS~\cite{luo2025dmaps} is a concurrent end-to-end approach that combines
KaHyPar-based partitioning with a \SABRE{}-derived router. Its cross-core
primitive is a two-EPR remote SWAP, whereas \dSABRE{} teleports one logical
state into a layout-reserved free slot, and that choice propagates into a
different device model. A remote SWAP exchanges two states across the link
and a one-EPR remote CNOT moves nothing, so both leave each chip's occupancy
unchanged. DMapS therefore never needs a free slot: a chip may stay full for
the whole route, and no capacity condition is maintained during
routing. Its coupling graphs omit communication qubits accordingly, so
every physical qubit carries data, an inter-chip edge weighs the same as an
intra-chip one, and no remote operation can be blocked by an occupied port.
These are modelling choices, not errors, but they leave the two EPR counts
comparable only with the assumptions stated. Over the nine-circuit 64-qubit
suite \dSABRE{} uses $1.43\times$ fewer EPR pairs in geometric mean, DMapS
winning only on GHZ and Graphstate, which KaHyPar partitions almost
perfectly; the per-circuit head-to-head is in the online appendices.

\textbf{Device model.}
The three comparisons differ in what they assume the hardware provides, and
\dSABRE{}'s assumptions are the most conservative. Following \TeleSABRE{}, it
represents communication ports explicitly, treats a port as an ordinary slot
that must be evacuated before it can carry a state, gives an inter-core link
a cost of its own, bounds every core's capacity, and consumes each EPR pair
at once. The other two relax exactly these points: \texttt{pytket-dqc} allows
unboundedly many live link qubits per core and entanglement that survives a
whole group of gates, and DMapS omits the ports and with them the capacity
question. Modelling more of the device is not demanding more of it:
\dSABRE{} asks only for a connected coupling graph, the two to three ports
per core the evaluated devices already have, two spare slots per core and
one more on the device (Eqs.~\ref{eq:feasible}--\ref{eq:invariant}),
and no entanglement storage at all, whereas the alternative models assume
communication memory or occupancy-preserving remote operations the
evaluated architecture does not represent. Extra ports, heterogeneous link
costs, or a non-uniform core graph enter through the same distance tables and
the same $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ term, leaving the score and the completion
argument unchanged.

\textbf{Allocation, communication, and scheduling.}
Hypergraph-based methods assign qubits to cores by minimising cut
communication~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024}. They are
complementary to \dSABRE{}: their output can supply an initial allocation,
while \dSABRE{} resolves the local movement and capacity constraints that
arise during execution. Finally,
time-aware DQC compilers schedule entanglement generation and remote
operations subject to link and buffer constraints~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler}. Such scheduling is orthogonal to the present EPR-count
objective and is a natural downstream extension of the teleport trace
produced by \dSABRE{}.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{Telegate and communication bursts.}
Scoring a telegate is not the hard part, and it is not what stands in the
way. A telegate executes a remote gate without relocating either operand, so it
consumes no destination capacity and enters Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre} with
$c_{\mathrm{cap}}=0$, keeping only the port staging $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ its two
link qubits need; its progress term is the front-layer gate it retires outright
rather than a distance reduction. Implemented that way and allowed to compete
with teledata, it costs $93.5\%$ more EPR on the 64-qubit suite, and the best of
seventeen scoring variants we swept is still $1.0\%$ worse than teledata
alone.
Amortisation is the reason, and it is measurable: one teledata pair already
serves $15.75$ gates on this suite, $83.4\%$ of relocations serving two or more,
whereas an ungrouped telegate serves exactly one. A telegate therefore pays only
in grouped form, for which the router must expose commutation-derived bursts,
choose the beneficiary core, and hold entanglement for the group's duration.
AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm} and QuComm~\cite{wu2023qucomm} give
pre-processing directions, and lifetime-bounded grouping
in~\cite{bandini2026optimized} shows that a burst is worth taking only when its
entanglement survives the hardware limit. The measurement is in the online
appendices.

\textbf{Scheduling, fidelity, and interconnect co-design.}
This paper minimises EPR consumption rather than makespan or output error, and
the trace it emits is unscheduled. A scheduling pass should batch teleports
whose links, ports, and staging operations do not conflict, and should account
for entanglement-generation rate, storage lifetime, and hardware error rates;
existing time-aware compilers~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler} provide
starting points. The objective should stay explicitly multi-objective, since
EPR count, circuit depth, and fidelity trade off in hardware-dependent ways.
The interconnect is the other free variable. The evaluated devices give each
pair of adjacent cores a single link and each core two or three ports, which
is what the baseline architectures provide rather than a limit of the method:
Section~\ref{sec:tele} already enumerates every link joining two cores as
its own candidate, though on the evaluated devices that loop never has more
than one link to weigh.
Widening it should relieve the availability term $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ first,
since a second link turns an occupied port into an alternative rather than an
obstacle, and should interact with the capacity mechanism, because a core with
more ports has more ways to spend and to recover a free slot. Finding where
the return on added links falls off against its hardware cost is a useful
input to interconnect
co-design~\cite{escofet2023interconnect,wu2023qucomm,liu2025codesign}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented \dSABRE{}, a \SABRE{}-style teledata router for
multi-core distributed quantum computers. Its design principle is to treat an
inter-core move as a complete macro-action rather than a local SWAP with a
larger edge cost, scoring the routing progress it buys---front-layer distance
reduction and DAG-depth-discounted lookahead---against the two costs specific
to a directional teleport, port staging and destination capacity. Capacity
enters twice, and the separation is the point: as a graded score term that
ranks candidates, and as a hard legality floor that, with a bounded recovery
transaction, makes \dSABRE{} terminate having routed every gate under checkable
pre-routing conditions.

On 21 MQT Bench circuits at 25--64 logical qubits \dSABRE{} reduces
geometric-mean EPR consumption by 49--62\% against \TeleSABRE{} under the
shared teledata cost model, completes all 45 evaluated circuit--architecture
instances to the baseline's 40, and compiles a 360-qubit QFT on twenty cores in
48\,s. Removing both capacity mechanisms costs $87.8\%$ and leaves two
instances unrouted, whereas replacing the BFS-layer extended set costs
$12.4\%$: the principal advance is not the number of heuristic terms but the
separation of resource-aware ranking from an explicit legality condition and a
completion-preserving recovery. Recovery itself is a safeguard, not an
optimisation---no reported route at 25--64 qubits invokes it, while at 100,
200, and 360 qubits it retires 6, 10, and 66 gates.

These reductions are in one resource, under one communication model. The
direct comparison is with \TeleSABRE{}, which shares teledata and local-SWAP
accounting; remote-SWAP and telegate-based tools need their own capacity and
entanglement-lifetime assumptions stated before their counts can be read
against ours. The emitted traces are also unscheduled, so fewer EPR pairs does
not by itself imply a shorter makespan or a higher-fidelity result. Joint
selection between teledata and grouped telegate operations, followed by time-
and fidelity-aware scheduling of the resulting trace, is the natural next
step.
