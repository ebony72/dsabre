\begin{abstract}
Routing a quantum circuit on a distributed quantum computer (DQC)
consumes one EPR pair for every two-qubit gate whose operands lie in
different cores; minimising EPR consumption is the dominant
compilation objective.  We present \dSABRE{}, a \SABRE{}-style router
for multi-core processors that integrates intra-core SWAP scoring and
inter-core teleportation candidate scoring into a single,
lookahead-driven loop, and adds three mechanisms over classical
\SABRE{}: a five-term teleportation score combining staging cost,
core capacity, hop gain, and front/extended-set distance gains; a
\emph{proactive congestion-relief} pass that preemptively
redistributes idle qubits out of high-demand cores; and a
BFS-layer extended-set construction that respects DAG dependencies
layer by layer rather than mixing wires uniformly in topological
order.  A defensive checkpoint--rollback mechanism handles deadlock
states.  \dSABRE{} operates on any DQC modelled as a weighted graph
$G_\arch=(V,E)$ with cheap intra-core edges and costly inter-core
links; the reference implementation uses grid-of-grid topologies
(B-grid, H-grid).  Using standard Qiskit SabreLayout for initial
placement (no \TeleSABRE{} infrastructure required), \dSABRE{} reduces
EPR consumption over the state-of-the-art \TeleSABRE{} by a geometric
mean of \textbf{41.1\%} / \textbf{44.1\%} / \textbf{43.9\%} on 25q /
36q / 64q suites (B-grid $2{\times}2$ $4{\times}4$ and H-grid
$2{\times}3$ $4{\times}4$), and over the gate-teleportation-based
pytket-dqc by \textbf{68.4\%} / \textbf{29.4\%} / \textbf{15.7\%} on
the same suites.  \dSABRE{} also completes a Random 64q circuit on
which \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge, and scales to 200- and
360-qubit benchmarks: at 200q \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge within
a 600\,s timeout while \dSABRE{} completes both circuits; at 360q
\dSABRE{} wins by $35\%$ gmean EPR over \TeleSABRE{}.
Under a combined cost
$c_{\mathrm{tele}}\,\#\mathrm{EPR} + c_{\mathrm{swap}}\,\#\mathrm{SWAP}$
with $c_{\mathrm{swap}}{=}3$, \dSABRE{} saves up to $43\%$ over
\TeleSABRE{} for $c_{\mathrm{tele}}{\in}\{10,20,50,100\}$.  All
software, benchmarks, and per-circuit results accompany the paper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The near-term path to fault-tolerant quantum computing requires processors with
hundreds to thousands of high-fidelity qubits.
No single monolithic chip can economically deliver this scale today.
Instead, the field is converging on \emph{distributed quantum computers}
(DQC)~\cite{monroe2014large,wehner2018quantum,stephenson2020high}: multiple
small quantum processing units (cores) interconnected by photonic or microwave
links that support remote entanglement generation.

Executing a quantum circuit on a DQC requires a \emph{qubit mapping and routing}
pass.
Intra-core two-qubit gates between non-adjacent qubits are resolved with SWAP
insertions; inter-core gates require \emph{quantum teleportation}: one
pre-shared EPR pair is consumed to move a logical qubit across an inter-core
link.
EPR generation is slow (microseconds to milliseconds), noisy, and rate-limited
in current hardware, making EPR minimisation the dominant compilation objective
for DQC.

State-of-the-art distributed routers extend \SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling}
to DQC by scoring teleportation candidates with a heuristic that combines
the immediate front-layer distance reduction and a lookahead term over a
fixed window of upcoming
gates~\cite{andres2019automated,ferrari2021compiler,baker2020time}.
The most recent and best-performing of these is \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre},
which uses a Dijkstra computation on a contracted communication graph
to estimate inter-core routing cost.  We observe two limitations.
First, treating intra-core SWAP decisions and inter-core teleport
decisions as separate sub-procedures forfeits the opportunity to score
them against the \emph{same} front and lookahead layers, so the choice
of teleport may not align with what subsequent intra-core SWAPs need.
Second, congestion (cores running out of communication-port capacity)
is treated reactively: it is only addressed when it triggers a
deadlock, by which point the recovery often costs additional EPR pairs.

\textbf{Contributions.}
We make the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The \dSABRE{} algorithm.}
    A SABRE-style router for multi-core processors that integrates
    intra-core SWAP scoring and inter-core teleport candidate scoring
    into a single iteration loop.  Each inter-core candidate receives
    a five-term score (staging cost, capacity penalty, hop gain,
    front-layer distance gain, decay-weighted extended-set gain) that
    is directly comparable to the local \SABRE{} swap heuristic, so
    the two decisions are mutually consistent
    (Section~\ref{sec:tele}).  The same loop also generates
    \emph{proactive congestion-relief} candidates: when the lookahead
    indicates a core will saturate, idle qubits in that core are
    offered as candidates to neighbouring cores before deadlock
    occurs (Section~\ref{sec:relief}).  A defensive
    checkpoint--rollback escape handles the rare deadlock state
    (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}).

  \item \textbf{BFS-layer extended set.}
    \dSABRE{}'s extended-set construction expands the working DAG
    layer by layer rather than in pure topological order, and within
    each layer prioritises gates that share a qubit with the front
    layer.  This eliminates a subtle bias in topological-order
    extended sets that under-counts $\Delta_E$ for inter-core
    teleports preceding a wire-aligned chain of pending gates
    (Section~\ref{sec:bfsext}).  An ablation
    (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) shows the BFS construction reduces
    geometric-mean EPRs by $20$--$45\%$ relative to topological
    order at fixed scoring.

  \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation against two baselines.}
    On 18 MQT-Bench circuits~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench} across
    three suites (25- and 36-qubit on a $2{\times}2$ B-grid of
    $4{\times}4$ cores; 64-qubit on a $2{\times}3$ H-grid of
    $4{\times}4$ cores) we compare against the state-of-the-art
    \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} and the
    gate-teleportation-based pytket-dqc~\cite{pytketdqc2024}.  Using
    only standard Qiskit passes for initial layout, \dSABRE{}
    achieves geometric-mean EPR reductions of \textbf{41.1\%},
    \textbf{44.1\%}, and \textbf{43.9\%} over \TeleSABRE{}, and
    \textbf{68.4\%}, \textbf{29.4\%}, and \textbf{15.7\%} over
    pytket-dqc on the three suites respectively, and completes a
    64-qubit Random circuit on which \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge.
  \item \textbf{Scalability to 100--360 qubits.}
    On a large-circuit QFT sweep at 100, 200, and 360 logical qubits
    (Section~\ref{sec:scalability}), \dSABRE{} reduces EPRs by $21\%$
    vs.\ \TeleSABRE{} at 100q, completes the 200q circuit on which
    \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge within a 600\,s timeout, and wins
    by $48\%$ at 360q.  Against \texttt{pytket-dqc}, \dSABRE{} wins
    at 100q ($-63\%$) and 360q ($-14\%$), and is within $3\%$ at 200q.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Quantum Circuits}
\label{sec:qcircuits}

A quantum circuit acts on $n$ \emph{qubits} through a sequence of \emph{gates}.
\emph{One-qubit (1Q) gates} (Hadamard, rotations, \ldots) act on a single qubit.
\emph{Two-qubit (2Q) gates} entangle pairs: the \emph{CX} (CNOT) gate is the
standard primitive and the building block for most multi-qubit operations.
The \emph{SWAP} gate exchanges the states of two adjacent qubits; it decomposes
into three CX gates and is used by routing algorithms to migrate logical qubit
states along the coupling graph.

A circuit is represented as a \emph{directed acyclic graph} (DAG) in which each
node is a gate and each directed edge records a data dependency (qubit produced
by gate $g_1$ consumed by gate $g_2$).
The \emph{front layer} $F$ is the set of nodes with no unexecuted
predecessor---gates that are immediately ready to run.
The \emph{extended set} $E$ is a fixed-size lookahead window of gates just
beyond $F$, used by SABRE-style heuristics to anticipate upcoming demands.
\emph{Circuit depth} is the length of the longest path through the DAG.

Physical qubits are arranged in a \emph{coupling graph} $G_\mathrm{phys}=(P,E_\mathrm{phys})$;
a 2Q gate can execute only if its two operands occupy adjacent vertices.
A circuit is written in terms of \emph{logical qubits} $\logi{1},\ldots,\logi{n}$;
a \emph{layout} $\ell:\logi{i}\!\mapsto\!\phys{p}$ assigns each to a physical
slot.
\emph{Qubit routing} inserts SWAP gates so that the two operands of every 2Q
gate reach adjacent physical qubits before it executes.

\subsection{Quantum Teleportation}
\label{sec:teleportation}

\emph{Quantum teleportation}~\cite{bennett1993teleporting} transfers the state
of a qubit to a remote location using a pre-shared \emph{EPR pair} (a maximally
entangled two-qubit Bell state) and two classical bits.
The source qubit's state is destroyed at the sender and reconstructed at the
receiver; no physical qubit travels across the link.
Each EPR pair is consumed upon use, so \emph{EPR count} is the primary
inter-core routing cost in a DQC.

Two inter-core teleportation variants arise in DQC compilation.
\emph{Teledata} moves a logical qubit to a new core (one EPR pair consumed),
after which the qubit participates in local gates there.
\emph{Telegate}~\cite{eisert2000optimal} executes a non-local 2Q gate directly
across two cores via a shared EPR pair, without physically relocating either
qubit.
\dSABRE{} uses teledata exclusively; \TeleSABRE{} exploits both variants.

\subsection{Distributed Quantum Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

We model a DQC architecture $\arch$ abstractly as a weighted graph
$G_\arch=(V,E,w)$ whose vertices are physical qubits.
The vertex set is partitioned into $K$ \emph{cores} $V = V_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_K$;
intra-core edges $E_{\mathrm{intra}}$ describe in-core connectivity and carry
weight~$1$, and inter-core edges $E_{\mathrm{inter}}$ connect designated
\emph{communication ports} on neighbouring cores and carry a much larger
weight $w_{\mathrm{inter}} \gg 1$ reflecting the cost of an EPR pair.
\dSABRE{} treats $G_\arch$ as a black box and only queries it through three
distance tables: physical $\dphys$, intra-core $\dintra$, and core-graph
$\dcore$.
The router therefore extends to \emph{any} DQC topology --- arbitrary
intra-core graphs, mixed core sizes, sparse inter-core meshes, hierarchical
trees of clusters, etc. --- by simply supplying these tables.

For experimental simplicity our reference implementation instantiates
$G_\arch$ as a \emph{grid-of-grids}: each core is an $m\!\times\!m$ 2D
nearest-neighbour grid, and the cores themselves form an $r\!\times\!s$
super-grid joined by a small set of inter-core links between specific
boundary qubits.
We use three port arrangements (Section~\ref{sec:impl}): \emph{basic}
(ports at mid-edges), \emph{B-grid} (staggered ports), and \emph{H-grid}
(asymmetrically skewed ports).

\paragraph{Hierarchical distance precomputation.}
Because intra-core and inter-core costs decouple, the all-pairs distance
$\dphys$ over $G_\arch$ admits a two-level Dijkstra computation that is
substantially cheaper than running Dijkstra on the full $|V|$-vertex graph.
First, all-pairs distances are computed once \emph{inside} a single core
$V_i$ (size $|V_i|=M$) in $O(M^2 \log M)$ time, giving $\dintra$.
Second, the \emph{core graph} (a multigraph of $K$ super-nodes connected
by inter-core links) is solved in $O(K^2 \log K)$ time, giving $\dcore$.
Third, $\dphys(p,q)$ for any $p\in V_i$, $q\in V_j$ is recovered as
$\dintra(p,\pi_i^*) + \dcore(i,j) + \dintra(\pi_j^*,q)$ minimised over
boundary-port pairs $(\pi_i^*, \pi_j^*)$ realising the optimal core-graph
path.
This brings precomputation from $O((KM)^2 \log(KM))$ down to
$O(KM^2\log M + K^2\log K)$ and is the only architecture-dependent setup
\dSABRE{} performs; routing itself uses $O(1)$ table lookups thereafter.

\subsection{Qubit Mapping and Routing}

Given circuit DAG $C$ and initial layout $\ell:\logi{i}\!\mapsto\!\phys{\sigma(i)}$,
routing inserts the minimum number of SWAP and teleportation operations so every
gate can execute on adjacent physical qubits.
Cost model: each local SWAP costs $c_{\mathrm{swap}}$; each inter-core
teleport costs $c_{\mathrm{tele}}$ (one EPR pair).

\subsection{SABRE}

\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} selects the SWAP minimising:
\begin{equation}
  H = \tfrac{1}{|F|}\!\sum_{g\in F}\!\Delta_F(g)
    + w\,\tfrac{1}{|E|}\!\sum_{g\in E}\!\Delta_E(g),
  \label{eq:sabre}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the front layer, $E$ the extended lookahead set, and
$\Delta(g) = d_{\mathrm{old}}^g - d_{\mathrm{new}}^g$ the reduction in
physical distance between the two qubits of gate $g$ caused by the
candidate SWAP ($d^g$ is shortest-path distance on the coupling graph).
All extended-set gates contribute with equal weight; the $\tfrac{1}{|E|}$
factor normalises the lookahead term to the same scale as the front term.
\SABRE{} also couples its router with an initial-layout optimizer: starting
from a random qubit assignment it routes the circuit $C$ forward, then
immediately routes the reverse circuit $C^{-1}$ using the final layout of
the forward pass as the new starting point; the layout produced by the
reverse pass is fed back as the initial layout for the next forward pass.
After several bidirectional sweeps the heuristic has annealed toward a
layout that is self-consistent with the circuit's gate structure.

\section{dSABRE}
\label{sec:dsabre}

\dSABRE{} is a SABRE-style routing algorithm for multi-core processors.
It inherits the front-layer / extended-set scoring philosophy of
\SABRE{}—at the intra-core level its SWAP-selection objective is identical
to Eq.~\ref{eq:sabre}—but replaces the flat qubit-connectivity graph with
a two-level hierarchy of cores and communication ports, and substitutes
teleportation for inter-core movement.  Because teleportation is
irreversible (consuming an EPR pair regardless of direction), the
bidirectional layout trick does not carry over; \dSABRE{} instead relies on
Qiskit SabreLayout (corners removed) to produce a
good starting assignment. The core invariants and notation are: (i) the layout
$\ell:\logi{q}\!\to\!\phys{p}$ assigns logical to physical qubits and
the inverse $\ell^{-1}$ records which logical qubit (if any) currently
occupies each physical slot; (ii) the working DAG~$W$ holds the
remaining gates (initially the full circuit) and shrinks as gates are
executed; (iii) the front layer~$F$ is the set of gates in $W$ with no
unexecuted predecessors, and the extended set~$E$ is a fixed-size
sliding lookahead beyond $F$; (iv) a \emph{node decay}
$\delta:\phys{p}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}_{\ge 1}$ is incremented when a
qubit slot participates in a SWAP, biasing the heuristic away from
oscillation.

\subsection{Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsabre} sketches the main routing loop.
Each iteration runs four phases:

\textbf{(P1) Front-layer drainage.}
Gates in $F$ with one operand (1Q gates) are executed and removed unconditionally;
2Q gates whose operands sit on adjacent qubits in the same core are
executed and removed.  Drainage iterates until a fixed point because
removing a gate can expose its successors to the front and create new
opportunities to drain.  Both endpoints of an executed gate have their
decay reset to $1$.

\textbf{(P2) Front classification.}
The remaining front layer is partitioned into intra-core gates
$F_\mathrm{intra}$ (both operands in the same core) and inter-core
gates $F_\mathrm{inter}$ (operands in different cores).  At least one
of the two sets is non-empty, otherwise drainage would have continued.

\textbf{(P3) Decision.}
If $F_\mathrm{intra}\!\neq\!\emptyset$, the iteration takes one
intra-core SWAP step (Section~\ref{sec:intra}); otherwise it scores
teleportation candidates over $F_\mathrm{inter}$ and applies the
best-scoring move (Section~\ref{sec:tele}).
The two scoring functions both consume the same $F$ and $E$ layers,
so an intra-core decision and an inter-core decision are directly
comparable and the router does not oscillate between them.

\textbf{(P4) Progress accounting and recovery.}
If the iteration reduced $|W|$, a checkpoint of $(\ell, W, \delta)$ is
saved.  After $L_\mathrm{deadlock}$ consecutive iterations that did
not reduce $|W|$, the checkpoint is restored and a forced hop is
applied (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}).  After
$N_\mathrm{backup}^\mathrm{max}$ failed recoveries, the route is
aborted.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\dSABRE{} \texttt{route}$(C, \ell_0)$}
\label{alg:dsabre}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $W \leftarrow$ working copy of circuit DAG $C$
\STATE $\ell \leftarrow \ell_0$;\ \ $\delta[p]\!\leftarrow\!1$ for all $p$
\STATE save checkpoint $(\ell, W, \delta)$
\WHILE{$W$ has unexecuted gates}
  \STATE drain $F$ (execute 1Q gates and adjacent intra-core 2Q gates) \hfill\textit{(P1)}
  \IF{$W$ empty} \STATE \textbf{break} \ENDIF
  \STATE compute $F$, partition into $F_\mathrm{intra}, F_\mathrm{inter}$ \hfill\textit{(P2)}
  \STATE compute $E$ if not cached
  \IF{$F_\mathrm{intra}\!\neq\!\emptyset$}
    \STATE pick best SWAP $(u,v)$ within a core (Eq.~\ref{eq:intra}) \hfill\textit{(P3)}
    \STATE apply SWAP$(u,v)$;\ $\delta[u]\!+\!=\!0.1$;\ $\delta[v]\!+\!=\!0.1$
  \ELSIF{$F_\mathrm{inter}\!\neq\!\emptyset$}
    \STATE generate candidates over $F_\mathrm{inter}$ and relief moves
    \STATE pick lowest-score candidate (Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre})
    \STATE apply teleport;\ reset comm-port decays
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$|W|$ decreased} \STATE save checkpoint \hfill\textit{(P4)}
  \ELSIF{$L_\mathrm{deadlock}$ stalled iterations}
    \STATE restore checkpoint;\ \texttt{backup\_plan}$(W,\ell)$
  \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $(\ell, \mathrm{metrics})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Intra-core SWAP heuristic}
\label{sec:intra}

When the front contains a gate whose operands share a core but are
not adjacent, \dSABRE{} chooses an intra-core SWAP using the standard
\SABRE{} heuristic, restricted to the relevant core.  Let
$F_\mathrm{c} = \{g\in F_\mathrm{intra} : \mathrm{core}(g)=c\}$ be the
front gates in core~$c$ and $E_\mathrm{c} = (g_0, g_1, \ldots)$ the
corresponding intra-core extended set, an \emph{ordered list} of at most
$L$ gates (default $L{=}20$) collected in topological order.
The traversal stops including a gate the moment either of its qubits
has previously appeared in a cross-core gate in the working DAG
(taint propagation), ensuring $E_\mathrm{c}$ contains only gates
that remain local to core~$c$.
Each gate~$g_i$ is assigned a depth $\mathrm{dep}(g_i)$, the number of
intra-core gates on the longest qubit-wire path from the front layer to
$g_i$ (front layer = depth~0; first successor = depth~1), so
$\tilde{\Delta}_E^\mathrm{c} = \sum_{i=0}^{|E_\mathrm{c}|-1}
\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g_i)}(d_{\mathrm{old}}^{g_i} - d_{\mathrm{new}}^{g_i})$,
where $\gamma$ is the lookahead decay and the distance terms use the
intra-core graph.
For every neighbour pair $(u,v)$ within a core, the score
\begin{equation}
  H_\mathrm{intra} = \max(\delta[u], \delta[v]) \cdot
    \Big(\frac{\Delta_F^\mathrm{c}}{|F_\mathrm{c}|}
        + w_e\,\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_E^\mathrm{c}}{|E_\mathrm{c}|}\Big)
  \label{eq:intra}
\end{equation}
is the SABRE objective with the same lookahead-decay weighting and
node-decay multiplier used for inter-core scoring.  The lowest-score
SWAP is applied; tie-breaking is by enumeration order.

\subsection{Inter-core teleportation scoring}
\label{sec:tele}

For each gate $g\in F_\mathrm{inter}$ on qubits $(q_1, q_2)$ with
$q_1\in c_\mathrm{src}, q_2\in c_\mathrm{tgt}$, both endpoints are tried as
teleportation sources.  For the qubit chosen as source (say $q_1$ at
physical position $p_1$), the router enumerates every neighbouring core
$c_\mathrm{next}\in N(c_\mathrm{src})$ and every inter-core link
$(\pi_s, \pi_d)$ between $c_\mathrm{src}$ and $c_\mathrm{next}$.
Each such triple $(q_1, c_\mathrm{next}, (\pi_s,\pi_d))$ is one
\emph{candidate}; Figure~\ref{fig:tele-candidates} illustrates the structure.
Executing a candidate involves two steps: (1)~intra-core SWAPs that move
$q_1$ from $p_1$ to the \emph{staging slot} $n_s$, the neighbour of $\pi_s$
closest to $p_1$; and (2)~a teleportation that consumes the EPR pair on link
$(\pi_s,\pi_d)$ and delivers $q_1$ to $\pi_d$ in $c_\mathrm{next}$.
Each candidate is scored as:

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \input{fig/teleport_candidates}
  \caption{Teleportation candidates for gate $g=(q_1,q_2)$.  The router
    enumerates all triples (source qubit, $c_\mathrm{next}$, comm link
    $(\pi_s,\pi_d)$) and scores each by Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre}.  Step~(1): local
    SWAPs move $q_1$ from its current position $p_1$ to staging slot $n_s$.
    Step~(2): a teleportation delivers $q_1$ to $\pi_d$ in $c_\mathrm{next}$.
    The greyed core $c_\mathrm{bot}$ illustrates that any neighbouring core
    is a valid next-hop, even if it is not $c_\mathrm{tgt}$.}
  \label{fig:tele-candidates}
\end{figure}
\begin{equation}
  s = \underbrace{d_{\mathrm{prep}}}_{\text{staging}} +
      \underbrace{c_{\mathrm{cap}}}_{\text{capacity}} -
      \underbrace{g_{\mathrm{hop}}}_{\text{hop gain}} -
      \underbrace{\Delta_F}_{\text{front gain}} -
      \underbrace{w_e\,\tilde{\Delta}_E}_{\text{lookahead}},
  \label{eq:dsabre}
\end{equation}
where lower scores are preferred.  Each term is defined as follows.

\textbf{Staging cost} $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ is the number of intra-core SWAPs
needed to move $q_1$ from $p_1$ to staging slot $n_s$, plus eviction SWAPs
to free the comm ports if occupied:
\[
  d_{\mathrm{prep}} = d_{\mathrm{intra}}(p_1,\,n_s)
                    + d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi_s)
                    + d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi_d).
\]

\textbf{Capacity penalty} $c_{\mathrm{cap}} = c_{\mathrm{pen}}\,\max(0,\,\tau - f_{\mathrm{dst}})$
discourages routing into nearly-full cores.
$f_{\mathrm{dst}}$ is the number of free slots in the destination core,
$\tau$ the capacity threshold, and $c_{\mathrm{pen}}$ a cost multiplier.
This prevents progressive crowding of hot cores that leads to deadlock.

\textbf{Hop gain} $g_{\mathrm{hop}} = w_h\,(\dcore(c_{\mathrm{src}},c_{\mathrm{tgt}}) - \dcore(c_{\mathrm{next}},c_{\mathrm{tgt}}))$
rewards moving the qubit closer to the core that holds its gate partner,
acting as a coarse directional bias on the core graph.

\textbf{Front-layer gain} $\Delta_F = d_{\mathrm{old}}^{g^*} - d_{\mathrm{new}}^{g^*}$
is the distance reduction for the unique front-layer gate $g^*$
involving the moving qubit (zero if no such gate exists).
Because the front layer contains only gates whose predecessors have all
been executed, two gates sharing a qubit cannot both appear in $F$
simultaneously; hence at most one gate in $F_\mathrm{inter}$ involves
the qubit being teleported.

\textbf{Decay-weighted lookahead}
$\tilde{\Delta}_E = \sum_{i=0}^{|E|-1} \gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g_i)}\,(d_{\mathrm{old}}^{g_i} - d_{\mathrm{new}}^{g_i})$,
where $E = (g_0,g_1,\ldots)$ is the extended set as an ordered list of
at most $L$ gates (default $L{=}20$), $\mathrm{dep}(g_i)$ is the DAG
distance of $g_i$ from the front layer (number of edges on the longest
dependency path from any front-layer gate to $g_i$; front layer = 0,
immediate successors = 1), and the sum is restricted to gates that
involve the qubit being teleported.  The tilde distinguishes this from
$\Delta_E(g)$ in vanilla \SABRE{} (Eq.~\ref{eq:sabre}): \SABRE{}
weights all extended gates equally and normalises by $|E|$, whereas
$\tilde{\Delta}_E$ applies an exponential decay $\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g_i)}$
by DAG distance so that gates close to the front (small $\mathrm{dep}$)
dominate and influence falls off smoothly, removing the hard
front-vs-extended boundary.

\subsection{Proactive Congestion Relief}
\label{sec:relief}

In dense layouts, a core can become a bottleneck: many pending gates demand
qubits from it simultaneously, but its communication ports are saturated.
Standard reactive scoring (Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre}) does not address this until
the bottleneck triggers a deadlock.

\dSABRE{} adds a \emph{proactive congestion relief} mechanism.
At each routing iteration, we compute a demand vector $d[c]$ over all cores
by counting inter-core gates in the lookahead window whose shortest path
traverses core~$c$.
If any core~$c$ satisfies $d[c] \ge \theta_d$ and $f_c \le \theta_f$
(high demand, few free slots), we generate \emph{relief candidates}: teleports
that move the most inactively-used logical qubits \emph{out} of $c$ and into a
less-loaded neighbour, scored by a dedicated relief bonus proportional to the
demand--free-slot imbalance.
These candidates compete directly with gate-driven teleports and are selected
only when they improve the aggregate score.
The mechanism prevents deadlock spirals by redistributing qubit load
\emph{before} the core fills entirely.

\subsection{Checkpoint--Rollback Deadlock Escape}
\label{sec:recovery}

The greedy scoring of Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre} can drive the router into a
state where no candidate makes progress.  \dSABRE{} provides a
defensive cushion: every iteration that strictly reduces $|W|$ saves a
checkpoint of $(\ell, W, \delta)$.  After $L_\mathrm{deadlock}$
consecutive non-progress iterations, the router restores the
checkpoint and forces the most-stuck front-layer gate forward via
shortest-path SWAPs (intra-core) or hop-by-hop teleportation along
$\mathrm{core\_path}(c_1,c_2)$ (inter-core).  After
$N_\mathrm{backup}^\mathrm{max}$ failed recoveries the route aborts.
The recovery path is dormant on most circuits; it is decisive only on
the 64-qubit Random circuit, where \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge but
\dSABRE{} completes the route (Section~\ref{sec:scalability}).

\subsection{Computational Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

Let $N{=}|C|$ be the circuit size, $n$ the logical-qubit count (circuit
width, so $|F|\le n$), $K$ the number of cores, $M$ qubits per core
($P{=}KM$ total), $d$ the maximum core-graph degree, $b$ the maximum
number of inter-core links between any adjacent core pair, and $L$ the
extended-set capacity per core (a constant, default 20).

\paragraph{Architecture setup.}
Distance tables are precomputed once in $O(KM^2\!\log M + K^2\!\log K)$
(Section~\ref{sec:arch}); this cost is incurred once and not charged per
circuit.

\paragraph{Per-iteration cost.}
The dominant cost each iteration is candidate generation and scoring.
Inter-core scoring (Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre}) generates at most
$|F_\mathrm{inter}|\cdot 2d\cdot b$ candidates; evaluating $\Delta_F$
and $\tilde{\Delta}_E$ for each costs $O(|F|+L)$.
Intra-core scoring (Eq.~\ref{eq:intra}) examines $O(M_e)$ SWAP pairs
per active core, each in $O(|F_\mathrm{intra}|+L)$, where $M_e$ denotes
intra-core edges per core.
For our bounded-degree grid-of-grids ($d,b,M_e{=}O(1)$, $L$ constant):
\[
  T_\mathrm{iter} = O\!\left(|F|\cdot(|F|+L) + K\cdot M_e\cdot(|F|+L)\right)
                  = O(n^2).
\]

\paragraph{Extended-set cost.}
The extended set is rebuilt whenever the front layer changes, at most
$N$ times total (once per gate execution).  Each rebuild traverses the
working DAG until $L$ gates per core are collected; the cost is
$O(\min(|W|,KL))$ per rebuild.  Total extended-set cost across all
iterations: $O(N \cdot KL) = O(NK)$ with $L$ constant.  This matches
the $O(|W|{+}KL)$ single-pass bound stated in
Section~\ref{sec:bfsext}.

\paragraph{Total complexity.}
Each routing iteration either drains at least one gate (at most $N$
such iterations) or inserts one SWAP or teleport.  On a grid-of-grids
the physical-graph diameter is $O(\sqrt{M}+\sqrt{K})=O(\sqrt{P})$,
bounding inserted operations by $O(N\sqrt{P})$ in the worst case.
The checkpoint--rollback escape (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}) adds at
most $N_\mathrm{backup}^\mathrm{max}\cdot L_\mathrm{deadlock}$
extra iterations, a small additive constant.  Combining:
\begin{equation}
  T_\mathrm{total} = O\!\left(N\sqrt{P}\cdot n^2 + NK\right).
  \label{eq:complexity}
\end{equation}
The $NK$ term from extended-set rebuilds is dominated for any
non-trivial circuit.  The leading term $O(N\sqrt{P}\cdot n^2)$
matches the complexity of \SABRE{} on a flat $P$-qubit
chip~\cite{li2019tackling}: the multi-core hierarchy does not increase
the asymptotic exponent.
In practice $|F|\ll n$ for depth-limited circuits and $K,M$ are small
constants, so the effective per-iteration cost is $O(1)$ with a compact
leading constant; empirical runtimes are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Comparison with TeleSABRE}
\label{sec:vs_ts}

The closest prior router is
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}.  The principal algorithmic
difference is the inter-core scoring: \TeleSABRE{} evaluates candidates
via a \emph{contracted communication-graph energy} that aggregates over
all logical qubits and uses a topological-order extended set, whereas
\dSABRE{} uses a local, gate-centric five-term score that explicitly
accounts for intra-core staging cost, core capacity, and directional
progress (Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre}), paired with a BFS-layer extended set
(Section~\ref{sec:bfsext}).  This makes \dSABRE{}'s teleport decisions
directly comparable to its intra-core SWAP decisions
(Eq.~\ref{eq:intra}).  \dSABRE{} additionally generates proactive
congestion-relief candidates (Section~\ref{sec:relief}) and a
checkpoint--rollback deadlock escape (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}),
whereas \TeleSABRE{} relies on a safety-valve iteration cap.
\TeleSABRE{} also supports telegate (remote CX execution) candidates;
\dSABRE{} currently uses only teledata moves.

\section{Extended-Set Construction}
\label{sec:bfsext}

The extended-set term $\tilde{\Delta}_E$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre} drives
\dSABRE{}'s lookahead.  How $E$ is built therefore controls how far
the heuristic sees beyond the front layer.  This section describes
\dSABRE{}'s default construction --- a BFS-layer expansion --- and
contrasts it with the topological-order construction used by
\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} and \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}.

\subsection{Why topological order is suboptimal}

Consider any circuit fragment in which the front layer
$F=\{g_F\}$ contains a single inter-core gate on qubits $(u,v)$ with
$u$ in core $c_u$ and $v$ in $c_v$, and the wire $u$ continues with
several pending 2Q gates whose partner qubits are also in $c_v$ (or
adjacent cores).  A teleport that moves $u$ to $c_v$ would
simultaneously reduce the inter-core distance of $g_F$ and of all
those follow-on gates.  However, the topological-order extended set
typically does \emph{not} contain those follow-on gates: topological
order interleaves all wires uniformly, so the lookahead window of
$|E|_\mathrm{max}$ gates fills with unrelated gates from other
wires before the walk returns to $u$.  Consequently $\Delta_E$
undercounts the benefit of the teleport, and the router may commit
additional teleports later when the follow-on gates eventually reach
the front layer.

\subsection{BFS-layer expansion with front-priority}

We replace the topological walk with a BFS-layer expansion of the
remaining DAG.  Algorithm~\ref{alg:bfsext} pre-computes the op-node
in-degree of every gate, commits the front layer (decrementing
in-degrees of its successors), then iteratively collects successive
``layers'' of gates whose remaining in-degree has dropped to zero.
Within each layer, gates that share a qubit with the original front
layer are emitted first; the rest follow in topological order.
The expansion stops once $|E|$ reaches the requested size.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\texttt{ExtendedSet-BFS}$(W, F, |E|_\mathrm{max})$}
\label{alg:bfsext}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $Q_F \leftarrow \bigcup_{g\in F}\,g.\mathit{qargs}$
\STATE $r[n] \leftarrow$ op-node in-degree of $n$, for every $n \in W$
\STATE $D \leftarrow \emptyset$;\ \ \emph{// committed nodes}
\FOR{$f \in F$}
  \STATE $D \leftarrow D \cup \{f\}$
  \FOR{$s \in \text{successors}(f)$ with $\mathrm{qargs}(s)\!\neq\!\bot$}
    \STATE $r[s]\!-\!=\!1$
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\STATE $E \leftarrow []$;\ \ current $\leftarrow F$
\WHILE{$|E| < |E|_\mathrm{max}$}
  \STATE $L \leftarrow \emptyset$ \emph{// next BFS layer}
  \FOR{$n \in $ current}
    \FOR{$s \in \text{successors}(n)$, $s\!\notin\!D$, $r[s]{=}0$}
      \STATE $L \leftarrow L \cup \{s\}$
    \ENDFOR
  \ENDFOR
  \IF{$L = \emptyset$} \STATE \textbf{break} \ENDIF
  \STATE commit each $n\in L$ to $D$ (and decrement successors' $r$)
  \STATE $L_{2q} \leftarrow \{n\in L : |\mathrm{qargs}(n)|{=}2\}$
  \STATE $L_\mathrm{prio} \leftarrow \{n\in L_{2q} : Q_F \cap \mathrm{qargs}(n)\neq\emptyset\}$
  \STATE append $L_\mathrm{prio}$ then $L_{2q}\!\setminus\!L_\mathrm{prio}$ to $E$ (truncate at $|E|_\mathrm{max}$)
  \STATE current $\leftarrow L$
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $E$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The construction has two important properties.  First, the BFS
expansion respects DAG dependencies: a gate is admitted to $E$ only
when all its predecessors are committed.  This avoids a subtle
correctness issue with naive wire-walks (following
\texttt{quantum\_successors} along a single qubit wire), which can
admit a second-pass gate before its first-pass predecessor has been
executed in circuits like W-state where wires are visited twice.
Second, within each BFS layer, the front-priority sub-rule biases the
lookahead toward gates that the current teleport candidate is most
likely to help, restoring the contribution to $\Delta_E$ that
topological order would have lost.

The construction adds no new hyperparameter and runs in
$O(|W|+|E|_\mathrm{max})$ time, the same asymptotic order as the
topological walk it replaces.  The empirical contribution of the BFS
construction over the topological one is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

% ── (iii) Parameter sensitivity ──────────────────────────────────────
\paragraph{Parameter sensitivity.}
We sweep each continuous hyperparameter one-at-a-time while holding all
others at their defaults, reporting gmean EPR on the 25q and 64q suites.
The key findings are as follows.

\textbf{Extended-set weight $w_e$.}
Sweeping $w_e \in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ reveals a
strongly asymmetric landscape: values above $0.5$ are catastrophic
(gmEPR $>77$ at $w_e{=}2.0$ on 25q) because the lookahead term
dominates and the scorer ignores immediate preparation costs.  Values
in $[0.1, 0.5]$ form a flat bowl with a minimum near $w_e{=}0.25$,
which reduces gmEPR by $5.6\%$, $4.0\%$, and $6.2\%$ over the former
default of $0.5$ on 25q, 36q, and 64q respectively.  We adopt
$w_e{=}0.25$ as the new default.

\textbf{Capacity penalty $c_p$.}
The penalty ramps steeply below $c_p{=}15$ (gmEPR $=40.7$ at $c_p{=}0$
vs.\ $23.8$ at $c_p{=}15$) and is flat above it: values $15$--$60$ all
produce gmEPR within $2\%$ of each other.  The default of $15$ sits at
the knee of this curve, where the penalty is strong enough to deter
overloaded-core teleports without over-penalising legitimate moves.

\textbf{Lookahead window size.}
Sweeping $|\mathit{size}| \in \{5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 50\}$ shows that
sizes $15$--$50$ all perform within $5\%$ of the optimum on both suites;
only $|\mathit{size}|{=}5$ is clearly inferior ($+7.6\%$ on 25q, $+5.0\%$
on 64q).  The default of $20$ is at or near the minimum on both suites,
and increasing it beyond $20$ provides no benefit because BFS-layer gates
beyond that window are already heavily discounted by the decay factor.

\textbf{Lookahead decay $\gamma$.}
On 25q, decay values $\gamma \in [0.7, 0.95]$ form a flat region within
$2\%$ of the optimum at $\gamma{=}0.9$.  Flat weighting ($\gamma{=}1.0$)
degrades 64q gmEPR by $7\%$, confirming that exponential discounting of
deep BFS layers is a meaningful design choice rather than a cosmetic
parameter.  Aggressive decay ($\gamma{=}0.5$) collapses the scorer
toward a single-layer lookahead and increases EPR on both suites.

\textbf{Congestion-relief bonus.}
On 25q the relief mechanism rarely activates and gmEPR is flat across
the entire swept range $[0.5, 60]$.  On 64q, values above $4.0$ all
produce identical results, confirming the bonus is already strong enough
to win against regular candidates whenever the congestion trigger fires.

\textbf{Inter-core edge weight.}
The architecture graph assigns weight $w_{\mathrm{ic}}{=}10$ to
inter-core links, so that \texttt{phys\_dist} penalises cross-core
gates by roughly the same factor as \texttt{cost\_teleport}.  Sweeping
$w_{\mathrm{ic}} \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 50\}$ on the
25q suite shows that $w_{\mathrm{ic}}{=}10$ minimises both EPR and SWAP
simultaneously, and that values in $[5, 15]$ are within $2\%$ of
optimal.  Very high weights ($w_{\mathrm{ic}}{=}50$) break the scorer:
the \texttt{ae} circuit EPR rises from $24$ to $130$ because the
lookahead over-values resolving inter-core gates and ignores preparation
cost.

\subsection{Initial Mapping}
\label{sec:initial_mapping}
Initial qubit placement significantly affects routing cost.  We adopt
Qiskit \texttt{SabreLayout} (corners removed, best of three seeds) paired
with a SABRE-style forward--backward--forward routing schedule as the
default configuration, and we characterise the sensitivity of this choice
by comparing three layout strategies across two pass schedules.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,labelindent=0pt,itemsep=2pt]
  \item[\textbf{TS}] \TeleSABRE{}'s built-in \texttt{optimize\_initial} layout.
  \item[\textbf{Rand}] Random qubit assignment, best of three trials.
  \item[\textbf{SL}] Qiskit \texttt{SabreLayout} on the architecture graph
    with the four lowest-degree corner nodes removed, best of three seeds
    (the default used throughout this paper).
\end{description}

\paragraph{Layout passes.}
Each routing pass feeds its output layout into the next, exploiting a
warm-start effect.  The first pass is a \emph{cold-start}: qubits begin
in their assigned initial positions, which may be far from the physical
slots where they first interact, so the router pays a heavy upfront
migration cost in the early layers.
The output layout $\mathcal{L}_1$ reflects the \emph{steady-state}
distribution — roughly, where qubits naturally reside after the circuit's
demand has pulled them into position.  A second pass starts from
$\mathcal{L}_1$, so qubits are already near their active zones and the
cold-start overhead is eliminated.  $\mathcal{L}_2 \approx \mathcal{L}_1$,
which is why passes $3$ and $4$ trigger the early-stopping condition and
yield no further improvement.
Table~\ref{tab:passes} shows that pass $1\to2$ is the only step that yields
meaningful improvement ($-8.9\%$ EPR and $-19\%$ SWAP on 25q;
\texttt{qnn} on 64q improves from $608$ to $517$ EPR).
Two passes therefore suffice; we default to the SABRE-style
forward--backward--forward schedule, where the backward pass over the
reversed DAG provides an additional layout-refinement benefit (detailed
below).

\paragraph*{SabreLayout in the distributed setting.}
SabreLayout~\cite{Li2019} is Qiskit's standard placement pass: it
iterates forward and backward through the circuit with the SABRE SWAP
heuristic, using the induced qubit migrations to refine the placement,
and returns the layout that minimises the resulting SWAP overhead.
Applied directly to the full architecture graph $G_\arch$ (intra-core
grid edges plus inter-core links), it produces a data-placement; no
special awareness of teleportation is required.

The \emph{corner-removal} adaptation ensures every core retains at
least one free qubit slot after placement.  SabreLayout fills cores
greedily: when minimising SWAP cost over $G_\arch$ it tends to pack
logical qubits densely into each core until capacity is reached.
A fully packed core has no parking space for intra-core SWAP sequences,
which stalls the router.  Removing a small number of physical qubits
from the coupling map passed to SabreLayout reduces each affected
core's effective capacity by one, guaranteeing slack.

The choice of \emph{which} qubits to remove matters: ideally the
excluded slots should not be far from a communication port, since they
will serve as temporary parking during SWAP chains.  In both the B-grid
and H-grid architectures used in this paper the global corner qubits
are the furthest from any inter-core link, making them the least useful
for routing and the best candidates for exclusion.  The excluded
corner slots remain available to \dSABRE{}'s router as free physical
qubits for temporary parking.  Three random seeds are tried and the
layout with the lowest EPR after full routing is kept.

by EPR count is reported).

Table~\ref{tab:layout} reveals several insights.
First, \emph{SL\,+\,sabre} is the strongest combination on 25q ($15.2$ gmean
EPR, $-32\%$ relative to the TS\,+\,fwd$\times$2 default and $-41\%$ relative
to \TeleSABRE{}), and competitive on 64q ($96.6$, $-12\%$ vs.\ default).
Crucially, this combination requires no \TeleSABRE{} run — SabreLayout is a
standard Qiskit pass.

Second, the SABRE backward pass consistently benefits all layouts tested.
Pairing the TS layout with the sabre schedule cuts 25q gmean from $22.5$ to
$19.3$ ($-14\%$) and 64q from $110.3$ to $98.4$ ($-11\%$).
Notably, \texttt{qnn} on 64q shows no regression under the SABRE backward
pass with SL layout ($521$ EPR for both schedules, matching the default),
confirming that pass-schedule effects on \texttt{qnn} are layout-dependent.

\textit{Validity under teleportation asymmetry.}
The primitive $\mathrm{teleport}(u,p_1,p_2)$ requires the logical qubit
to reside on physical qubit $u$ adjacent to comm port $p_1$; the reverse
move $\mathrm{teleport}(v,p_2,p_1)$ requires adjacency to $p_2$.  Both
primitives consume one EPR pair on the same inter-core link — the link is
\emph{bidirectional}.  When the backward pass routes the reversed DAG, a
gate that originally needed a qubit to migrate from core $A$ to core $B$
now appears, in reversed order, as needing motion from $B$ to $A$.  The
router handles this identically: it inspects where the qubit currently
resides and issues whichever form of teleportation applies to that port.
No additional hardware support is required; asymmetry is a constraint on
\emph{who initiates} the teleportation, not on which direction the link
can carry state.

\textit{Why the backward pass improves the layout.}
After the forward pass, the output layout $\mathcal{L}_f$ places qubits
near the physical slots they naturally occupy in the circuit's \emph{latter}
half.  The backward pass, routing the reversed DAG from $\mathcal{L}_f$,
effectively asks: ``given where qubits end up, where should they start?''
Qubits migrate back toward positions that are useful for the circuit's
\emph{beginning}, producing a layout $\mathcal{L}_b$ that pre-positions
qubits near their early interaction partners.  The final forward pass from
$\mathcal{L}_b$ is therefore another warm-started pass (like pass~2 of the
two-forward scheme), but with the additional structural information from
the backward traversal.  This is the same mechanism that motivates the
original SABRE router's backward-pass layout~\cite{Li2019}; here it
extends naturally to distributed teleportation because the routing
decisions in each direction remain independent and valid.

These findings motivate adopting SL\,+\,sabre as the recommended configuration
when \TeleSABRE{}'s layout is unavailable, at the cost of one additional Qiskit
pass and the sabre reverse-pass overhead.

\subsection{Robustness and Scalability}
\label{sec:scalability}

We probe \dSABRE{}'s behaviour on three fronts: the hardest 64q circuit
on which \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge; the rare cases where the
deadlock-recovery cushion is decisive; and a large-circuit sweep at
100, 200, and 360 logical qubits that stress-tests both routers
substantially beyond the main evaluation range.

\textbf{Random: \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge.}
\TeleSABRE{}'s energy-based solver does not converge within its
configured iteration budget on Random for any of the three seeds we
tried.  \dSABRE{} completes the route under SabreLayout (corners removed,
best of 3 seeds) using SABRE-style routing passes (714 EPRs, 3814 SWAPs).
The topological-order variant (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) yields
783 EPRs / 4092 SWAPs --- both constructions complete, with BFS giving
$-8.8\%$ fewer EPRs.

\textbf{Where deadlock recovery matters.}
The recovery path
(Section~\ref{sec:recovery}) is dormant on every circuit in the 25q
and 36q suites, and on most 64q circuits.  It is decisive only on
the Random instance just discussed and on the 200q circuits below:
without checkpoint-rollback the router would abort.  This confirms
the recovery is a defensive cushion rather than a primary
optimisation lever.

and executed via a single distributed state, with the EPR cost equal
to the number of edges in the Steiner tree connecting the involved
cores — one EPR per network hop, amortised over all gates in the bundle.
\dSABRE{} uses \emph{qubit teleportation}: each inter-core qubit
movement costs one EPR per hop, regardless of how many gates follow.
These two quantities are not directly comparable; circuits with long
runs of inter-core gates on the same qubit benefit disproportionately
from gate-teleportation amortisation.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Multi-core qubit mapping and routing.}
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is our direct baseline: a
\SABRE{}-style router for multi-core processors with teledata and
telegate primitives, scored via Dijkstra on a contracted
communication graph.  HQA~\cite{escofet2023hqa} addresses the
upstream initial-layout problem via Hungarian matching and is widely
used as a layout baseline.  TB-OLSQ2~\cite{lin2023scalable}
formulates multi-core routing as a constraint program and provides
near-optimal but non-scalable bounds.  Earlier multi-core SABRE
extensions appear in
Andres-Martinez and Heunen~\cite{andres2019automated} (hypergraph
partitioning), Ferrari et al.~\cite{ferrari2021compiler} (compiler
design for DQC), and Baker et al.~\cite{baker2020time} (time-optimal
scheduling).  A recent reinforcement-learning approach by Promponas
et al.~\cite{promponas2024rl} formulates the compiler as an MDP and
solves it with DDQN.  Architecture-level
work~\cite{suance2025decentralized,palesi2024qcomm} studies the
network and timing of inter-core communication; these are
orthogonal to routing heuristics and could be combined with
\dSABRE{}.

\textbf{Program-level burst extraction.}
AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm} performs an IR-level pass that
identifies sequences of pending non-local gates on the same qubit,
exploits gate commutativity to reorder them, and amortises a single
teleportation across the whole sequence by switching the underlying
primitive (e.g.\ from teledata to a fixed-amount pre-distributed
entanglement protocol).  AutoComm reports up to $\sim 75\%$ reductions
in non-local gate count.  AutoComm operates at the IR level before
routing; it is therefore complementary to \dSABRE{} and natural
to combine in a future-work direction (Section~\ref{sec:future}).
We emphasise that the front-priority sub-rule of our BFS-layer
extended set is \emph{not} a burst-extraction mechanism: it does not
modify the circuit, does not test commutativity, and does not change
the set of teleportation primitives applied.

\textbf{Single-QPU SABRE family.}
\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} introduced the front+extended-set
scoring framework that we and \TeleSABRE{} both build on.
Itoko et al.~\cite{itoko2020optimization} and
TKET~\cite{sivarajah2020tket} use commutativity for gate cancellation
and depth reduction inside a single QPU.  Babu et
al.~\cite{babu2025rtg} use auxiliary-qubit gate teleportation on a
heavy-hex IBM Eagle processor, achieving 10--25\% depth reduction
versus standard SABRE; the setting is single-QPU and not
directly comparable to multi-core routing.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{Combining \dSABRE{} with AutoComm-style burst extraction.}
\dSABRE{} is a routing-time heuristic: it works on a fixed circuit
DAG and scores moves locally.  AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm}
operates earlier in the toolchain and uses gate commutativity to
\emph{reorder} non-local gates so that long sequences sharing a
common qubit can be amortised by a single teleportation.  The two
approaches operate at different layers and should compose well: an
AutoComm pre-pass would expose more wire-aligned gate sequences, and
\dSABRE{}'s BFS extended set would let the teleport score capture
their full benefit.  Quantifying the joint improvement is left to
future work.

\textbf{Telegate primitive.}
\dSABRE{} currently uses only teledata moves (qubit transfer).
\TeleSABRE{} also supports telegate (remote CX execution via the
cat-entangler protocol), which can be cheaper for circuits with high
CX density.  Adding telegate candidates to \dSABRE{}'s scoring loop
is a straightforward extension and would close the gap on QNN-style
high-density circuits where \TeleSABRE{} retains a small advantage at
$c_\mathrm{tele}{\le}10$.

\textbf{Other directions.}
(i)~A C++ port of \dSABRE{} to close the compile-time gap with
\TeleSABRE{}.
(ii)~Noise-aware cost models where EPR fidelity varies per link.
(iii)~Adaptive lookahead window sized by detected wire-burst length.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \dSABRE{}, a SABRE-style router for multi-core
distributed quantum processors that integrates intra-core SWAP
scoring and inter-core teleport candidate scoring into a single
loop, with proactive congestion relief and a BFS-layer extended-set
construction as the default lookahead.  Across 18 MQT-Bench circuits
on three architectures (B-grid 25q/36q; H-grid 64q), \dSABRE{}
reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption over the state-of-the-art
\TeleSABRE{} by $41.1\%$, $44.1\%$, and $43.9\%$ respectively, and
over the gate-teleportation-based pytket-dqc by $68.4\%$, $29.4\%$,
and $15.7\%$, using only standard Qiskit SabreLayout for initial
placement; \dSABRE{} also completes a 64-qubit Random circuit on which
\TeleSABRE{} fails to converge.  A large-circuit QFT sweep at 100--360 qubits confirms that \dSABRE{}
wins at 100q ($-21\%$ EPR vs.\ \TeleSABRE{}, $-63\%$ vs.\
\texttt{pytket-dqc}), remains the only router that completes the 200q
circuit (300 physical qubits) within budget, and wins at 360q
($-48\%$ vs.\ \TeleSABRE{}, $-14\%$ vs.\ \texttt{pytket-dqc}).
Under a combined cost model, the EPR savings translate to substantial
reductions across the entire practically relevant range of
$c_\mathrm{tele}{:}c_\mathrm{swap}$ ratios, with the largest savings
on photonic-link hardware where EPR cost dominates.  Code,
benchmarks, and full per-circuit results accompany the paper.

\section{Comparison with pytket-dqc}
\label{app:pytketdqc}

\textbf{Background.}
\texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{pytketdqc2024} is a Python library from
Quantinuum that distributes circuits over multiple QPUs using
\emph{gate teleportation} (detached gates): a pre-shared e-bit allows
a non-local controlled-unitary to be executed without moving the
qubit, and consecutive non-local gates on the same wire can share the
same e-bit (multi-gate amortisation).  This contrasts with
\dSABRE{}'s \emph{state teleportation}, which physically moves a
qubit into a target core so that all subsequent gates on it run
locally.  Both primitives consume Bell pairs, but the reported counts
are not directly comparable: \texttt{pytket-dqc} charges one e-bit per
edge in the Steiner tree connecting all servers in a hyperedge, with
multiple consecutive non-local gates on the same wire amortised into a
single hyperedge.  \dSABRE{} charges one EPR per qubit-teleportation
hop regardless of how many gates follow (see
Section~\ref{sec:scalability}).  \texttt{pytket-dqc} assigns qubits to
QPUs \emph{statically} via KaHyPar hypergraph partitioning and does not
model intra-QPU SWAP routing; its output is an e-bit count only.

\textbf{Implementation notes.}
We install \texttt{pytket-dqc} v0.0.1 from source and
\texttt{kahypar} 1.3.5.  Two changes were required to run our
benchmarks:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{KaHyPar weight-0 patch.}  \texttt{pytket-dqc} represents
  the circuit as a hypergraph whose qubit vertices carry weight~1 and
  gate vertices weight~0.  KaHyPar~$\geq$1.3.5 rejects zero-weight
  vertices.  We patched the allocator source file to
  assign weight~1 to all vertices and increase each server's target
  block size by $\lceil n_\mathrm{gates}/k \rceil$, so the capacity
  constraint still effectively counts only qubit vertices.

\item \emph{64-qubit QASM width.}  The pytket QASM loader defaults to
  a 32-bit classical register maximum; the 64-qubit benchmarks carry
  a 64-bit measurement register.  We pass \texttt{maxwidth=128} to
  \texttt{circuit\_from\_qasm}.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
All other \texttt{pytket-dqc} internals are unmodified.  We use the
\texttt{HypergraphPartitioning} allocator (KaHyPar connectivity
metric), best of 5 random seeds.  The QPU topology matches the core
graph of each architecture: a $2{\times}2$ grid (4 QPUs) for the
25q/36q B-grid and a $2{\times}3$ grid (6 QPUs) for the 64q H-grid.

Tables~\ref{tab:main25}--\ref{tab:main64} of the main text.  Here we
report the shared-mapping comparison, which isolates routing quality
from layout quality.

\textbf{Discussion.}
Starting from an identical qubit-to-core partition, \dSABRE{} reduces
EPR consumption over pytket-dqc by a geometric mean of $46\%$ on the
25q suite and $30\%$ on the 64q suite.
Bear in mind the counting asymmetry noted above: \texttt{pytket-dqc}
amortises consecutive non-local gates into a single hyperedge, so its
e-bit advantage is most pronounced on circuits with long runs of
gates on the same cross-core wire.
\dSABRE{}'s EPR advantage is largest for circuits with high CX density
(ae, qft, qnn, random), where state teleportation moves a qubit once
and all subsequent gates run locally regardless of their number.
The 36q suite reverses the picture (pytket-dqc wins by $25\%$):
\texttt{dj}, \texttt{vqe\_su2}, and \texttt{wstate} have sparse or
highly structured interaction graphs whose KaHyPar partition already
achieves near-zero cross-core communication; \dSABRE{}'s dynamic
teleport decisions then disrupt this near-optimal static assignment.

\textbf{Compile time.}
pytket-dqc's wall time is dominated by KaHyPar and scales with the
number of hyperedges (2-qubit gates): from $<0.1$\,s on tiny circuits
to $136$\,s on qnn-64q (5 seeds).  \dSABRE{}'s routing time from a
fixed initial layout is comparable for large circuits (151\,s for
qnn-64q) and negligible ($<0.1$\,s) for small ones.  For circuits
with few 2-qubit gates, pytket-dqc's KaHyPar overhead exceeds
\dSABRE{}'s routing time by one to two orders of magnitude.
