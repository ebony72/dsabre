\begin{abstract}
\rev{A distributed quantum computer (DQC) runs a circuit across several
small cores linked by entanglement.  Executing a gate whose operands sit
in different cores consumes Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR) pairs, orders
of magnitude costlier than a local gate and so the quantity a
distributed router must minimise.  On a single chip that role falls to
\SABRE{}, the state of the art for monolithic layout and routing and
Qiskit's default, which scores a
SWAP by the front-layer distance it saves plus a bounded lookahead.
We present \dSABRE{}, which carries that objective across cores,
scoring each inter-core move by one rule: the resource it spends, plus
the capacity it consumes, plus the progress it buys in one
\emph{routing potential}---front-layer distance plus a lookahead discounted by depth
in the circuit's directed acyclic graph (DAG).  Its added terms are not
independent heuristics but the two respects in which a teleport is
unlike a SWAP: it fires only at a communication port, and it consumes a
free slot in the core it lands in.
Across 21 Munich Quantum Toolkit (MQT) Bench circuits at 25--64 logical
qubits \dSABRE{} cuts geometric-mean EPR consumption by $49$--$62\%$
against \TeleSABRE{}, and routes all four of this paper's 27 circuits on
which \TeleSABRE{} does not converge---including a 360-qubit QFT on
twenty cores, compiled in $48$\,s; a
capacity condition, checked once before routing, further guarantees
\dSABRE{} itself never aborts.}  Code
is at \url{https://github.com/ebony72/dsabre}.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
distributed quantum computing, circuit routing, qubit teleportation,
SABRE, EPR minimisation, qubit mapping, lookahead heuristic

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The near-term path to fault-tolerant quantum computing requires processors with thousands of high-fidelity qubits.
No single monolithic chip can economically deliver this scale today.
Instead, the field is converging on \emph{distributed quantum computers}
(DQC)~\cite{cuomo2020towards}: multiple
small quantum processing units (QPUs, also called cores) interconnected by photonic or microwave
links that support remote entanglement generation.

\rev{Executing a quantum circuit on a DQC requires a \emph{distributed
compilation} pipeline that, like its monolithic counterpart, decomposes
into three problem classes~\cite{escofet2024review}.
\emph{(i) Qubit allocation} statically assigns logical qubits to cores,
typically by hypergraph
partitioning~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024}.
\emph{(ii) Communication-primitive selection} decides how each non-local
two-qubit gate is realised---by bringing both qubits to the same core, or
by executing the gate remotely~\cite{bennett1993teleporting,eisert2000optimal}
(Section~\ref{sec:teleportation} describes both primitives and their
costs).  \emph{(iii) Routing and scheduling} orders the resulting
intra-core SWAPs, teleports, and entanglement-generation requests in
time, subject to finite entanglement rates and to the capacity of the
\emph{communication ports}---the physical qubits that terminate an
inter-core link, of which each core has
finitely many~\cite{ferrari2021compiler,baker2020time}.
All three are judged in one currency: every primitive consumes EPR
pairs, whose generation is slow, noisy and rate-limited in current
hardware~\cite{stephenson2020high,daiss2021quantum}---orders of
magnitude costlier than a local gate---so EPR consumption is the
dominant objective of distributed compilation, and the one this paper
minimises.}

\rev{\textbf{Two families.}
Compilers that solve (i)--(iii) differ less in their optimisation
machinery than in what they assume the machine can do.  The first
family reduces distribution to \emph{(hyper)graph partitioning},
cutting a hypergraph whose hyperedges collect the gates that can share
one entangled pair, so that the cut weight is the number of EPR pairs
consumed~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024}.  The appeal is a
global, one-shot optimisation backed by mature solvers; the cost is an
idealised machine, in which intra-core routing is free, the number of
links joining two cores goes unrecorded, per-core communication memory
is effectively unbounded, and an entangled pair survives its whole
hyperedge.  These are claims about the machine rather than bookkeeping
conventions, and they set the counts: giving the same partitioner this
device's own link and slot budgets is enough to reverse which tool wins
at 64 qubits (Section~\ref{sec:pytket}).}

\rev{The second family gives up the global view and decides during
routing, pricing those same constraints into each candidate move
instead of idealising them away.  Its monolithic ancestor is
\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling}, Qiskit's default layout and routing
pass (Section~\ref{sec:routing}): at each step it inserts the SWAP that
most reduces the distance between the operands of the gates waiting to
execute, with a discounted look at the gates behind them.  Two recent
routers carry that score across cores.
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} prices
an inter-core move by a Dijkstra computation on a contracted
communication graph; DMapS~\cite{luo2025dmaps} pairs a hypergraph
partitioner with a \SABRE{}-derived router whose only cross-core
primitive is a two-EPR \emph{remote SWAP}.}

\rev{What neither prices is the free capacity of the core a move lands
in.  A remote SWAP exchanges two qubits and leaves every core's
occupancy unchanged, so DMapS never faces the question---and pays two
EPR pairs for each crossing.  \TeleSABRE{} pays one, and meets the
resulting pressure defensively: a fixed penalty once a core is already
nearly full, and a safety valve that drops most of the lookahead once
routing stalls, rather than a signal that grades continuously with how
much slack a landing core has left.  Dense or large instances therefore
either burn additional EPR pairs or fail to converge.}

\rev{The defect is not specific to one penalty: porting a SWAP-based
router to a DQC is not a drop-in substitution of teleport for SWAP.  A
SWAP is symmetric and leaves the chip's occupied-slot count unchanged.
A teleport is one-directional; it fires only at a communication port,
so it is ready only once its qubit has been walked there and whatever
occupies the port has been evicted; and where it lands, the destination
core is left more crowded than before.}

\rev{This paper presents \dSABRE{}, whose answer is a single rule
rather than a collection of fixes: one score for every inter-core move,
adding to \SABRE{}'s distance-plus-lookahead objective exactly two
terms, one per departure---the staging that \emph{availability}
demands, and the \emph{capacity} the move consumes
(Section~\ref{sec:principle}).  Everything else in
Section~\ref{sec:dsabre} is one of those two terms, the lookahead
construction they are scored against (Section~\ref{sec:bfsext}), or the
recovery for the one state in which a distance objective stops being a
usable surrogate---the cores an action needs are saturated
(Section~\ref{sec:relief}).  \dSABRE{} moves qubits by teledata alone,
which is what makes its counts unconditional: a pair is consumed by the
teleport that creates it, so no score models entanglement lifetime and
every count we report holds under any bound on it
(Section~\ref{sec:lifetime}).}
%Telegate candidates are a genuine extension rather than a missing feature, for a reason Section~\ref{sec:dsabre} gives and Section~\ref{sec:future} takes up.

\textbf{Contributions.}

\begin{itemize}

\item \rev{\textbf{A capacity-aware macro-action teleportation score.}
\dSABRE{} scores the whole macro-action---the staging SWAPs, any
eviction, and the teleport itself---as one move, carrying \SABRE{}'s
front-layer distance change and depth-discounted lookahead
into the inter-core scorer and adding one term per
departure: a staging cost for availability, a
\emph{core-capacity penalty} for capacity
(Section~\ref{sec:tele}).  Capacity is the largest ablation
effect: removing both capacity mechanisms costs $+87.8\%$
geometric-mean EPR and aborts two circuits (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).}

\item \rev{\textbf{A capacity condition with a termination-with-success
guarantee.}  Five preconditions, checked before routing starts so that
a violation is reported rather than surfacing as a mid-route abort,
make \dSABRE{} provably route every gate rather than merely terminate
(Section~\ref{sec:recovery}).  The same argument prices the recovery
they license---$O(KD_K)$ EPR pairs per remote gate, \dSABRE{}'s first
non-vacuous worst-case bound---at a measured cost of $0.2\%$ EPR pairs
in geometric mean.}

\item \textbf{Comprehensive empirical validation.}
Across \rev{Munich Quantum Toolkit (MQT)} Bench suites at 25, 36, and 64
qubits, \dSABRE{} reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption by
\rev{$50.8\%$, $62.0\%$, and $49.1\%$} over
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}, \rev{the one baseline sharing
its cost model, architecture and SWAP accounting.  The margin survives
heavy-hex cores on a ring and on a star, a shared initial allocation,
and 360 logical qubits on twenty cores, at near-linear compile time
($N^{1.05}$)
(Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:scalability}).}

\item \rev{\textbf{A controlled comparison across EPR cost models.}
\texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{pytketdqc2024} distributes by
hypergraph partitioning and gate teleportation, so its counts share our
unit but not our work per unit.  Rebuilding its network to this
device's own links and slots moves the three suite margins to
$-44.4\%$, $-0.9\%$ and $-1.7\%$, $13$ of the $21$ corrected
distributions exceed the device's capacity, and under a three-gate
entanglement lifetime the narrow 64-qubit margin widens to a $46.5\%$
\dSABRE{} win (Section~\ref{sec:pytket}).
}
\end{itemize}

\rev{
Three minor contributions support that score.  A teleport is scored
only when no front-layer gate has both operands in one core, so local
work is drained before an EPR pair is spent and the two
scorers---one per-core, one chip-wide---never have to be made
commensurable (Section~\ref{sec:principle}); \TeleSABRE{}, which pools
both in a single arg\,min, fires $59.5\%$ of its 64-qubit communication
operations while a same-core front gate is still waiting---moments at
which \dSABRE{} spends a local SWAP instead
(Section~\ref{sec:related}).  The inter-core extended set is built BFS
layer by layer over the remaining DAG, so that a gate's depth in the
circuit is what discounts its contribution to the lookahead; replacing
it with a topological-order set costs $12.4\%$ geometric-mean EPR at 64
qubits (Sections~\ref{sec:bfsext},~\ref{sec:ablation}).  Finally, the
initial layout supplies the free slots both capacity mechanisms depend
on: stock Qiskit \texttt{SabreLayout}, adapted to reserve them in
\emph{every} core by a rule reading only vertex degree and remoteness,
so it transfers unchanged to non-grid cores
(Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping}).
}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Quantum Circuits}
\label{sec:qcircuits}

In this paper, a quantum circuit acts on $n$ \emph{qubits} through a sequence of \emph{gates}:
\emph{one-qubit (1Q) gates} (Hadamard, rotations, \ldots) and \emph{two-qubit
(2Q) gates}, of which the \emph{CX} (CNOT) gate is the standard primitive.
\rev{The two classes are together universal---CX and the single-qubit
unitaries decompose every $n$-qubit unitary
exactly~\cite{barenco1995elementary}---and a finite basis such as
$\{\mathrm{CX},H,T\}$ suffices approximately; it is what the benchmark
circuits of Section~\ref{sec:setup} are transpiled into.}
The \emph{SWAP} gate exchanges the states of two adjacent qubits; it decomposes
into three CX gates and is what routing algorithms insert to migrate logical
qubit states across the hardware.

A circuit is represented as a \emph{directed acyclic graph} (DAG) in which each
node is a gate and each directed edge records a data dependency (qubit produced
by gate $g_1$ consumed by gate $g_2$).
For every qubit $q$, each gate acting on $q$ is connected to the
\emph{next} gate acting on $q$: the second cannot begin until the first
has written its output on $q$.
Figure~\ref{fig:dag-example} shows a small example, where \emph{circuit depth} is the length of the longest path through the DAG.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \input{fig/dag_example}
  \caption{DAG for a three-gate circuit on qubits $q_1,q_2,q_3$.
    $\mathrm{CX}(q_1,q_2)$ and $\mathrm{H}(q_3)$ share no qubit and have
    no incoming edges, so both appear in the initial front layer $F=\{g_1,g_2\}$
    and may execute in parallel.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Quantum Teleportation}
\label{sec:teleportation}

\emph{Quantum teleportation}~\cite{bennett1993teleporting} transfers the
state of a qubit from a sender to a receiver---in the DQC setting, from
one core to another over an inter-core link---using a pre-shared
\emph{EPR pair} (a maximally entangled two-qubit state) and two
classical bits.  The source qubit's state is destroyed at the sender
and reconstructed at the receiver; no physical qubit travels across
the link.  Each EPR pair is consumed upon use, so the \emph{EPR count}
is the primary inter-core routing cost in distributed quantum
compilation.  Throughout this paper, ``EPR'' as a unit (e.g.\ ``$10$
EPRs'') denotes EPR-pair count; ``EPR pair'' is used when emphasising
the physical entangled state itself.

Two inter-core teleportation variants arise in DQC compilation.
\emph{Teledata} moves a logical qubit to a new core (one EPR pair consumed),
after which the qubit participates in local gates there.
\emph{Telegate}~\cite{eisert2000optimal} executes a non-local 2Q gate directly
across two cores via a shared EPR pair, without physically relocating either
qubit.
\rev{Which is cheaper is circuit-dependent---teledata amortises over a
qubit's residence in a core, telegate over a group of non-local gates
on a shared control bundled onto one entangled state---but the two make
different demands on the hardware.  A teledata pair is consumed by the
teleport that creates it, whereas an amortised telegate needs its link
to stay entangled, and to occupy a link qubit, for the span of the gate
group it serves.  Gate-teleportation counts are therefore contingent on
entanglement lifetime and on how many pairs a core can hold at once;
Section~\ref{sec:pytket} measures what those two assumptions are worth.}

\subsection{Distributed Quantum Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{fig/architectures}}
  \caption{DQC architectures used in this paper (B-grid and H-grid
    families introduced by \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}).
    Small grey circles are qubits; teal circles are inter-core communication

    Each core is a $4{\times}4$ grid.
    (a)~$2{\times}2$ B-grid.
    (b)~$2{\times}3$ H-grid.}
  \label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

We model a DQC architecture $\arch$ abstractly as a weighted graph
$G_\arch=(V,E,w)$ whose vertices are physical qubits.
The vertex set is partitioned into $K$ \emph{cores} $V = V_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_K$;
intra-core edges $E_{\mathrm{intra}}$ describe in-core connectivity and carry
weight~$1$, and inter-core edges $E_{\mathrm{inter}}$ connect designated
\emph{communication ports} on neighbouring cores and carry a much larger
weight $w_{\mathrm{link}} \gg 1$ reflecting the cost of an EPR pair.
A communication port is a physical qubit like any other and may also
hold a logical (data) qubit between teleportations; the
``communication'' label only indicates that the port is one end of an
inter-core EPR link, not that the slot is reserved.
\dSABRE{} treats $G_\arch$ as a black box and only queries it through three
distance tables: physical $\dphys$, intra-core $\dintra$, and core-graph
$\dcore$.
The router therefore extends to \emph{any} DQC topology --- arbitrary
intra-core graphs, mixed core sizes, sparse inter-core meshes, hierarchical
trees of clusters, etc. --- by simply supplying these tables.

For experimental simplicity our reference implementation instantiates
$G_\arch$ as a \emph{grid-of-grids}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}: each core
is an $m\!\times\!m$ 2D nearest-neighbour grid, and the cores themselves
form an $r\!\times\!s$ super-grid joined by a small set of inter-core
links between specific boundary qubits.  
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows two
concrete topologies used in our experiments.



\subsubsection{Hierarchical distance precomputation}
Because intra-core and inter-core costs decouple, $\dphys$ need never be
materialised as a dense all-pairs table: it composes from the two cheap
ones.  \rev{Every $p\to q$ path leaves $p$'s core through some
communication port and enters $q$'s through another, so with $\dport$ a
port-to-port table over the $O(K)$ ports, and $\Pi_i$ the ports of core
$i$, $ \dphys(p,q)$ is calculated exactly as 
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\pi\in\Pi_i,\ \pi'\in\Pi_j}
    \dintra(p,\pi)+\dport(\pi,\pi')+\dintra(\pi',q).
  \label{eq:dphys}
\end{equation} It cuts
precomputation from $O((KM)^2\log(KM))$ to $O(KM^2+K^2\log K)$ and
resident space from $\Theta(P^2)$ to $O(KM^2+K^2)$, and queries are
memoised, so routing sees $O(1)$ lookups after the first.  This is the
only architecture-dependent setup \dSABRE{} performs.}

\subsection{Qubit Routing and \SABRE{}}
\label{sec:routing}

\rev{Let $C$ be a circuit DAG on $n$ \emph{logical qubits}
$\logi{1},\ldots,\logi{n}$, to be run on an architecture with
$P=|V|\ge n$ physical qubits.  An \emph{initial layout} is an injection
$\ell:\logi{i}\!\mapsto\!\phys{\sigma(i)}$ placing them on distinct
physical slots, where $\sigma$ is a permutation on $[P]=\{0,1,\ldots,P-1\}$.}  A 2Q gate can execute only when
its two operands occupy adjacent vertices of the \emph{coupling graph}
($G_\arch$ in the distributed case), so \emph{routing} inserts SWAP and
teleportation operations---as few as possible---to make every gate
executable in turn.  Cost model: each local SWAP costs
$c_{\mathrm{swap}}$; each inter-core teleport costs $c_{\mathrm{tele}}$
(one EPR pair).

\rev{\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} is the state of the art for this
problem on a single chip: Qiskit~\cite{qiskit2024} selects it by
default for both layout and routing, and successive later work has
refined its scoring form~\cite{zou2024lightsabrelightweightenhancedsabre}, on single chips
and in the multi-core setting~\cite{russo2025telesabre,luo2025dmaps}
alike.  Building the distributed router on it is a deliberate choice.
A DQC compiler must handle circuits of the size the machine exists to
run, on hardware whose link counts, port capacities and core sizes are
all finite and all binding; a formulation that is globally optimal on
an idealised machine buys its elegance by assuming those constraints
away, and pays for it twice---in compile time, and in solutions the
device cannot execute (Section~\ref{sec:pytket}).  Extending a
heuristic that production toolchains already run keeps both costs
bounded and confines the open question to a single one: what its
objective must become when one chip becomes several.}
\LightSABRE{}~\cite{zou2024lightsabrelightweightenhancedsabre}, the
refinement Qiskit ships, selects the SWAP minimising:
\begin{equation}
  H = \tfrac{1}{|F|}\!\sum_{g\in F}\!\Delta_F(g)
    + w\,\tfrac{1}{|E|}\!\sum_{g\in E}\!\Delta_E(g),
  \label{eq:sabre}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the front layer, $E$ the extended lookahead set, and
\rev{$\Delta(g) = d_{\mathrm{new}}^g - d_{\mathrm{old}}^g$} the change in
physical distance between the two qubits of gate $g$ caused by the
candidate SWAP \rev{(negative when the SWAP moves them closer)}; $d^g$ is
shortest-path distance on the coupling graph.
All extended-set gates contribute with equal weight; the $\tfrac{1}{|E|}$
factor normalises the lookahead term to the same scale as the front term.
\SABRE{} also couples its router with an initial-layout optimiser:
alternating forward and reverse-circuit passes, each seeded with the
previous pass's final layout, anneal towards a layout self-consistent
with the circuit's gate structure.

\section{dSABRE}
\label{sec:dsabre}

\dSABRE{} is a \SABRE{}-style routing algorithm for multi-core
processors.  \rev{Of the two communication primitives of
Section~\ref{sec:teleportation} it uses teledata alone, for three
reasons.  What a telegate does well---settling a group of non-local
gates for one EPR pair---teledata also does, since one relocation makes
\emph{every} later gate on that qubit local.  Realising telegate's
advantage therefore means deciding which gates to group, as
\texttt{pytket-dqc} does globally by hypergraph partitioning
(Section~\ref{sec:pytket}), and taking that decision \emph{online}---as
routing proceeds, from the front layer and a bounded lookahead rather
than from the whole circuit---would complicate the score; a single ungrouped telegate, by contrast, fits
Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre} directly---no qubit changes core, so
$c_{\mathrm{cap}}$ drops---but then saves a pair only where teledata's
amortisation would not have, so we expect little from it.  And grouping
is what makes a compiler's counts contingent on the device: the link
must stay entangled, and hold a link qubit, for the span of the group
(Section~\ref{sec:teleportation}), which
Section~\ref{sec:pytket} measures. }

%\rev{\dSABRE{} accepts \emph{any} legal initial layout---any injective assignment of logical qubits to physical slots that respects per-core capacity.  We propose a \SABRE{}-inspired layout with a per-core capacity reservation (Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping}), which is used as the default initial layout of \dSABRE{}.}

The core invariants and notation are: (i) the layout
$\ell:\logi{i}\!\mapsto\!\phys{\sigma(i)}$ of Section~\ref{sec:routing}
assigns logical to physical qubits and the inverse $\ell^{-1}$ records
which logical qubit (if any) currently occupies each physical slot; (ii) the working DAG~$W$ holds the
remaining gates (initially the full circuit) and shrinks as gates are
executed; (iii) the front layer~$F$ is the set of gates in $W$ with no
unexecuted predecessors, and the extended set~$E$ is a fixed-size
sliding lookahead beyond $F$.  Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects
every symbol used in this section.

\rev{The rest of the section is organised as follows.
Sections~\ref{sec:principle} and~\ref{sec:workflow} give the design as a
whole---the two actions the router chooses between, the quantities its
score trades off, and the loop that applies one action per iteration.
Sections~\ref{sec:intra}--\ref{sec:bfsext} then fill that score in: the
intra-core SWAP heuristic, the teleport candidates and what they cost,
and the lookahead set both scorers read.  Together they decide every
move on a circuit the greedy search can route.
Section~\ref{sec:recovery} treats the case it cannot---saturated cores
leaving no cheap legal move---where capacity becomes a hard constraint
rather than a preference and a provably terminating transaction retires
the blocked gate.  Sections~\ref{sec:initial_mapping}
and~\ref{sec:complexity} give the initial layout the loop starts from
and the cost of the whole procedure.}

\subsection{\rev{Scoring: Potential, Cost, and Capacity}}
\label{sec:principle}

\rev{\dSABRE{} has two actions, an intra-core SWAP and an inter-core
teleport, and a priority rule rather than a common score decides
between them: while any front-layer gate has both operands in one core
the router applies an intra-core SWAP (Section~\ref{sec:intra}); only
when none remains does it score teleport candidates
(Section~\ref{sec:tele}).  The two scorers are therefore never compared
against each other---one is per-core, the other chip-wide.}

\rev{The intra-core scorer is \SABRE{} restricted to a core, with the
depth discount of Eq.~\ref{Delta_E_Tilde}.  The teleport scorer needs
more care, because a teleport differs from a SWAP in two ways and each
difference adds one term.  \emph{(i)~Availability}: a SWAP is legal on
every edge of the coupling graph, whereas a teleport fires only at a
communication port, so a candidate must also carry the intra-core SWAPs
that bring its qubit there and any eviction needed to free the port.
We score that whole sequence as one \emph{macro-action}, so that a port
already occupied makes a candidate dearer rather than deleting it
(Section~\ref{sec:tele}).  \emph{(ii)~Capacity}: a
SWAP exchanges the contents of two slots and leaves the occupied set
unchanged, so capacity never binds on a monolithic chip; a teleport
moves a qubit into a different core and consumes one of its free slots,
so a candidate must be charged for the capacity it takes.}

\rev{Those two terms sit on an objective inherited from \SABRE{}.  Let
$d(g;\ell)$ be the weighted distance between the operands of a 2Q gate
$g$ on $G_\arch$ under layout $\ell$, minimal exactly when $g$ is
executable, and let the \emph{routing potential} $\Phi(\ell)$ be $d$
summed over the front layer plus a lookahead sum over $E$ in which each
gate is discounted.  $\Phi$ is a surrogate,
not the true cost-to-go and only its
difference is ever evaluated.  Writing $\Delta\Phi(a)$ for the change a
candidate $a$ induces---\emph{negative} when the move leaves the
waiting gates closer to executable---$\mathrm{cost}(a)$ for the resource it consumes and
$\mathrm{pen}(a)$ for the capacity constraint $\Phi$ omits, \dSABRE{}
greedily minimises}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:principle}
  \rev{s(a) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathrm{cost}(a)}_{\text{resource spent}}
       \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{pen}(a)}_{\text{capacity}}
       \;+\; \underbrace{\Delta\Phi(a)}_{\text{progress bought}} .}
\end{equation}
\rev{Every
candidate in an iteration spends exactly one EPR pair, so that cost is
constant across the arg\,min and does not appear.}

\rev{The capacity term is not a tie-breaker added to a distance heuristic:
it is \emph{graded} in the landing
core's free-slot count rather than switching on at a fixed occupancy.
A graded penalty can express preference but never guarantee
feasibility, so we also put (cf. Section~\ref{sec:recovery}) two hard constraints
behind it.  A \emph{legality floor} admits a teleport only if its
destination core holds at least $\phi_1{=}2$ free slots when it is
scored; and a \emph{feasibility}
condition on the instance, checked once before routing, is what makes
the router provably complete.  The score term is then left to choose
among already-legal moves. }

\subsection{Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} sketches the main routing loop.  Each
iteration runs four phases: \textbf{(P1)}~drain $F$ (execute 1Q gates
and any 2Q gate whose operands are already adjacent in the same core,
iterated to a fixed point); \textbf{(P2)}~partition the remaining
front into $F_\mathrm{intra}$ (both operands in one core) and
$F_\mathrm{inter}$ (operands in different cores); \textbf{(P3)}~if
$F_\mathrm{intra}\!\neq\!\emptyset$ apply one intra-core SWAP
(Section~\ref{sec:intra}), else score teleportation candidates over
$F_\mathrm{inter}$ \rev{and apply the cheapest} (Section~\ref{sec:tele});
\textbf{(P4)}~checkpoint $(\ell, W)$ if $|W|$ decreased, otherwise,
after $L_\mathrm{deadlock}$ stalled iterations, restore the checkpoint
and retire the stuck gate by \rev{the guaranteed transaction of
Section~\ref{sec:recovery}.  Two other states hand P3 straight to the
same transaction without waiting for $L_\mathrm{deadlock}$: an empty
candidate list, meaning no neighbouring core clears the legality floor,
and a candidate set every member of which is rejected at execution time
(Section~\ref{sec:tele}).  Neither is a stall a local SWAP could
resolve, so there is nothing to gain by stalling first.}

\subsection{Intra-Core SWAP Heuristic}
\label{sec:intra}

When the front contains a gate whose operands share a core but are not
adjacent, \dSABRE{} applies the standard \SABRE{} heuristic restricted
to that core.  Let
$F_\mathrm{c} = \{g\in F_\mathrm{intra} : \mathrm{core}(g)=c\}$ be the
front gates in core~$c$ and $E_\mathrm{c} = (g_0, g_1, \ldots)$ the
corresponding intra-core extended set.  \rev{$E_\mathrm{c}$ is the
extended set $E$ \emph{restricted} to core $c$---the gates of $E$ with
both operands resident there, carrying $E$'s own depths---so one
construction (Section~\ref{sec:bfsext}) feeds both scorers and
$\mathrm{dep}(g)$ means the same thing in each.  The submitted design
instead built a separate per-core set by its own traversal; the online
appendices compare the two.}
With $\mathrm{dep}(g_i)$ the BFS layer $g_i$ occupies in $E$, the
lookahead contribution is
\begin{equation}\label{Delta_E_Tilde}
\tilde{\Delta}_E^\mathrm{c} = \sum_{i=0}^{|E_\mathrm{c}|-1}
\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g_i)}(d_{\mathrm{new}}^{g_i} - d_{\mathrm{old}}^{g_i}),
\end{equation}
where $\gamma\in (0,1]$ is the lookahead decay and the distance terms
use the intra-core graph; the tilde marks the departure from
\SABRE{}'s flat extended-set weighting
(Section~\ref{sec:principle}).
For every neighbour pair $(u,v)$ within a core, the score
\begin{equation}
  H_\mathrm{intra} = \frac{\Delta_F^\mathrm{c}}{|F_\mathrm{c}|}
        + w_e\,\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_E^\mathrm{c}}{|E_\mathrm{c}|}
  \label{eq:intra}
\end{equation}
is \SABRE{}'s objective with that weighting; the lowest-scoring SWAP is
applied, ties broken by enumeration order.  Because the scorer sees one
core and $O(M)$ candidate pairs, per-core selection is trivially
parallel across active cores.

\subsection{Inter-Core Teleportation Scoring}
\label{sec:tele}

\rev{This is where the \emph{capacity-aware macro-action} of
Section~\ref{sec:principle} is instantiated: a candidate is the whole
sequence---staging SWAPs, evictions, teleport---and it is priced by
what that sequence costs plus what it takes from the core it lands in.}

\rev{\textbf{Operational definition.}  Executing a candidate is
deterministic given the state at the moment it is scored.  The
destination port $\pi_d$ need not be empty: an occupant is evicted to
the nearest free slot in $c_\mathrm{next}$ by the same rule applied to
the source port $\pi_s$.  The legality floor of
Section~\ref{sec:principle} leaves a candidate destination two free
slots when it is scored, so that eviction cannot fail.  A
fully-occupied \emph{source} core, by contrast, cannot teleport at
all---clearing $\pi_s$ needs a slot to displace its occupant into---so
one qubit is first teleported out of it, into whichever neighbouring
core has room.  That hop can land in $c_\mathrm{next}$ and spend one of
its two slots, so both cores are re-checked afterwards: a candidate
whose preconditions no longer hold when it executes is rolled back at
zero cost, and the next-cheapest is tried in its place---a safety net
the conditions of Section~\ref{sec:recovery} render provably
unnecessary, as the online appendices show.}

For each gate $g\in F_\mathrm{inter}$ on logical qubits $q_1, q_2$,
let $c_\mathrm{src}$ and $c_\mathrm{tgt}$ be the cores currently
hosting $q_1$ and $q_2$ respectively (i.e.\ $\ell(q_1)\in c_\mathrm{src}$
and $\ell(q_2)\in c_\mathrm{tgt}$, where $\ell$ is the current layout).
Both endpoints are tried as teleportation sources.
For the qubit chosen as source (say $q_1$ at physical position $p_1 = \ell(q_1)$),
the router enumerates every neighbouring core
$c_\mathrm{next}$ and every inter-core link
$(\pi_s, \pi_d)$ between $c_\mathrm{src}$ and $c_\mathrm{next}$.
Each such triple $(q_1, c_\mathrm{next}, (\pi_s,\pi_d))$ is one
\emph{candidate}; Figure~\ref{fig:tele-candidates} illustrates the structure.
Executing a candidate involves two steps\rev{, plus eviction of either
communication port if occupied (operational definition above)}: (1)~intra-core
SWAPs that move $q_1$ from $p_1$ to the \emph{staging slot} $n_s$, the
neighbour of $\pi_s$ closest to $p_1$; and (2)~a teleportation that
consumes the EPR pair on link $(\pi_s,\pi_d)$ and delivers $q_1$ from
$n_s$ to $\pi_d$ in $c_\mathrm{next}$.
\rev{Both steps belong to the action and are scored together, so a
teleport is available from \emph{anywhere} in the source core and an
occupied port raises a candidate's score rather than removing it
(Section~\ref{sec:related} contrasts this with \TeleSABRE{}).}
Each candidate is scored as:

\begin{equation}
  s = \underbrace{d_{\mathrm{prep}}}_{\text{staging}} +
      \underbrace{c_{\mathrm{cap}}}_{\text{capacity}} +
      \underbrace{\Delta_F + w_e\,\tilde{\Delta}_E}_{\Delta\Phi\ \text{(front $+$ lookahead)}},
  \label{eq:dsabre}
\end{equation}
where lower scores are preferred.  \rev{The last group is \SABRE{}'s
front-plus-lookahead objective (Eq.~\ref{eq:sabre}), the whole score on
a monolithic chip; the first two are the availability and capacity
terms of Section~\ref{sec:principle}.  Unlike Eq.~\ref{eq:intra} this
score carries no denominators: $\Delta_F$ is taken over the single
front gate the candidate is scored for, and $\tilde{\Delta}_E$ is
already scaled by $\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g)}$ and capped at
$|E|_{\max}$, so there is no cardinality to divide out.}  Each term is
defined as follows.

\textbf{Staging cost} $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ charges the intra-core SWAPs the
move needs: the walk taking $q_1$ from $p_1$ to staging slot $n_s$, plus
eviction SWAPs to free the communication ports if occupied:
\[
  d_{\mathrm{prep}} = d_{\mathrm{intra}}(p_1,\,n_s)
                    + d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi_s)
                    + d_{\mathrm{evict}}(\pi_d).
\]

\textbf{Capacity penalty} $c_{\mathrm{cap}} = c_{\mathrm{pen}}\,\max(0,\,\tau - f_{\mathrm{nxt}})$
discourages routing into nearly-full cores.
$f_{\mathrm{nxt}}$ is the number of free slots in the immediate landing
core $c_\mathrm{next}$ (not the final partner-qubit core $c_\mathrm{tgt}$),
$\tau$ the capacity threshold, and $c_{\mathrm{pen}}$ a cost multiplier.
This discourages progressive crowding of high-demand cores and empirically reduces the cascading evictions associated with saturated landing cores.
\rev{The two capacity mechanisms act at different points: the legality
floor is a filter, so a core with fewer than $\phi_1$ free slots never
becomes a candidate, while $c_{\mathrm{cap}}$ grades the survivors.  At
the defaults the surviving band is narrow---a legal landing core has
$f_{\mathrm{nxt}}\ge 2$, so the penalty is $c_{\mathrm{pen}}$ exactly
when $f_{\mathrm{nxt}}{=}2$ and zero above---which is why it costs
little once the floor is in place (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).}

\textbf{Front-layer change} \rev{$\Delta_F = d_{\mathrm{new}}^{g} - d_{\mathrm{old}}^{g}$
is the change in weighted physical distance between $q_1$ and $q_2$
after the teleport (negative when they are brought closer)}, computed on
the full-chip graph $G_\arch$ (intra-core
edges weight~1, inter-core links weight~$w_{\mathrm{link}}$).
Exactly one front-layer gate involves $q_1$: gates sharing a qubit are
DAG-ordered, so at most one can be unblocked at a time, and $g$ is
already that gate by the premise of this scoring step.

\textbf{Decay-weighted lookahead.}
$\tilde{\Delta}_E$ is Eq.~\ref{Delta_E_Tilde} over the whole extended
set $E$ (Section~\ref{sec:bfsext}) rather than a single core's
restriction $E_\mathrm{c}$, with one adaptation for the inter-core
scorer: the sum is restricted to gates involving the teleported qubit,
the only ones the move affects.

\subsection{Inter-Core Extended-Set Construction}
\label{sec:bfsext}

\rev{The lookahead in Eq.~\ref{eq:dsabre} is the same
$\tilde{\Delta}_E$ as Eq.~\ref{Delta_E_Tilde}, with $\mathrm{dep}(g)$
now read off the inter-core set $E$ rather than an intra-core wire; how
$E$ is filled is therefore what decides whether the discount means
anything.}
\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} fills $E$ in topological order,
interleaving all wires uniformly; \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}
uses BFS layers but within each layer emits gates in arbitrary order
and weights the $g$-th by $(1+g/10)$, growing with emission index
rather than decaying with depth.  \rev{Both can deprioritise the very
follow-on gates a candidate teleport would help.}

\dSABRE{} expands the remaining DAG by BFS layer, assigning every gate
of layer~$k$ $\mathrm{dep}(g){=}k$ and emitting, within a layer, the
gates sharing a qubit with the front first.  It adds no hyperparameter
and runs in $O(|E|_\mathrm{max})$ time per call when remaining
in-degrees are maintained incrementally.

\rev{The construction matters structurally: a gate's influence is
$\gamma^{\mathrm{dep}(g)}$, so the lookahead is meaningful only if the
gates surviving the size-$L$ truncation are the shallow ones.  BFS
layering guarantees that---layer $k{+}1$ is emitted only once layer $k$
is exhausted---whereas a topological sweep can spend the whole budget on
one deep wire and omit a depth-1 gate on another, which is why its cost
grows with circuit size (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).}

\subsection{\rev{Capacity Conditions and a Guaranteed
Transaction}}
\label{sec:relief}
\label{sec:recovery}

\rev{One state defeats the score of Eq.~\ref{eq:principle}: every cheap
action blocked by saturated cores, or no action reducing $|W|$ at all.
\dSABRE{} meets it with the checkpoint--rollback plumbing \SABRE{}-style
routers carry, and a transaction that provably retires the stuck
gate.  Every iteration that reduces $|W|$ checkpoints $(\ell,W)$; after
$L_\mathrm{deadlock}$ stalled iterations---or immediately, if no
candidate is legal---the router restores the checkpoint and hands the
gate to that transaction.  
%What \LightSABRE{}~\cite{zou2024lightsabrelightweightenhancedsabre} forces at this point may fail; what follows cannot.
}

\textbf{\rev{Three capacity conditions.}}
\rev{Write $F=P-n$ for the free slots the architecture leaves the
circuit.  The first condition is on the \emph{instance},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:feasible}
  F \;\ge\; rK+1 ,
\end{equation}
with reserve $r{=}2$ by default.  Every SWAP and teleport conserves $F$
exactly, so this is a property of architecture and circuit alone:
checked once before routing, and true at every step thereafter.  Its
$+1$ guarantees a \emph{donor}---if every core held at most $r$ free
slots the total would be at most $rK$, so some core always holds
$r{+}1$ and can give one away without dropping below $r$ itself.}

\rev{The second is the per-core \emph{reserve}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:invariant}
  \mathrm{free}(c) \;\ge\; r \qquad \text{for every core } c ,
\end{equation}
the state from which the transaction below can plan.  It is \emph{not}
maintained: ordinary scored moves may spend a core below it.  It holds
at exactly two kinds of moment---once at entry, where the initial
layout is brought up to it, and on each entry to a transaction, which
re-establishes it for itself by relaying slack from a donor and exits
having restored it.  Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} is what makes that relay
always possible, so the reserve is paid for when it is used rather than
on every iteration.}

\rev{The third is what ordinary scoring must respect, and it is the
weakest: a teleport is admitted only if its destination holds
$\phi_1{=}2$ free slots when it is scored, so one survives the arrival.
Zero free slots is what actually breaks routing: a qubit in a full core
generally cannot leave it, since clearing the outgoing port needs
somewhere to displace its occupant.  Forbidding that costs one
comparison per candidate, and together with the two conditions above
yields an invariant---no core is ever full---that makes every scored move
executable as scored.}

\rev{Given Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}, any remote gate can be resolved by a
\emph{transaction}: a plan executed all-or-nothing, which either retires
its gate and leaves Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} restored, or leaves the
layout exactly as it found it.  The plan is to choose a meeting core
minimising hop count plus the cost of relaying slack to it, walk the
operands there, and execute.  Every hop stays legal---a shortest core path touches each
intermediate core twice, and an arrival leaves it at $r{-}1\ge 1$,
still enough to clear the destination port, with the departure
restoring it---and the transaction exits having restored
Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}, or rolls back at zero cost.  Hop count and
relay cost are its whole objective, not Eq.~\ref{eq:principle}: its job
is feasibility, not EPR economy.}

\rev{\textbf{Theorem (termination with success).} \emph{If $r\ge 2$,
the core graph and every core's coupling graph are connected, every
core holds at least $r{+}3$ physical qubits, Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible}
holds, and checkpoint--rollback is enabled, then \dSABRE{} routes every
gate of the input circuit: it terminates, and it does not abort.}}

\rev{\emph{Proof (Sketch):} We use induction on the unrouted-gate count.  From any
reachable state Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} supplies a donor, so relaying its
slack re-establishes Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}, from which the whole plan
is legal.  The transaction therefore retires its target gate, and
the count strictly decreases, so at most one transaction fires per
gate; between two of them the loop runs at most $L_\mathrm{deadlock}$
non-progressing iterations before the next one does.  Charging each
gate its stall window, its own transaction and one progressing
iteration bounds the whole loop at $L_\mathrm{deadlock}{+}2$ iterations
per gate---the two being the iteration in which its transaction fires
and the one in which some gate retires, the stall window covering the
rest.  This bounds \emph{iterations}, which is what
Eq.~\ref{eq:complexity} costs: an ordinary iteration applies one
intra-core SWAP per active core or one teleport, so it spends at most
one EPR pair, while the transaction's iteration spends the $O(KD_K)$
priced below.  The induction
runs on Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} rather than Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant}, which
is why the reserve need not be maintained.  It also prices the
transaction.  Co-locating the operands costs $D_K$ hops, and clearing
room at the meeting core at most $2D_K$ more.  Re-establishing
Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} first is the dominant term: ordinary moves can
leave every core one slot short, and each unit of slack travels up to
$D_K$ hops from its donor, so the repair costs $O(KD_K)$ in the worst
case---on a core path whose slack all sits at one end, $\Theta(K^2)$.
Even a circuit drained entirely by transactions therefore costs
$O(KD_K)$ EPR per remote gate, the first non-vacuous upper bound
\dSABRE{} has had.  It is loose: what the transaction actually spends
is measured in Section~\ref{sec:tier2}.}

\rev{All five hypotheses are checked at entry, so a violation is
reported before routing starts rather than as an abort mid-route, and
the initial layout is brought up to Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} at the same
point.}

\subsection{\rev{Initial Layout}}
\label{sec:initial_mapping}
\rev{Both the capacity penalty and the guaranteed transaction depend on
the initial layout leaving free slots; this is how it is produced.}
\dSABRE{} bootstraps from Qiskit
\texttt{SabreLayout}~\cite{li2019tackling} run on the full
architecture graph $G_\arch$ (intra-core edges plus inter-core
links), with two adaptations for the distributed setting.

\emph{Per-core corner reservation.}
SabreLayout packs each core to capacity, which stalls the inter-core
scorer---an occupied port cannot accept a teleport without first
evicting its occupant---and trips the capacity penalty on every
candidate landing there.  \rev{We therefore withhold from SabreLayout's
coupling map the $k$ most-remote \emph{corner} qubits of \emph{every}
core: the minimum-degree vertices farthest, by total shortest-path
distance, from all others.  $k$ is adaptive, the largest value in
$\{r,\dots,4\}$ holding the usable-slot fill
$n_q/(N_{\mathrm{cores}}(m^2-k))$ at or below $0.80$---$k{=}4$ on the
25/36/100/200-qubit suites, $k{=}2$ on the denser 64- and 360-qubit
ones.  The floor at $r$ is what makes Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} hold on
the produced layout by construction rather than by coincidence, and
Eq.~\ref{eq:feasible} is exactly the condition under which it is
affordable.  Reserving \emph{per core} is what matters: under core-major
numbering a whole-chip rule reserves slots in one core and packs every
other.  Since the rule reads only vertex degree and remoteness it
transfers to any core shape, selecting leaf qubits on the heavy-hex
cores of Section~\ref{sec:main_results} where it selects corners on a
grid.}

\emph{Forward--backward--forward schedule.}
We run three passes---forward, then the reversed DAG, then forward
again---and keep the better of the two forward passes by EPR.  The
backward pass pre-positions qubits near their early interaction
partners, which the third warm-starts from: \SABRE{}'s backward
refinement~\cite{li2019tackling}, and better than pure-forward
warm-restart.  Best of three SabreLayout seeds is reported.

\subsection{\rev{Complexity}}
\label{sec:complexity}
Write $N{=}|C|$ for the gate count,
$D_M$ and $D_K$ for the intra-core and core-graph diameters, and $A$,
$B$, $T$ for the intra-core SWAPs, the teleports, and the iterations the
router performs; the rest is as in Table~\ref{tab:notation}.  Distance
tables are precomputed once per architecture in $O(KM^2{+}K^2\log K)$
(Section~\ref{sec:arch}) and are not charged per circuit.

\rev{Scoring is charged per \emph{applied move} rather than per
iteration, since an iteration applies one SWAP in each active core and
charging both per-iteration and per-move would count that parallelism
twice.  A gate needs $O(D_M)$ SWAPs and $O(D_K)$
teleports, so $A=O(ND_M)$, $B=O(ND_K)$ and $T=O(A+B)$.}
\rev{Scoring one intra-core SWAP costs $O(|F_\mathrm{c}|{+}L)$: a
candidate moves at most \emph{two} front-layer operands, since front
gates are qubit-disjoint, leaving every other term of $\Delta_F$
identically zero.}  Scoring one teleport costs $O(Mn{+}P)$:
$O(n)$ candidates at $O(M{+}L)$ each---the $M$ is the scan for an eviction
target---over an $O(P)$ free-slot census.  \rev{Both lookahead sets are
cheap once in-degrees are carried between calls: $O(n{+}L)$ for the
inter-core set, and $O(L)$ for every $E_\mathrm{c}$ together, one pass
over it.}  Hence
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  T_\mathrm{total} = O\big(&\underbrace{N(D_M{+}D_K)(n{+}L{+}M)}_{\text{lookahead}}\\
    &+ \underbrace{N D_M (M{+}L)}_{\text{SWAP scoring}}
     + \underbrace{N D_K (Mn{+}P)}_{\text{teleport scoring}}\big)
\end{split}
  \label{eq:complexity}
\end{equation}
\rev{---unconditionally, because the theorem of
Section~\ref{sec:recovery} caps the loop at $L_\mathrm{deadlock}{+}2$
iterations per gate and so supplies a budget instead of assuming one.
$D_M{+}D_K$ is $O(\sqrt{M}{+}\sqrt{K})$ on a grid-of-grids but linear
in $K$ on a core ring, and the bound is linear in $N$---which the
router measures close to, $N^{1.05}$ across every suite here.}
Restricting intra-core scoring to core-local edges further reduces the
front-layer term by up to a factor $K$ over flat
\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} on the same $P$ qubits.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Benchmarks.}
We use three circuit sets from the MQT Bench suite~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench},
all in the IBM native-gate set (Qiskit opt3 transpilation, measurements and
barriers removed).

\emph{Suite~1 (25-qubit):} quantum amplitude estimation (AE), quantum Fourier
transform (QFT), quantum neural network (QNN), random circuit (Random), GHZ
state preparation, and graph-state preparation.

\emph{Suite~2 (36-qubit):} six circuits spanning a broader range of algorithmic
families and CX densities: Bernstein--Vazirani (BV, linear chain),
Deutsch--Jozsa (DJ, oracle), W-state preparation (cascaded star),
VQE with SU(2) ansatz (layered variational), QAOA (dense all-to-all), and
quantum phase estimation (QPEexact, hierarchical).

\emph{Suite~3 (64-qubit):} the same six circuit families as Suite~1 scaled to
64~logical qubits (AE, QFT, QNN, Random, GHZ, Graphstate), run on the larger
H-grid architecture\rev{, extended with three further circuits that
complete the algorithmic-class coverage at the largest scale: QPEexact
(phase estimation), QAOA (dense max-cut cost layers with shared-control $ZZ$ structure), and
a 16-bit Multiplier (arithmetic, the suite's largest circuit at
13\,040 CX)}.

\textbf{Architecture.}
Suites~1 and~2 use a $2\!\times\!2$ B-grid: four $4\!\times\!4$ cores
(64~physical qubits, 4~EPR links).  Suite~3 uses a $2\!\times\!3$
H-grid: six $4\!\times\!4$ cores (96~physical qubits, 7~EPR links). See Figure~\ref{fig:arch} for the full
qubit-level topology. The 25q suite on
the B-grid and the 64q suite on the H-grid follow the benchmark setup
of \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}.  \rev{Section~\ref{sec:heavyhex} adds two
further architectures, built from IBM heavy-hex cores wired as a ring
and as a star, to test whether the router's advantage depends on the
grid-of-grids structure above.}

\textbf{Baselines.}
\rev{\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is the baseline every headline
number is measured against. It shares
\dSABRE{}'s cost model, physical architecture, and intra-core SWAP
accounting.}  It is also a \SABRE{}-style router that
jointly optimises intra-core SWAPs, teledata (qubit moves), and telegate
(remote CX execution) operations, run
using its compiled C binary with the
\texttt{optimize\_initial} flag enabled (built-in layout heuristic +
3-success sampling) on the same device.

\rev{We also compare against \texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{pytketdqc2024}, a
Quantinuum library that partitions a circuit's interaction hypergraph
with KaHyPar and then \emph{embeds} each hyperedge as a sequence of
gate teleportations sharing one EPR pair---a Steiner tree or cover
embedding spanning the servers the hyperedge touches---so multiple
non-local gates on the same wire amortise onto a single pair.  The
machine-model caveats listed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, all of them in
\texttt{pytket-dqc}'s favour, are large enough that the comparison needs a
section of its own (Section~\ref{sec:pytket}).  The third baseline,
DMapS~\cite{luo2025dmaps}, is the closest peer---a \SABRE{}-derived router
sharing our joint EPR-plus-SWAP objective, differing mainly in its
cross-core primitive, a two-EPR remote SWAP.  Section~\ref{sec:related}
gives the head-to-head: $2$--$4\times$ fewer EPR pairs at 25q,
$1.6$--$1.8\times$ at 64q.}

\emph{What one EPR pair buys.}
\rev{All three tools report in the same unit, one consumed EPR
pair}---but what each unit \emph{buys}
differs, and no cross-tool comparison is meaningful without stating
the difference.  \dSABRE{} uses \emph{teledata}: one EPR per
inter-core hop relocates a logical qubit, after which subsequent
intra-core gates on it are free.  \TeleSABRE{} uses that primitive and
telegate, and we count each operation once.  \rev{\texttt{pytket-dqc}, not
a router but a static partitioner, amortises many gates onto one pair
under an unbounded-entanglement-lifetime assumption
(Section~\ref{sec:teleportation}).}
\rev{Its column is therefore unit-consistent with the others but not
work-consistent, in the sense given in the Introduction; the online
appendices tabulate what a pair buys under each tool's model.}

\textbf{Parameters.}
Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists all hyperparameters; the defaults are used
unchanged on every suite and architecture\rev{, and the recovery budgets are
read off the architecture rather than set, so no suite carries a tuned
value}.  Each router uses its authors'
convention: best-of-3 seeds for \dSABRE{} and
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre,li2019tackling}, best-of-5 for
\texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{pytketdqc2024}, which reflects operational cost
rather than per-attempt variance.

\subsection{\rev{Generality and Scale}}
\label{sec:scalability}

\subsubsection{\rev{Architecture independence: heavy-hex cores on a ring and on a star}}
\label{sec:heavyhex}
\rev{Section~\ref{sec:arch} models the architecture as an abstract weighted
graph queried only through three distance tables, but everything above runs on
a grid of grids.  If the scoring or the capacity mechanism tacitly assumed grid
structure at either level, that generality claim would be empty, so we vary
both levels at once.  The core becomes a 27-qubit IBM heavy-hex tile of the
Falcon family~\cite{chamberland2020topological}, which shares none of the
grid's regularity---degrees over $\{1,2,3\}$, six degree-1 leaves, diameter 12
against the $4{\times}4$ grid's 6---and four such tiles (108 physical qubits)
are wired as a \emph{ring} (4 links) and as a \emph{star} (3 links, core~0 a
hub every inter-core route must pass).  The star is the harsher case, and the
one in which a scorer that quietly assumed a homogeneous core graph would
break.  Nothing is specialised for either: the corner reservation of
Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping} reads vertex degree and remoteness, so it
selects leaf qubits where it selected grid corners, and takes $k{=}4$ off the
$59\%$ fill on its own.}

\rev{\dSABRE{} keeps its advantage on both core graphs, over the same nine
circuits as the H-grid suite.  On the ring both routers complete all nine and
the margin is $\mathbf{-52.0\%}$; read on the eight circuits \TeleSABRE{}
completes on both architectures it is $-53.8\%$ against the H-grid's
$-49.1\%$, so leaving the grid widens the gap rather than costing it.  On the
star it \emph{narrows} to $\mathbf{-41.5\%}$, tightest on QPEexact at $120$
EPR against $132$---the closest race \dSABRE{} runs anywhere in this paper,
and still a win.  \TeleSABRE{} fails on Random, which \dSABRE{} routes at
$475$ EPR.}

\subsubsection{\rev{Large circuits}}
\label{sec:largecircuits}

\rev{To stress-test \dSABRE{} beyond the main evaluation range we run it
and \TeleSABRE{} on the MQT-Bench QFT at \textbf{100}, \textbf{200} and
\textbf{360} logical qubits.  Core size is held at $5{\times}5$ and only
the core count grows---$2{\times}3$, $3{\times}4$, $4{\times}5$ (6, 12 and
20 cores)---so logical qubits per core stay near-constant at $16.7$--$18.0$
while the core graph lengthens, diameter $3\to5\to7$.  Fixing occupancy is
what makes the three rows one series rather than three unrelated points.}

\rev{Table~\ref{tab:main} reports them.  \dSABRE{} leads at both sizes
where \TeleSABRE{} converges, and the margin widens with diameter: at
100q's diameter-3 core graph there is less for the capacity term and
lookahead to decide ($-19.0\%$), while 200q's diameter-5 graph gives those
mechanisms real decisions to make ($-37.8\%$).  At \textbf{360q on twenty
cores \TeleSABRE{} does not converge on any of three seeds}---every attempt
exhausts its ten internal retries---while \dSABRE{} completes at
$\mathbf{1890}$ EPR. }

\subsubsection{\rev{Compile time}}
\label{sec:timing}

\rev{All three compilers are timed on the 64-qubit suite, over the eight
circuits \TeleSABRE{} completes.  In geometric mean \dSABRE{} leads at
$1.9$\,s against \TeleSABRE{}'s $4.7$\,s and \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s
$65$\,s.  Per circuit it splits four--four with \TeleSABRE{} but
asymmetrically, losing by under a second and winning by up to
$9.9\times$; it beats \texttt{pytket-dqc} everywhere, by
$20$--$118\times$ above $1000$ gates, that tool's cost being set by

timed with the abandoned embedding attempt included.}

\subsubsection{\rev{How often the guarantee is used}}
\label{sec:tier2}

\rev{The rows above price mechanisms by removing them; this one counts,
on the shipped default, how often the completion guarantee of
Section~\ref{sec:recovery} is invoked.  The \emph{guarantee} is the
all-or-nothing transaction: when scoring stalls, the router restores its
last checkpoint and hands the stuck gate to a plan that walks both
operands to a meeting core and executes there, which
Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} makes always possible.  One transaction retires
one gate, so its count is also a gate count, and is read two ways:
\emph{reported} counts only the route whose EPR figure reaches
Table~\ref{tab:main}, \emph{search} all nine routes behind it---three
\texttt{SabreLayout} seeds by the three passes of
Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping}---of which the reported route is one,
so search includes it.  A \emph{relay} is a different unit.  To give a
core the free slot Eq.~\ref{eq:invariant} asks for, the router walks
slack in from the nearest core that has some; each hop teleports one
uninvolved qubit the other way---a \emph{filler}, never an operand of
the gate being routed---so that the empty slot advances, at the price of
an EPR pair.  Both the transaction and ordinary deadlock recovery relay,
which is why relays appear on suites where no transaction fires, and
they are counted whole-search only.}

where $27\,279$ gates are routed in all.  The transaction's rollback
paths are assertions on the theorem's hypotheses---no donor core, no
meeting core, a relay that cannot complete---so that a violated one
would be visible rather than silent; across all $1074$ none fired.  Nor
is the scored path any different: under those conditions the cheapest
candidate provably always commits, and none of the $1.08$ million
candidates scored across four architectures was rejected.}

\subsubsection{\rev{The initial layout}}
\label{sec:sharedmap}
\rev{The last row prices Section~\ref{sec:initial_mapping}'s layout by
replacing it: \dSABRE{} routing from \TeleSABRE{}'s own
\texttt{optimize\_initial} layout, so that only the routing loop differs.
It costs $30.9\%$, and the margin over \TeleSABRE{} persists without our
layout---$-33.4\%$ against $-49.1\%$ with it. }

\subsubsection{Parameter sensitivity}
\rev{Sweeping each continuous hyperparameter one-at-a-time, every default
sits on a flat region of its curve (within
${\sim}2\%$ of the local minimum); only pushing a control far from its
default causes a sharp regression, e.g.\ $\gamma{=}1.0$ (flat weighting)
costs $7\%$ gmEPR.  No single hyperparameter is a hidden lever.  The
full sweep is reported separately, with the cost model swept
alongside it: raising $c_{\mathrm{tele}}$ from 10 to 100 at fixed
$c_{\mathrm{swap}}$ widens \dSABRE{}'s margin monotonically on every suite.}


\subsection{\rev{Comparison with \texttt{pytket-dqc}}}
\label{sec:pytket}

\rev{\texttt{pytket-dqc} is not a router but a
hypergraph partitioner emitting gate teleportations.  It reports its
cost in \emph{e-bits}, one e-bit being one shared EPR pair, so its
counts are in our unit; we keep its term in this subsection, where its
own numbers are read.  Of the four
differences listed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, two are architectural
assumptions rather than cost-model conventions, and both can be corrected;
Table~\ref{tab:fair} reports the comparison before and after, throughout using
its strongest distributor, \textsc{CoverEmbeddingSteinerDetached}, best of five
seeds.\label{sec:fair}}

\rev{\emph{Correcting the network.}  The network \texttt{pytket-dqc}
normally given is more permissive than our device in two respects.  Its
per-server \emph{communication capacity}---the number of link qubits a core may
hold simultaneously---is unbounded
unless set, whereas a core here has one port per incident link, $\deg(c)=2$
or~$3$; and its server sizes split the \emph{logical} qubit count evenly,
whereas a core physically offers $M-\deg(c)$ data slots.}

\rev{\emph{The corrected counts are still not executable.}  Communication
capacity does not reach the cost model: as
noted in~\cite{andresmartinez2024heterogeneous}, none of the distribution
routines consider it, and declaring it left all $21$ distributions
unchanged.  Generating the circuit is what tests executability, and usually
it fails: \textbf{13 of the 21 distributions cannot be built within the
communication capacity the device provides}, AE at 25 qubits needing $19$
simultaneous link qubits per core against the $2$ available.  This is no
coincidence---a hyperedge over $g$ gates buys a factor of $g$ in e-bits and
pays in link-qubit occupancy, so the cheapest-looking distributions demand
the most memory---and the repair pass that would restore executability does
not reach them: on the 25-qubit suite it returns only on the two
distributions that already fit, and not within $300$\,s on any that do
not.}

\rev{\emph{What survives the corrections.}\label{sec:pytket_results}
Read with that caveat in force---most \texttt{pytket-dqc} counts below are the
cost of a distribution its own tool cannot build on this device
(Table~\ref{tab:fair}, last column)---the direction is set by circuit structure
rather than size.  \dSABRE{} wins five of six circuits at 25 qubits; at 64 the
comparison reverses on four of nine---QPEexact, where Steiner-tree embedding
reuses a few pairs across many controlled operations ($+58.5\%$), AE
($+16.2\%$), QAOA ($+17.3\%$), and the trivially small Graphstate (7 against

\dSABRE{} leads on the densest circuits (Multiplier $-55.6\%$, QNN

\rev{\emph{Entanglement lifetime.}\label{sec:lifetime}
A second assumption is separable from the first: capacity is how many pairs
are live at once, lifetime is how long one must live.  Every e-bit count
above assumes an \emph{unbounded} lifetime, a link qubit staying entangled
until the last gate of its hyperedge.  Bandini \emph{et
al.}~\cite{bandini2026optimized} argue this is unrealistic and cap
gates-per-EPR at $D_{\max}$, proposing $D_{\max}{=}3$.  Re-scored under that
bound by a counter that generalises \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s own cost model and
reproduces it at $D_{\max}{=}\infty$---\dSABRE{} needs no adjustment, a
teledata pair being consumed instantly---the \texttt{pytket-dqc} lead
($+15.0\%$ at $D_{\max}{=}\infty$) survives only above a threshold: the
geometric-mean gap crosses parity between $D_{\max}{=}\infty$ and $10$, and
becomes a \dSABRE{} win of $\mathbf{46.5\%}$ at $D_{\max}{=}3$.  Gate
teleportation's e-bit advantage is therefore conditional on sustaining
entanglement across ${\gtrsim}10$ gates per pair; teledata routing delivers
its counts under any lifetime.  Per-circuit sweeps are in the online
appendices.}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The Introduction already places \dSABRE{} within the
partition--communicate--schedule decomposition of the DQC compilation
stack and lists the major lines of work in each stage.  Here we focus
on what specifically distinguishes \dSABRE{} from the closest prior
art.

\textbf{\SABRE{}-style distributed routers.}
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is our closest prior work and
direct experimental baseline.  \rev{Section~\ref{sec:principle} states
what \dSABRE{} scores differently; the three differences that produce
the measured gap are where it prices \emph{availability} (a macro-action
against a bare hop that an occupied port deletes), how it
\emph{selects} (a priority rule against a pooled arg\,min with a flat
bonus, under which $59.5\%$ of \TeleSABRE{}'s 64-qubit communication
operations fire while a same-core front gate is still waiting), and how
it charges \emph{capacity} (graded in the landing core's slack and
backed by a legality floor, against a fixed penalty charged along the
whole Dijkstra path).}
\TeleSABRE{} supports telegate candidates
while \dSABRE{} currently uses teledata only.

DMapS~\cite{luo2025dmaps} is a concurrent end-to-end DQC compiler
pairing a KaHyPar partitioner with a \SABRE{}-derived router, and shares
our joint EPR-plus-local-SWAP objective.  Its only cross-core primitive
is a two-EPR \emph{remote SWAP}, where \dSABRE{} uses one-way teledata
into a layout-reserved free slot.  \rev{Over the whole nine-circuit
64-qubit suite that asymmetry shows: \dSABRE{} uses $1.43\times$ fewer
EPRs in geometric mean and $1.5$--$3.1\times$ fewer on each of the seven
circuits above $1000$ CX.  DMapS wins
only on GHZ and Graphstate, which KaHyPar partitions almost perfectly.}
%The code-level audit, per-circuit head-to-head and a fill-ratio sweep showing parity near full occupancy are reported separately.

Allocation methods built on hypergraph
partitioning~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024} are complementary
to \dSABRE{}: any can supply the initial layout its routing loop
consumes.  Similarly, time-aware
schedulers~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler} sit upstream or
downstream of the routing stage \dSABRE{} addresses.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{Composing \dSABRE{} with burst execution \rev{and telegate}.}
\dSABRE{} optimises EPR count on a fixed circuit DAG \rev{ using teledata
alone (Section~\ref{sec:dsabre}); burst execution and telegate are two
views of the same next step, not two separate ones}.  Burst-execution
passes such as AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm} and its collective
extension QuComm~\cite{wu2023qucomm} operate earlier in the
toolchain: they use gate commutativity to reorder consecutive non-local
gates on the same wire (or across multiple wires) and amortise the
whole block onto a single shared entanglement state, reporting EPR
savings of $50$--$75\%$ on amenable circuits\rev{, which the
lifetime-bounded grouping of Bandini \emph{et
al.}~\cite{bandini2026optimized} tempers with the $D_{\max}$ constraint
of Section~\ref{sec:lifetime}}.  \rev{That amortisation is exactly what
a telegate candidate needs to be worth scoring: Section~\ref{sec:dsabre}
identifies the obstacle as a \emph{grouping} decision, and a
burst-extraction pre-pass is one way to supply it externally, exposing
wire-aligned sequences for \dSABRE{}'s BFS extended set to score as a
unit.  It is also where the reversal of
Section~\ref{sec:pytket_results} comes from---gate teleportation beats
\dSABRE{} on QPEexact and AE, whose long shared-control sequences such
a pre-pass would expose---so closing that gap and adding telegate are
one open question, not two.}
Quantifying the joint improvement---especially
on circuits where a burst's beneficiary core is itself a function of
the routing decisions \dSABRE{} makes---remains open.

\textbf{Parallel teleportation for circuit-runtime minimisation.}
\dSABRE{} optimises EPR count and emits one teleport per iteration;
wall-clock runtime depends additionally on whether teleports with
disjoint links and staging sets can fire concurrently.  A natural
follow-on is a scheduling pass over \dSABRE{}'s teleport trace that
batches non-conflicting moves, co-designed with denser inter-core
links~\cite{escofet2023interconnect} and entanglement buffer
qubits~\cite{wu2023qucomm,liu2025codesign}.  Time-sliced
compilers~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler} provide a starting
point but are not paired with a SABRE-style scoring loop; naively
adding links without retuning the routing energy can \emph{increase}
EPR consumption.

\rev{\textbf{Beyond EPR count: latency and fidelity.}
This paper optimises a resource count, not an execution time or an
output fidelity.  Two of the three system-level costs are already
partly visible: intra-core SWAP count, the dominant contributor to
routed depth, is reported beside EPR count in every table and falls
alongside it, and entanglement quality enters through the lifetime
bound of Section~\ref{sec:lifetime}.  A joint objective is the same
scheduling layer as the parallel-teleportation pass above, asked to do
more: EPR count, depth and per-operation error rates are not
interchangeable---a teleport is slow and noisy, a SWAP fast and cheap,
and the exchange rate is hardware-specific---so it needs a device error
model on top of the conflict check that concurrency alone requires.
The cost-ratio sweep is a first step, the conclusions holding across
the two orders of magnitude of relative teleport cost separating
microwave-coupled from photonic-link hardware.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \dSABRE{}, a \SABRE{}-style router for multi-core
distributed quantum computers.  \rev{It carries \SABRE{}'s objective
across cores under a single rule---the resource a move spends, plus the
capacity it consumes, plus the progress it buys---whose two added terms
are not independent heuristics but the two respects in which a teleport
is unlike a SWAP: it fires only at a communication port, and it consumes
a free slot in the core it lands in (Section~\ref{sec:principle}).}
\rev{Across 21 MQT Bench circuits at 25--64 logical qubits that rule
cuts geometric-mean EPR consumption by $49$--$62\%$ against
\TeleSABRE{}, and it scales to 360 logical qubits on twenty cores.
Capacity handling is what carries it: ablated jointly with the legality
floor that substitutes for it, capacity is worth $87.8\%$ and two
completions, against $12.4\%$ for the BFS-layer extended set.  A
capacity condition checked once before routing turns that from a
tendency into a guarantee---\dSABRE{} routes all four circuits here on
which \TeleSABRE{} does not converge, and under five checked
preconditions termination and success coincide by construction
(Section~\ref{sec:relief}).  More broadly, the results indicate that
distributed routing benefits less from a richer per-candidate formula
than from letting the architecture's capacity signal reach the scorer in
\emph{graded} form---and, independently of any score, from never leaving
feasibility to a heuristic's preference.}
