\begin{abstract}
Executing large quantum circuits on distributed quantum computers requires
routing two-qubit gates whose operands reside in different processing cores via
quantum teleportation, consuming one EPR pair per transfer.
Existing distributed routers extend the \SABRE{} algorithm to propose
teleportation actions one gate at a time, ignoring the commutativity structure
of the circuit.
We present \BurstDSABRE{}, which integrates an \emph{EigenCluster Hypergraph}
(ECH) into the teleportation scoring function.
The ECH partitions each qubit wire into maximal runs of mutually commuting
gates (\emph{SuperNodes}) using gate eigen-directions, and for each candidate
teleport computes a \emph{burst value}---the number of additional pending gates
on the same wire that benefit from the same qubit displacement.
Teleports with higher burst value are scored more favourably, amortising EPR
cost across multiple gates.
On the MQT benchmark suite (six 25-qubit circuits, IBM native-gate set) mapped
to a $2\!\times\!2$ grid of $4\!\times\!4$-qubit cores, \BurstDSABRE{} reduces
EPR consumption by \textbf{7.1--52.9\%} over \dSABRE{} across five of six
benchmarks.
Under a controlled fair comparison that fixes the initial layout to that used
by \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}, a recent state-of-the-art distributed
router, \BurstDSABRE{} achieves a geometric-mean \textbf{37.1\%} EPR reduction
over \TeleSABRE{} on \emph{all six} circuits, demonstrating that burst scoring
provides gains independently of layout quality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The near-term path to fault-tolerant quantum computing requires processors with
hundreds to thousands of high-fidelity qubits.
No single monolithic chip can economically deliver this scale today.
Instead, the field is converging on \emph{distributed quantum computers}
(DQC)~\cite{monroe2014large,wehner2018quantum,stephenson2020high}: multiple
small quantum processing units (cores) interconnected by photonic or microwave
links that support remote entanglement generation.

Executing a quantum circuit on a DQC requires a \emph{qubit mapping and routing}
pass.
Intra-core two-qubit gates between non-adjacent qubits are resolved with SWAP
insertions; inter-core gates require \emph{quantum teleportation}: one
pre-shared EPR pair is consumed to move a logical qubit across an inter-core
link.
EPR generation is slow (microseconds to milliseconds), noisy, and rate-limited
in current hardware, making EPR minimisation the dominant compilation objective
for DQC.

State-of-the-art distributed routers adapt the \SABRE{}
algorithm~\cite{li2019tackling} to DQC by scoring teleportation candidates using
heuristics that measure how much closer the teleported qubit gets to its
immediate partner gate, with a lookahead term over a fixed window of upcoming
gates~\cite{andres2019automated,ferrari2021compiler,wu2022collcomm,
rodrigues2022noise,baker2020time}.
A fundamental limitation of all these methods is that they evaluate each
teleport in isolation:\ they do not consider whether multiple pending gates on
the same wire would benefit from the same qubit displacement.
In circuits with substantial commutativity---where long runs of gates on a qubit
wire share identical eigen-directions and are mutually commuting---a single
teleport can simultaneously reduce the distance to \emph{many} pending gates.
Ignoring this structure leads to under-valuation of such teleports and, in
practice, to excessive EPR consumption.

\textbf{Contributions.}
We make the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{EigenCluster Hypergraph (ECH).}  We adapt the commutativity
    hypergraph of Hong et al.~\cite{hong2024commutativity} to the distributed
    routing problem.  Each qubit wire is partitioned into SuperNodes---maximal
    runs of gates sharing identical eigen-directions.  We maintain the front
    layer incrementally in $O(k)$ per gate removal, eliminating the
    $O(|V_\HG|)$ scan of naive implementations.

  \item \textbf{Burst value scoring.}  For each teleportation candidate we
    compute a \emph{burst value $\burstval$}: the number of pending gates on the
    moving qubit's wire that will have strictly reduced distance after the
    teleport, with a rescue-pass mechanism that handles commuting beneficial
    gates blocked by a single harmful one.

  \item \textbf{\BurstDSABRE{} router.}  A full distributed routing algorithm
    that integrates burst value into the \dSABRE{} scoring function and adds
    locality-optimised initial placement (centrality-ranked, local-search refined).

  \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation.}  Experiments on six MQT benchmark
    circuits~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench} mapped to a $2\!\times\!2$ core
    grid show EPR reductions of up to 52.9\%.  We characterise the circuit
    properties that determine when burst scoring helps and when it does not.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Distributed Quantum Architecture}

A DQC architecture $\arch$ consists of $K$ cores, each a 2D grid of $M$
physical qubits with nearest-neighbour connectivity.
Designated \emph{communication ports} at core boundaries can participate in
inter-core EPR links.
We model $\arch$ as a weighted graph $G_\arch$: intra-core edges carry weight~1,
inter-core links weight $w_{\mathrm{inter}} \gg 1$.
All-pairs Dijkstra distances over $G_\arch$ (stored as $\dphys$), all-pairs
intra-core distances ($\dintra$), and core-graph distances ($\dcore$) are
precomputed at architecture construction time, enabling $O(1)$ lookups during
routing.

\subsection{Qubit Mapping and Routing}

Given circuit DAG $C$ and initial layout $\ell:\logi{i}\!\mapsto\!\phys{\sigma(i)}$,
routing inserts the minimum number of SWAP and teleportation operations so every
gate can execute on adjacent physical qubits.
Cost model: each local SWAP costs $c_{\mathrm{swap}}$; each inter-core
teleport costs $c_{\mathrm{tele}}$ (one EPR pair).

\subsection{SABRE and dSABRE}

\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} selects the SWAP minimising:
\begin{equation}
  H = \tfrac{1}{|F|}\!\sum_{g\in F}\!\Delta_F(g)
    + w\,\tfrac{1}{|E|}\!\sum_{g\in E}\!\Delta_E(g),
  \label{eq:sabre}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the front layer, $E$ the extended lookahead, and $\Delta(g)$ the
gate-distance change induced by the swap.

\dSABRE{}~\cite{andres2019automated} replaces SWAP proposals with teleport
proposals, scored as:
\begin{equation}
  s_{\dSABRE} = d_{\mathrm{prep}} + c_{\mathrm{cap}} - g_{\mathrm{hop}}
              - \Delta_F - w_e\,\Delta_E,
  \label{eq:dsabre}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\mathrm{prep}}$ is the intra-core staging cost, $c_{\mathrm{cap}}$
penalises nearly-full destination cores, and $g_{\mathrm{hop}}$ rewards progress
toward the target core.

\subsection{Gate Commutativity via Eigen-Directions}

Two gates $g_1, g_2$ on qubit $q$ commute on wire $q$ iff
$\eig{g_1,q} = \eig{g_2,q}$, where $\eig{g,q}$ is the set of simultaneous
eigenstates of the gate's action on qubit $q$~\cite{hong2024commutativity}.
Examples: all CNOT controls share direction $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$, so any
two CNOT gates sharing a control qubit commute on that wire; $R_z$ gates all
share $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$, so any number of $R_z$ gates on the same qubit
can be freely reordered.

\section{EigenCluster Hypergraph}
\label{sec:hg}

\subsection{SuperNode Construction}

\begin{definition}[SuperNode]
A \emph{SuperNode} $\SN=(q,D,G)$ is a maximal contiguous run of gates on qubit
wire $q$ all sharing eigen-direction $D$.  Gates within an SN commute pairwise
on wire $q$.
\end{definition}

The ECH is built in a single $O(kn)$ pass (Algorithm~\ref{alg:build}), where
$k$ is gate arity and $n$ the gate count.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\texttt{BuildECH}(circuit $C$, $n_q$ qubits)}
\label{alg:build}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE frontier$[q] \leftarrow \texttt{None}$ for all $q$
\FOR{each gate $g$ in topological order}
  \FOR{each qubit $q \in g.\mathit{qubits}$}
    \IF{frontier$[q] \ne \texttt{None}$ \AND
        frontier$[q].D = \eig{g,q}$}
      \STATE frontier$[q].G \mathrel{\cup}= \{g\}$
    \ELSE
      \STATE $\SN' \leftarrow \text{newSN}(q,\,\eig{g,q},\,\mathrm{pred}=\text{frontier}[q])$
      \STATE $\SN'.G \leftarrow \{g\}$;\quad frontier$[q] \leftarrow \SN'$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE record $g \mapsto [\SN_q : q\in g.\mathit{qubits}]$
\ENDFOR
\STATE initialise $\mathcal{R} \leftarrow \{\SN : \SN.\mathit{pred}=\texttt{None}\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Incremental Ready-Set Maintenance}

A SuperNode is \emph{ready} iff its wire-predecessor is fully executed (or
absent).
Rather than scanning all nodes for readiness at each step ($O(|V_\HG|)$), we
maintain:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathcal{R}$: the set of all currently ready SuperNodes.
  \item $\pi[q]$: the unique ready SN on wire $q$ (or \texttt{None}).
\end{itemize}
On executing gate $g$, for each SN $s$ containing $g$: if $s$ becomes fully
executed, remove $s$ from $\mathcal{R}$; if $s$'s successor $s'$ exists and is
not yet executed, add $s'$ to $\mathcal{R}$ and set $\pi[s'.q]\leftarrow s'$.
Cost per gate removal: $O(k)$.  Front-layer query: $O(|\mathcal{R}|)$, bounded
by active wire count.

\subsection{Front Layer and Hyperedges}

For each 2-qubit gate $g$ on $(q_0,q_1)$, the ECH records a hyperedge
$e_g = (\SN_{q_0}^g, \SN_{q_1}^g)$.  Gate $g$ is in the \emph{front layer}
iff both member SNs are ready (\emph{ALL-SN rule}).
This correctly prevents a gate from being considered executable until its
wire-predecessors on all qubits are retired.

\section{BurstDSABRE}
\label{sec:burst}

\subsection{Routing Loop}

\BurstDSABRE{} iterates four phases until the circuit DAG is empty:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Drain.}  Execute all immediately applicable gates: adjacent
    same-core 2-qubit gates (no SWAP needed) and all 1-qubit gates.
  \item \textbf{Classify.}  Partition the ECH front layer into
    \emph{intra-core} (same core, non-adjacent) and \emph{inter-core} gates.
  \item \textbf{Resolve.}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item If intra-core gates exist: select and apply one local SWAP by the
        \dSABRE{} front-layer heuristic (Eq.~\ref{eq:sabre}).
      \item Else if inter-core gates exist: score burst candidates
        (Eq.~\ref{eq:burst}), apply the best teleport.
    \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Progress tracking.}  If no gate executes for
    $\Delta_{\mathrm{limit}}$ iterations, roll back to the last progress
    checkpoint and force a teleport for the most-stuck inter-core gate.
\end{enumerate}

For a candidate teleport moving qubit $v$ from $p_{\mathrm{src}}$ to
$p_{\mathrm{dst}}$, the burst value $\burstval$ is computed by
Algorithm~\ref{alg:burst}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\texttt{GetBurstValue}$(v, p_{\mathrm{dst}}, p_{\mathrm{src}}, \arch, \HG)$}
\label{alg:burst}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $\mathit{cur} \leftarrow \pi[v]$;\quad $\mathit{count} \leftarrow 0$
\WHILE{$\mathit{cur} \ne \texttt{None}$}
  \STATE $\mathit{found\_bad} \leftarrow \text{false}$
  \FOR{each pending 2-qubit gate $g \in \mathit{cur}$}
    \STATE $\delta \leftarrow \dphys(p_{\mathrm{src}},\,p_{\mathrm{partner}})
                             - \dphys(p_{\mathrm{dst}},\,p_{\mathrm{partner}})$
    \IF{$\delta > 0$}
      \STATE $\mathit{count} \mathrel{+}= 1$
    \ELSIF{$\delta < 0$}
      \STATE $\mathit{found\_bad} \leftarrow \text{true}$
      \FOR{front-ready $g' \in \mathit{cur}$, $g' \ne g$, with $\delta' > 0$}
        \STATE $\mathit{count} \mathrel{+}= 1$  \COMMENT{rescue pass}
      \ENDFOR
      \STATE \textbf{break}
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
  \IF{$\mathit{found\_bad}$} \textbf{break} \ENDIF
  \STATE $\mathit{cur} \leftarrow \mathit{cur}.\mathit{succ}$
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $\mathit{count}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The \emph{rescue-pass} mechanism is critical:\ when a harmful gate is
encountered, gates that commute with it on wire $v$ (guaranteed by SuperNode
membership) and are fully unblocked on all other wires (ALL-SN readiness) can
be reordered before it.
Rescue gates can be counted as burst beneficiaries without violating circuit
semantics.
The walk stops at the harmful gate's SN boundary to avoid counting gates whose
order cannot be freely moved past the harmful gate.

\subsection{Candidate Scoring and Selection}

For each inter-core front-layer gate and each candidate link $(p_s, p_d)$
between source core $c_s$ and neighbour core $c'$:
\begin{equation}
  s_{\BurstDSABRE} = d_{\mathrm{prep}} + c_{\mathrm{cap}} - g_{\mathrm{hop}}
    - w_\beta \cdot \min(\burstval,\, \burstval_{\max}),
  \label{eq:burst}
\end{equation}
where:
\[
  d_{\mathrm{prep}} = \dintra(p_{\mathrm{src}}, n_s)
                    + \mathrm{evict}(p_s) + \mathrm{evict}(p_d),
\]
\[
  c_{\mathrm{cap}}  = c_{\mathrm{pen}}\!\cdot\!\max(0, \tau - \mathrm{free}(c')),\quad
  g_{\mathrm{hop}}  = w_h\!\cdot\!(\dcore(c_s,c_t) - \dcore(c',c_t)).
\]
$n_s$ is the nearest neighbour of $p_s$ to $p_{\mathrm{src}}$ within $c_s$
(the staging node); $\mathrm{evict}(\cdot)$ is the intra-core SWAP cost to
clear an occupied communication port; $w_\beta$ and $\burstval_{\max}$ are the
burst weight and normaliser hyperparameters.
Candidates are sorted by score (lower = better); the minimum is applied via
\texttt{ApplyTeleport} (evict both comm ports, SWAP-chain $v$ to the staging
node, teleport across the link).

\subsection{Decay-Weighted Lookahead}

Intra-core SWAP scoring uses a decay-weighted extended layer to down-weight
distant gates:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_E^{\gamma} = \sum_{i} \gamma^i (d_{\mathrm{before}}^{(i)}
                                         - d_{\mathrm{after}}^{(i)}),
  \label{eq:decay}
\end{equation}
with decay factor $\gamma \in (0,1]$ (default $\gamma = 0.9$).
Setting $\gamma = 1$ recovers the flat lookahead of \dSABRE{}.

\subsection{Initial Placement}

Three strategies are evaluated:
\textbf{(A) Random}: uniform random assignment.
\textbf{(B) SABRE-lock}: Qiskit's \texttt{SabreLayout} on a corner-reduced
architecture graph (four corner nodes removed to reserve free communication port
slots), averaged over three seeds.
\textbf{(C) Locality-aware}: qubits are partitioned across cores by greedy
modularity community detection~\cite{clauset2004finding} on the circuit
interaction graph; within each core, physical slots are ranked by intra-core
centrality (sum-of-distances), logical qubits by weighted interaction-graph
degree, then greedily matched and refined by pairwise local search
(up to 20 rounds minimising total weighted intra-core interaction distance).

\subsection{Complexity}

Let $n$ be the qubit count, $m$ the gate count, $k$ the max gate arity,
$K$ the number of cores, and $L$ the number of inter-core links.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item ECH construction: $O(km)$.
  \item Gate removal / ready-set update: $O(k)$ amortised.
  \item Burst value query: $O(n_w \cdot d_w)$ where $n_w$ is SuperNode count on
    the wire and $d_w$ is max gates per SuperNode.
  \item Candidate generation per iteration: $O(|F_{\mathrm{inter}}| \cdot L/K)$.
  \item Architecture precomputation: $O(n^2\log n)$ time, $O(n^2)$ space.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis}

\textbf{High-CX circuits (QNN, QFT, AE).}
\BurstDSABRE{} delivers the largest reductions on circuits with many CX gates
per qubit.
QNN achieves up to 52.9\% reduction under locality layout; QFT achieves up to
36.4\%.
These circuits have dense, structured commutativity:\ QNN consists of repeated
two-qubit rotation layers, producing long runs of gates with identical control
eigen-directions; QFT has regular Hadamard--controlled-phase sequences.
The burst value averages 17--18 gates per teleport on QNN (820--917 cumulative
burst saves over 48--50 teleports), meaning each EPR pair effectively resolves
the distance constraint for $\approx$18 future gates.
This is the core benefit of burst scoring: teleports that would be scored
identically under \dSABRE{} receive a strong preference bonus when they happen
to lie along a long commutativity chain.

\textbf{Random layout advantage.}
Under layout A (random), \BurstDSABRE{} achieves 32.4\% (QNN) and 15.9\% (QFT)
reductions where \dSABRE{} uses more EPRs.
We hypothesise that random placement leaves many qubit pairs far apart across
cores, creating long inter-core interaction chains.
In this regime, burst teleportation can exploit commutativity chains that span
many SuperNodes, accumulating large burst values.
The SABRE-lock layout, by contrast, already places frequently-interacting qubits
near communication ports, so fewer burst opportunities arise.

\textbf{Layout strategy sensitivity.}
The locality-aware layout (C) consistently yields the highest absolute EPR
counts for \dSABRE{}, yet \BurstDSABRE{} reduces these to competitive levels,
averaging 28.3\% reduction under layout C.
Post-hoc analysis of the community-detection partitioning reveals that the
interaction graph of these circuits has many cross-community edges---the
greedy partitioner splits highly connected sub-graphs, placing frequent
interaction partners in different cores.
This increases the inter-core gate fraction, which is precisely the regime where
burst teleportation is most effective.

\textbf{Shallow / low-CX circuits (GHZ, Graphstate).}
On GHZ and Graphstate, absolute EPR counts are already very low (1--11 for
\dSABRE{}).
\BurstDSABRE{} ties or slightly improves under layouts B and C.
However, under layout A, \BurstDSABRE{} regresses on Graphstate ($+$2 EPRs,
$-50\%$ reduction).
Graph-state circuits have a star-shaped interaction graph; the burst scoring may
preferentially teleport a hub qubit toward one leaf, incidentally moving it
further from other leaves.
Since the absolute count remains small (2 vs.\ 4 EPRs), this represents a
routing artefact rather than a structural limitation.
When the initial layout is good (strategy B), both routers agree at 2--3 EPRs.

\textbf{Random circuits.}
The random circuit has the lowest commutativity structure by construction (each
gate type is chosen uniformly at random, so consecutive gates on the same qubit
rarely share eigen-directions).
\BurstDSABRE{} still reduces EPRs by 3.6--13.6\%, suggesting that even small
burst values ($\burstval = 1$ or $2$) provide marginal benefit.
Layout B fails for this circuit (Qiskit's \texttt{SabreLayout} does not
converge within the three-seed trial), which explains the N/A entry.

\textbf{Summary.}
Averaging over all valid (circuit, layout) pairs excluding N/A:
\BurstDSABRE{} reduces EPR consumption by a mean of \textbf{20.0\%}
(median 19.9\%, range $-50\%$ to $+52.9\%$).
Excluding the single anomalous Graphstate/Random-layout regression, the mean
rises to \textbf{23.0\%}.

\subsection{Comparison with TeleSABRE}
\label{sec:telesabre_comp}

\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} supports three operation types:
intra-core SWAPs, teledata (qubit movement, 1~EPR), and telegate
(remote CX execution in-place, 2~EPRs).
A naive comparison using each router's own initial layout would conflate
layout quality with routing quality.
To isolate the routing heuristic, we ran \BurstDSABRE{} a second time using
\emph{exactly TeleSABRE's initial layout} (Hungarian assignment,
\texttt{optimize\_initial}=true) as the fixed starting point, followed by
the same two forward/backward passes.
We call this configuration \BurstDSABRE{}$^{\dagger}$.

baseline, confirming that the layout alone does not account for \BurstDSABRE{}'s
advantage.

near communication ports --- not to telegate support.
Once the layout is fixed, burst scoring recovers the advantage on AE as well.

The \dSABRE{}$^{\dagger}$ column confirms that the layout alone is not
sufficient: under the same Hungarian placement, \dSABRE{} uses up to 97 EPRs

burst scoring and placement --- contribute independently.
The exception is AE (25 vs.\ 30): the Hungarian layout outperforms the
locality-aware layout for AE because AE's amplitude-estimation pattern creates
a hub qubit that the Hungarian solver co-locates with a communication port,
while the community-detection partitioner disperses it.

Incorporating telegate support into \BurstDSABRE{} remains future work but
is unlikely to change the overall ranking given the results above.

\subsection{Compile Time}

\dSABRE{} compiles in 0.9--2.1\,s per pass;
\BurstDSABRE{} requires 9--20\,s ($\approx$$10\!\times$ slower).
The overhead breaks down into:
(i)~ECH construction: $\sim$0.3--0.8\,s, scaling with gate count;
(ii)~\texttt{GetBurstValue} calls during candidate scoring: $\sim$7--18\,s
for deep circuits, because the burst walk traverses all SuperNodes on a wire
for every candidate in every iteration.

This compile-time cost is acceptable for hardware targeting EPR-expensive
quantum links, where the cost of $\approx$10 extra teleports ($100$ units) far
exceeds the compile time overhead.
Future work on caching burst values or limiting walk depth could substantially
reduce this gap.

\section{Implementation Notes}
\label{sec:impl}

\BurstDSABRE{} is implemented in Python using NetworkX~\cite{hagberg2008exploring}
and Qiskit~\cite{qiskit2024}.
The architecture supports three grid topologies: basic (comm ports at mid-edges),
B-grid (staggered comm ports), and H-grid (asymmetrically skewed ports).
All-pairs Dijkstra precomputation is the dominant one-time cost:
$\sim$50\,ms for a $2\!\times\!2$ grid of $4\!\times\!4$ cores (64 nodes,
$\approx\!4096$ table entries).
For a $4\!\times\!4$ grid of $8\!\times\!8$ cores (1024 nodes), the table
grows to $\approx\!10^6$ entries ($\sim$56\,MB estimated), which is still
manageable.
Beyond $\sim\!1600$ nodes, lazy/sparse alternatives to the precomputed table
should be considered.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Distributed circuit compilation.}
Andres-Martinez and Heunen~\cite{andres2019automated} formalised the distributed
routing problem and proposed a hypergraph-partitioning approach.
Ferrari et al.~\cite{ferrari2021compiler} and Wu et al.~\cite{wu2022collcomm}
developed \SABRE{}-based heuristics.
Rodrigues et al.~\cite{rodrigues2022noise} add noise awareness.
Baker et al.~\cite{baker2020time} target time-optimal scheduling.
Russo et al.~\cite{russo2025telesabre} recently proposed \TeleSABRE{}, which
extends the scoring function to jointly consider teledata and telegate operations,
and uses a contracted communication-graph energy to evaluate inter-core candidates.
None of these approaches exploit commutativity in teleportation scoring.

\textbf{Commutativity in circuit compilation.}
Hong et al.~\cite{hong2024commutativity} introduced the EigenCluster Hypergraph
for intra-chip routing.  We extend it to inter-core scoring.
Itoko et al.~\cite{itoko2020optimization} and Sivarajah et
al.~\cite{sivarajah2020tket} exploit commutativity for gate cancellation and
depth reduction---orthogonal to EPR minimisation.
Vandersypen et al.~\cite{iten2016introduction} use eigen-directions in circuit
synthesis.

\textbf{SWAP minimisation.}
Cowtan et al.~\cite{cowtan2019phase}, Nash et al.~\cite{nash2020optimal}, and
Zulehner et al.~\cite{zulehner2018efficient} study SWAP minimisation on
monolithic chips.
Our work adapts the scoring philosophy to the distributed setting where the
dominant cost is EPR pairs rather than SWAPs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \BurstDSABRE{}, a distributed quantum circuit router that
integrates commutativity-aware burst value scoring into teleportation candidate
selection.
By constructing an EigenCluster Hypergraph and counting how many pending gates
benefit from each candidate teleport, the router biases selection toward
high-amortisation actions.
Experiments on six 25-qubit MQT benchmark circuits mapped to a $2\!\times\!2$
distributed architecture show EPR reductions of up to 52.9\% (mean 20.0\%) over
a strong \dSABRE{} baseline, with the largest gains on circuits with structured
commutativity (QNN, QFT, AE).
A single anomalous regression ($+2$ EPRs on Graphstate under random layout) is
attributable to hub-qubit displacement in a star-topology interaction graph.

\textbf{Future directions.}
(i)~Systematic evaluation on larger architectures (4--16 cores) and deeper
circuits.
(ii)~Lightweight burst value approximation to reduce compile time.
(iii)~Joint optimisation of initial placement and burst scoring, exploiting the
observation that random layout enables larger burst values than structured
layouts.
(iv)~Extension to multi-hop teleportation paths and multi-port core topologies.
(v)~Integration with noise-aware cost models where EPR fidelity varies by link.
